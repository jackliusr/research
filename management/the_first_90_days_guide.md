## 1. The Book Overview

### 1.1 The Premise

**"The First 90 Days, Updated and Expanded: Proven Strategies for Getting Up to Speed Faster and Smarter"** (Harvard Business Review Press, 2013) is Michael D. Watkins' tenth-anniversary revision of the international bestseller that made "the first 90 days" a management phrase. The publisher's own description (identical on the HBR Press store, Amazon, and Google Books — verified) states the problem the book was written to solve: *"Transitions are a critical time for leaders... While transitions offer a chance to start fresh and make needed changes in an organization, they also place leaders in a position of acute vulnerability. Missteps made during the crucial first three months in a new role can jeopardize or even derail your success."* The book's core promise, from the 2003 original's jacket copy (verified via the Open Library catalog record): its strategies *"will dramatically shorten the time it takes to reach what Watkins calls the 'breakeven point': the point at which your organization needs you as much as you need the job."*

The book is organized **by the ten transition challenges**: each chapter is one of the ten "principles" of effective transition (the introduction lists them — see §5), from preparing yourself (Ch.1) through accelerating everyone's ramp-up (Ch.10). Every chapter ends with a checklist and self-assessment (verified structural fact — the publisher's description states *"Each chapter also includes checklists, practical tools, and self-assessments"*; the 2013 text itself shows an "ACCELERATE YOUR LEARNING—CHECKLIST" at p. 66). This stage-free structure is what makes the book a *companion* rather than a cover-to-cover read: you can jump to the chapter that matches the transition you are in, which is exactly how this guide is meant to be used — the chapter map in §5–§15 tracks the verified 2013 structure, and §17 walks one Cymbal Bank leader through a full 90-day plan.

**Why it matters.** The book has been in continuous print since 2003, and its publisher-adjacent materials claim more than 1.5 million copies sold and translations into 23 languages, with *The Economist* calling it "the onboarding bible" (⚠ these figures are the author's IMD faculty page's claims, not independently audited this run — see §19). Its durability comes from a rare combination: a tight, research-grounded core model (the break-even point), a situational framework that survives contact with reality (STARS), and a toolkit (learning agenda, early wins, alliances) that is still standard practice in 2026 — the same toolkit the repo's [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) operationalizes as 30/60/90 plans for new engineering managers.

## 2. The Core Models

Six models carry the book. Each is explained here with its verification status; the chapter map (§5–§15) says where each lives, and the worked example (§17) exercises all of them.

### 2.1 The STARS Model (Ch.3)

**Verified verbatim from the 2013 text (p. 71):** *"STARS is an acronym for five common business situations leaders may find themselves moving into: start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, and sustaining success."* The five situations:

| Situation | What it is | The transition imperative |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------------|
| **S — Start-up** | Building something new: a new unit, product, or company. Everything must be created; there is no baseline. | Move fast, define the plan, attract talent, create wins from nothing; the "action imperative" is least dangerous here. |
| **T — Turnaround** | A unit or company in serious trouble that must be saved. Everything is broken; time is short. | Act decisively, cut fast, restore credibility with stakeholders; the organization is ready for change. |
| **A — Accelerated growth** | Rapid scaling of a successful business. The challenge is the opposite of turnaround: too much demand, too little capacity. | Build systems and structure before they are needed; delegate and institutionalize; keep quality from collapsing under speed. |
| **R — Realignment** | A fundamentally sound organization that has drifted out of alignment with its environment — the hardest situation to *see*, because the need for change is not obvious. | Diagnose the misalignment, overcome complacency, and create urgency without the cover of a crisis. |
| **S — Sustaining success** | A successful organization that must stay on top — the "successor's trap" of keeping momentum going. | Honor and build on the past; avoid dismantling what works while still refreshing strategy and talent. |

Two companion ideas are also verified in the 2013 text: the **STARS portfolio** (p. 76–77 — *"you must figure out which parts of your new organization belong in each of the five categories"*; most senior roles are a *mix*, e.g. a healthy core with one broken product line), and the **STARS implications for evaluation and culture** (p. 83–85 — *"The STARS framework has implications for how you should evaluate the people who work for you, and for the culture you want to"*; performance in start-ups/turnarounds is easy to measure against a baseline, hardest in realignment/sustaining success). The chapter also reports a survey of leaders' most-preferred and most-challenging situations (p. 84, ⚠ the survey's exact numbers were not captured).

**Repo mapping.** Matching your approach to the situation is the same logic as [situational_leadership_slii_guide.md](situational_leadership_slii_guide.md) §4–§6 (styles S1–S4 matched to development levels D1–D4); the "diagnose before you act" discipline recurs in [management_consulting_skills_guide.md](management_consulting_skills_guide.md) §2 (hypothesis-driven problem solving). The strategy dimension of each situation maps to [strategic_management_guide.md](strategic_management_guide.md) and [it_strategy_guide.md](it_strategy_guide.md).

### 2.2 The Break-Even Point (Introduction)

The book's central image, **verified from the 2013 text (p. 3–4)**: a chart of *value produced* vs. *value consumed* across "months after entry" (1 through 7+). A new leader starts by consuming value (learning, asking, absorbing); the **break-even point** is where *net contribution = 0* — the moment the organization gets back as much as it puts in. The goal of every transition is to reach break-even **faster** ("Accelerating break-even," p. 4) and to keep the cumulative value positive thereafter. The 2003 jacket's plain-language definition is verified via the Open Library catalog record: *"the point at which your organization needs you as much as you need the job."* The book's quantitative claim for its own method (2013 Notes, p. 261): First 90 Days program participants reported an **average 1.2-month decrease in time to break-even**, with ROI "roughly 300 percent based on salary costs only" (✅ verified snippet).

**Repo mapping.** The break-even mindset is why onboarding ramps exist: [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md) §3 (onboarding) and [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.3 (the 30/60/90 plan) are the repo's operationalizations of "get to break-even faster."

### 2.3 The Learning Agenda (Ch.2)

**Verified from the 2013 text (p. 50, 63):** *"Your learning agenda defines what you want to learn. Your learning plan defines how you will go about learning it."* The learning agenda is a written list of questions — about the business (history, financial health, strategy, capabilities), the people (who matters, who knows what), the culture (how things actually get done), and the technology — that you refine as you learn. The learning plan turns those questions into specific actions: which documents to read, which people to interview (your people, your boss, peers, customers, suppliers, analysts), and how to extract insight efficiently (p. 50: *"Efficient learning means identifying the best available sources of insight and then figuring out how to extract maximum insight"*). The chapter's checklist (p. 66–67, verified) asks you to compose your questions *before* day one and return to the agenda periodically.

**Repo mapping.** The learning-agenda discipline is the information-gathering half of [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) §7 (stakeholder analysis) and the "listen and learn" phase of the 30/60/90 plan in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.3.

### 2.4 The Early-Wins Strategy (Ch.5)

**Verified from the 2013 text:** early wins are "specific projects to secure early wins" translated from your learning agenda (p. 130: *"Then translate your goals into specific projects to secure early wins, using the following guidelines: Keep your long…"* — snippet truncated). The chapter's guidance: focus early wins where the business needs improvement **and** where you can build credibility; pick a *focus area* (financially, strategically, or politically central) rather than scattering effort; make each win specific and measurable (the widely-summarized **AOS** rule — *achieve* something specific, *overdeliver* on it, and *specify* expectations concretely — ⚠ the acronym itself was not captured verbatim this run); and sequence wins in "waves" (the 2013 index confirms "waves of" early wins at pp. 117–118, ✅). Early wins are how you create the **virtuous cycle** of credibility → resources → more wins, versus the vicious cycle of missed expectations → loss of credibility → loss of support.

**Repo mapping.** The early-wins focus-area logic maps to [balanced_scorecard_guide.md](balanced_scorecard_guide.md) (choosing where to concentrate and how to measure) and the "small wins" middle phase of 30/60/90 plans in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.3; the credibility-building goal maps to [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) §7–§8.

### 2.5 Alliance Building (Ch.8)

The chapter title itself is verified — **"Create Alliances"** (2013 ToC, p. 199; the 2003 edition called it "Create Coalitions"). The model: identify the stakeholders whose cooperation you need (boss, peers, customers, suppliers, regulators, unions, other units); diagnose each one's **position** (supporter, opponent, or neutral — and how strongly held); map influence and interdependence; and then *create alliances before you need them* — especially with peers whose goals overlap with yours, and with "neutral" stakeholders who are winnable. The chapter's political reading of organizations is pragmatic: politics is how decisions actually get made, so the question is not whether to play but how well. (⚠ the chapter's specific influence-strategy taxonomies were not captured verbatim this run; the theme and chapter title are verified.)

**Repo mapping.** The direct toolkits: [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) §7 (stakeholder analysis and influence without authority), [360_management_guide.md](360_management_guide.md) §7–§9 (360° stakeholder management), and [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md) §4 (managing sideways — peers).

### 2.6 Self-Management (Ch.9)

The 2013 chapter title is verified — **"Manage Yourself"** (p. 221; the 2003 edition called it "Keep Your Balance"). The model: transitions are marathons, so protect yourself on three fronts — (1) **your personal life**: warn your family a transition is coming, set expectations, and protect personal time; (2) **your energy**: monitor the balance between what drains you and what restores you, and watch for "work avoidance" behaviors (the 2013 index confirms "work avoidance" at p. 226, ✅) — busywork that lets you dodge the real, uncomfortable work of a transition; (3) **your development**: build a **personal advisory board** — a small set of trusted advisors (inside and outside the organization) who give you unfiltered counsel (⚠ the advisory-board framework's exact in-chapter treatment was not captured verbatim this run). The chapter's hard truth: how you manage yourself in the first 90 days sets the pattern for your whole tenure.

**Repo mapping.** The direct toolkit is [managing_inward_guide.md](managing_inward_guide.md) §1–§4 (self-awareness, energy, burnout) and [time_energy_management_guide.md](time_energy_management_guide.md) (energy management); the advisory-board idea maps to [360_management_guide.md](360_management_guide.md) §7 and the peer-network advice in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §10.

### 2.7 Negotiating Success with Your Boss (Ch.4)

The fourth model sits between the chapters: the boss relationship is *the* relationship of any transition ("Because no other single relationship is more important," verified in the 2013 introduction's principle list, p. 10). Ch.4's framework — widely summarized and consistent with the verified text — is the **five conversations** with your new boss: (1) the **situational-diagnosis** conversation (what situation are we really in — the STARS diagnosis, p. 97 verified: *"think carefully about the role you need your new boss to play"*), (2) the **expectations** conversation (what does success look like in 90 days, and how will it be measured), (3) the **style** conversation (how you each like to work and communicate), (4) the **resources** conversation (what you need to succeed — people, budget, authority), and (5) the **personal-development** conversation (how your boss will help you grow). (⚠ the five-conversation labels were not captured verbatim this run; the "negotiate success" principle, the chapter title, and the boss-relationship centrality are verified.)

**Repo mapping.** This is the managing-up half of the sandwich in [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md) §3, and the "give your manager a break / know what you want" theme of [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §3.

## 5. Introduction: The First 90 Days

**Where it sits.** Pages 1–17. The introduction sets the whole argument: transitions are the highest-leverage, highest-risk moments of a career, and the first 90 days decide the trajectory. Verified anchors: the **break-even point** chart (p. 3–4 — value produced vs. value consumed; "Accelerating break-even," p. 4), the **ten traps** discussion (p. 6–9 — the p. 6 snippet shows one trap verbatim: *"Attempting to do too much. You rush…"*), and the **ten principles** list (p. 9–10 — the p. 10 snippet verifies the bullet style: *"• Negotiate success. Because no other single relationship is more important…"*), plus the p. 17 self-assessment questions ("What can you do to create virtuous cycles and build momentum in your new role?").

**Core concepts.**

- **The break-even point** — reach net contribution = 0 faster than your predecessors and your stakeholders expect; the entire book is a toolkit for accelerating it (§2.2).
- **The ten transition traps** — the introduction's list of how new leaders fail: sticking with what you know; the action imperative (acting before understanding); unrealistic expectations; trying to do too much too quickly; coming in with "the answer"; learning the wrong things; neglecting horizontal (peer) relationships; not matching strategy to situation; treating everyone the same; and losing focus on self-management (⚠ the ten-item list's exact wording is from the book's widely-summarized framework and is consistent with the verified p. 6 snippet, but was not captured verbatim this run — see §19).
- **The ten principles** — the ten chapters in one list: prepare yourself, accelerate your learning, match strategy to situation, negotiate success, secure early wins, achieve alignment, build your team, create alliances, manage yourself, accelerate everyone (✅ chapter-title-level verified; the p. 10 bullet snippet confirms the list form).
- **Virtuous vs. vicious cycles** — early wins compound into credibility and resources; early failures compound into isolation and loss of support.

**Key takeaways.**

1. Transitions are when you have the most leverage and the least information — plan them like projects, not like accidents.
2. Diagnose which of the ten traps you are personally prone to before you start.
3. Define "success at day 90" with your boss in the first two weeks, not the last two.

**Memorable advice.** The introduction's framing line is the publisher's own: *"Missteps made during the crucial first three months in a new role can jeopardize or even derail your success"* (✅ verified publisher copy), and its closing self-assessment asks *"What can you do to create virtuous cycles and build momentum in your new role?"* (✅ verified snippet, p. 17).

## 6. Chapter 1: Prepare Yourself

**Where it sits.** Pages 19–43. The renamed version of 2003's "Promote Yourself." Core thesis: the psychological preparation — mindset, expectations, and support — matters as much as the business preparation. You are entering with a "promotion mindset" hangover from your previous role; the job now is to start fresh deliberately.

**Core concepts.**

- **Let go of the past** — the habits and identity that made you successful in the old role are a liability in the new one; the book's version of "unlearn before you learn."
- **The action imperative, resisted** — the pressure to act before you understand is strongest in the first weeks; Ch.1's discipline is to *diagnose first*.

1. Spend your first days *preparing*, not performing: write down what you need to unlearn.
2. Negotiate your own expectations down to something achievable, then overdeliver.
3. If you were promoted from within, plan explicitly for the awkward re-founding of peer relationships.

**Memorable advice.** The chapter's operating rule is the one Watkins repeats across the book: resist the action imperative — *diagnose before you act* (⚠ wording via standard summary; the trap is verified at p. 6 as "Attempting to do too much").

## 7. Chapter 2: Accelerate Your Learning

**Where it sits.** Pages 45–67. Core thesis: new leaders fail to learn fast not from lack of intelligence but from unsystematic learning — they learn the wrong things from the wrong people in the wrong order. The fix is a deliberate **learning agenda** and **learning plan** (both verified phrases, p. 50, 63; the chapter ends with the verified "ACCELERATE YOUR LEARNING—CHECKLIST," p. 66).

**Core concepts.**

- **The learning agenda** — what you need to learn, written as questions *before* you start: about the business (history, financials, strategy), the people (who matters, who knows what), the culture (how work actually gets done), and the technology (what runs the place) (✅ p. 50–51, 63).
- **The learning plan** — how you will learn it: which documents, which people, which observations; "efficient learning means identifying the best available sources of insight" (✅ p. 50).
- **The three learning channels** — documents (read them selectively), people (interview everyone: boss, peers, subordinates, customers, suppliers, analysts), and direct experience (visit, observe, do) (⚠ the "three channels" framing is the standard summary; not captured verbatim this run).
- **Learning about culture** — the informal rules and "how things really work" are the hardest and most important thing to learn; watch who gets listened to, what gets rewarded, and what is undiscussable (⚠ chapter-level theme; not captured verbatim).
- **STARS-aware learning priorities** — what you most need to learn differs by situation (a turnaround demands financial/operational learning first; a start-up demands market and talent learning) (⚠ theme consistent with the verified Ch.3 material).

**Key takeaways.**

1. Draft your learning agenda *before* day one — a page of questions, not a to-do list.
2. Interview widely in the first 30 days; ask the same questions of many people and triangulate.
3. Return to the agenda every two weeks; it should shrink as hypotheses form and get tested.

**Memorable advice.** The chapter's most-quoted sentence, verified verbatim (p. 63): *"Your learning agenda defines what you want to learn. Your learning plan defines how you will go about learning it."* Write both before day one.

**Repo mapping.** The information-gathering mechanics live in [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) §7 (stakeholder analysis) and [management_consulting_skills_guide.md](management_consulting_skills_guide.md) §2 (hypothesis-driven inquiry); the "ask, then verify" discipline is [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) §8's 1:1 practice applied to an entire organization.

## 8. Chapter 3: Match Strategy to Situation

**Where it sits.** Pages 69–85. Core thesis: there is no one right way to lead a transition — the right strategy depends on the **situation**, and most leaders misdiagnose it. This is the STARS chapter (verified verbatim, p. 71 — see §2.1).

**Core concepts.**

- **The five STARS situations** — start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success (✅ p. 71).
- **The STARS portfolio** — most roles are a mix; assign each part of your new responsibilities (products, processes, projects, businesses) to its situation and prioritize accordingly (✅ p. 76–77).
- **Matching leadership style to situation** — the same behaviors that save a turnaround (decisive, directive) will destroy a sustaining-success unit (the index confirms "leadership style and [STARS]" at p. 81, ✅).
- **Implications for evaluation and culture** — how you judge people and what you reward must fit the situation; performance is easiest to measure in start-ups and turnarounds, hardest in realignment and sustaining success (✅ p. 83–85).
- **Diagnose before you act** — the chapter's master rule: understand the situation you are actually in before choosing your strategy.

**Key takeaways.**

1. Diagnose your STARS portfolio in the first two weeks — and check your diagnosis with your boss (this feeds the Ch.4 situational-diagnosis conversation).
2. Choose your leadership style to match the situation, not your temperament.
3. Do not apply turnaround tactics to a realignment: the failure mode there is complacency, not chaos.

**Memorable advice.** The chapter's anchor sentence, verified verbatim (p. 71): *"STARS is an acronym for five common business situations leaders may find themselves moving into: start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, and sustaining success."* Diagnose before you act — always.

**Repo mapping.** Situation-matching is the core of [situational_leadership_slii_guide.md](situational_leadership_slii_guide.md) §4–§6 and [3d_managerial_effectiveness_guide.md](3d_managerial_effectiveness_guide.md) §5–§6; the portfolio idea maps to [strategic_management_guide.md](strategic_management_guide.md) (portfolio strategy) and [it_strategy_guide.md](it_strategy_guide.md) (technology-portfolio prioritization).

## 9. Chapter 4: Negotiate Success

**Where it sits.** Pages 87–113. Core thesis: your relationship with your new boss is the single most important relationship of the transition, and it must be **negotiated** — explicitly, early, and continuously — not left to chance. The introduction's principle list verifies the framing ("Because no other single relationship is more important," p. 10).

**Core concepts.**

- **The five conversations** — (1) situational diagnosis (what situation are we really in — the STARS check, with p. 97 verified: *"think carefully about the role you need your new boss to play"*), (2) expectations (what "success in 90 days" means and how it is measured), (3) style (how you each work and communicate), (4) resources (people, budget, authority), (5) personal development (how your boss will help you grow) (⚠ labels per the standard summary; not captured verbatim this run).

- **The boss's boss and the wider web** — your boss is not your only stakeholder; negotiate clarity with everyone whose expectations matter, including your boss's boss (⚠ theme-level).

**Key takeaways.**

1. Book the five conversations in your first two weeks — especially expectations, which most new leaders leave implicit.
2. Agree with your boss on *what* success looks like at day 90, *how* it will be measured, and *what you need* to get there.
3. Give your boss no surprises: the "no surprises" rule is the cheapest trust-builder in the book.

**Memorable advice.** The chapter's rule of thumb, in the book's own spirit: no surprises — bad news early, with a recovery plan attached (⚠ wording via standard summary; consistent with the verified "negotiate success" principle at p. 10 and the managing-up theme).

## 10. Chapter 5: Secure Early Wins

**Where it sits.** Pages 115–137. Core thesis: momentum is everything — a new leader's credibility is built or destroyed in the first three months, and the way to build it is a deliberate sequence of **early wins**: specific, measurable improvements in areas the business cares about.

**Core concepts.**

- **The early-wins plan** — translated from your learning agenda: "translate your goals into specific projects to secure early wins" (✅ p. 130); focus on areas where the business needs improvement *and* where you can build credibility.
- **Focus areas, not scatter** — pick one or two areas (financial, strategic, or political centrality) rather than trying to win everywhere (⚠ theme-level; not captured verbatim).
- **The AOS rule** — *achieve* something specific, *overdeliver* on it, and *specify* expectations so the win is visible and measurable (⚠ acronym per the standard summary; not captured verbatim this run).
- **Waves of wins** — sequence wins over the 90 days (the index confirms "waves of" early wins at pp. 117–118, ✅); early wins must also lay groundwork for later, bigger changes.
- **The virtuous cycle** — each win buys credibility, which buys resources, which buys the next win; the opposite spiral is how transitions fail.

**Key takeaways.**

1. Pick your early-wins focus in week one; have the first win *shipped* by day 30–45, not just planned.
2. Make each win measurable and attributed: a win nobody can see buys no credibility.
3. Sequence wins so the early ones are low-risk and the later ones are high-value.

**Memorable advice.** The chapter's operative line, verified verbatim (p. 130): *"Then translate your goals into specific projects to secure early wins"* — and the companion's gloss on it: a win nobody can see buys no credibility.

**Repo mapping.** The "small wins by day 30–60" phase of the 30/60/90 plan in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.3 is the same device; measuring wins maps to [balanced_scorecard_guide.md](balanced_scorecard_guide.md) (selecting and measuring what matters).

## 11. Chapter 6: Achieve Alignment

**Where it sits.** Pages 139–163. Core thesis: a new leader's strategy succeeds only if the organization is **aligned** behind it — and the typical organization is misaligned in predictable ways: strategy points one way while structure, systems, skills, and culture point another.

**Core concepts.**

- **The alignment diagnosis** — examine the fit among strategy, structure, systems, skills, and culture; misalignment shows up as strategy that never quite executes (⚠ the five-element framing is the chapter's standard reading; not captured verbatim this run).

## 12. Chapter 7: Build Your Team

**Where it sits.** Pages 165–197. Core thesis: your team is the instrument of everything you will achieve, and the team you inherit is rarely the team you need — assess it early, change it deliberately, and never let team problems fester while you "focus on the business."

**Core concepts.**

- **Keep, keep-and-develop, or replace** — the classic triage for inherited teams: not everyone needs to go, and moving too fast on people destroys trust (⚠ the triage labels are the standard summary; not captured verbatim this run).

## 13. Chapter 8: Create Alliances

**Where it sits.** Pages 199–219. Core thesis: you cannot succeed through your boss and your team alone — you need **alliances** across the organization, and they must be built *before* you need them, because by the time you need an ally it is too late to recruit one.

**Core concepts.**

## 14. Chapter 9: Manage Yourself

**Where it sits.** Pages 221–237. Core thesis: the transition is a marathon, and the most common cause of derailment is the leader themselves — exhaustion, isolation, avoidance, and a home front that was never told what was coming. Self-management is a *strategic* task, not a luxury.

**Core concepts.**

- **Protect your personal life** — warn your family about the transition before it starts; a transition is a family event, and an unsupported one leaks into work (⚠ theme-level; not captured verbatim this run).

- **Build a personal advisory board** — a small group of trusted counselors inside and outside the organization who will tell you the truth; the antidote to the loneliness of a new role (⚠ framework per the standard summary; not captured verbatim this run).
- **Keep your balance** — the 2003 chapter's title, still the chapter's soul: sustainable pace beats heroic sprints, because the job lasts longer than the adrenaline.

**Key takeaways.**

1. Tell your family about the transition in week zero, and protect a non-negotiable slice of personal time.
2. Audit your energy weekly: what drained you, what restored you, what are you avoiding?
3. Recruit two or three truth-tellers as a personal advisory board before you need them.

**Memorable advice.** The chapter's tell, verified in the index: watch for **work avoidance** (p. 226) — the busywork that lets you dodge the hard conversation. If your calendar is full and the important thing is still undone, that is not a time problem; it is an avoidance problem.

## 15. Chapter 10: Accelerate Everyone

**Where it sits.** Pages 239–258. The renamed (not new-in-2013) final chapter — the book's turn from the individual leader to the **organization**: companies pay a hidden tax every time anyone transitions, so the highest-leverage move a senior leader can make is to accelerate *everyone's* ramp-up, not just their own.

**Core concepts.**

- **Transitions are an organizational cost** — every failed or slow transition is lost productivity, lost customers, lost talent; the chapter quantifies the stakes (p. 239: a failed transition is "a severe, perhaps career-ending, blow" to the individual — and the organizational cost is larger; ✅ verified snippet).
- **Identify the critical transitions** — not all transitions matter equally; focus acceleration systems on the ones that carry the most organizational risk (index: "critical transitions, identifying, 242–243," ✅).
- **Diagnose existing support** — assess what help people in transition currently get (or don't) (index: "existing support for, diagnosing, 244–246," ✅).
- **Deliver support just in time** — coaching and support timed to the transition's phases, not generic onboarding (verified section heading "Deliver Support Just in Time," p. 247; "Transitions evolve through a series" of phases, p. 247 ✅).

**Key takeaways.**

1. As a senior leader, treat others' transitions as your responsibility: coaching, structured onboarding, and just-in-time support (index: "coaching for [transitions], 249, 251–252," ✅).
2. Identify the handful of transitions that matter most to the business and staff them with support.
3. Build the acceleration system — then measure it (time to break-even is the metric).

**Memorable advice.** The chapter's closing image, verified verbatim (p. 247): *"This kind of systematic support helps move the organization beyond sink or swim"* — the 2003 conclusion's title, now the book's final argument that transitions are an organizational discipline, not an individual ordeal.

## 16. The Core Themes

Five threads run through the book. Each gets a short subsection with its cross-references; the worked example in §17 exercises all of them end to end.

### 16.1 Promotion Transitions

The book's publisher description is explicit that it covers every transition type — "starting a new job, being promoted from within, embarking on an overseas assignment, or being tapped as CEO" (✅ verified) — and promotion is the one that feels easiest and is often the hardest: you must unlearn the old role while old peers watch (Ch.1's "promotion trap," §6), and your leverage shifts from your own output to the system's (the break-even chart, §5). The repo's closest sibling treats the same ground from the engineering side: [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §3 and §13.1 (the IC-to-manager identity shift — "management is a different job, not a promotion") and [managing_inward_guide.md](managing_inward_guide.md) §1–§2. The First 90 Days adds what the Manager's Path assumes: a *timed* discipline for the shift — the 90-day plan below.

### 16.2 Onboarding (and the Organizational View)

For most of the book, onboarding is the individual's job; Ch.10 (§15) makes it the organization's job — identify critical transitions, diagnose existing support, deliver just-in-time coaching, and measure time to break-even (the p. 261 program data: 1.2-month average reduction, ~300% ROI). This is the bridge to the repo's onboarding material in [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md) §3 and the Manager's Path onboarding ramp in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §7, and to the MAS-context people-management angle in [../banking/mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md](../banking/mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) (where onboarding intersects regulated-role approval and fitness-and-propriety checks — the worked example touches this in §17.5).

### 16.3 Constructing a 90-Day Plan

The book's through-line deliverable: a written 90-day plan built from (1) the learning agenda (Ch.2), (2) the STARS diagnosis (Ch.3), (3) the negotiated expectations (Ch.4), (4) the early-wins sequence (Ch.5), and (5) the alliance map (Ch.8) — revisited weekly and renegotiated with your boss at the 30-day mark (the p. 109 snippet confirms the book's own "key outputs at the end of the first 30 days" planning rhythm, ✅). The repo's canonical form of this artifact is the 30/60/90 plan in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.3; §17 of this guide builds one for a Cymbal Bank leader.

### 16.4 The 30/60/90 Cadence

⚠ One honest flag: the book organizes around "the first 90 days" and phases ("the end of the first 30 days," p. 109), but the crisp *30/60/90* naming — first 30 days learn, days 31–60 win, days 61–90 consolidate and scale — is the repo's and the industry's operationalization of the book, not a table the book prints (see §19). The mapping is faithful: days 1–30 = learning agenda + STARS diagnosis + the five conversations (Ch.1–4); days 31–60 = early wins + alignment + team assessment (Ch.5–7); days 61–90 = alliances consolidated, self-management locked in, the acceleration system launched (Ch.8–10). Cross-ref [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.3 for the same cadence in reporting-relationship form.

### 16.5 Transitions at All Levels

The book is deliberately level-agnostic: the same frameworks apply to a first-line manager and a new CEO (the STARS portfolio scales from a product line to a whole company; the five conversations scale from a boss to a board). The repo mirrors this in [mba_body_of_knowledge_guide.md](mba_body_of_knowledge_guide.md) (the general-management body of knowledge), [strategic_management_guide.md](strategic_management_guide.md) (the strategy lens at senior levels), and [3d_managerial_effectiveness_guide.md](3d_managerial_effectiveness_guide.md) (management styles at every rung).

### 16.6 Transitions in a Regulated Environment (the MAS Angle)

One context the book does not name but the repo's readers live in: **regulated banking**. In a Singapore bank, a leadership transition collides with regulatory expectations that the book's frameworks absorb naturally — the alliance map (Ch.8) must include the regulator as a stakeholder (for a MAS-licensed bank, the technology-risk and outsourcing expectations in [../banking/mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md](../banking/mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) make the regulatory relationship *material*, not peripheral); the "no surprises" rule of Ch.4 becomes the regulatory-notification discipline (escalate incidents early, with a recovery plan); and the early-wins focus of Ch.5 often lands on regulatory-reporting reliability — exactly the worked example's choice in §17. The 90-day plan of a bank leader is therefore the book's plan with a regulatory critical path drawn through it; [../banking/singapore_fintech_payments_guide.md](../banking/singapore_fintech_payments_guide.md) supplies the payments/fintech context that makes the example concrete.

## 17. The Worked Example: A Cymbal Bank Head of Technology 90-Day Transition Plan

**Scenario.** Nadia Rahman joins **Cymbal Bank** — the repo's fictional mid-size Singapore-based bank (see [../banking/singapore_fintech_payments_guide.md](../banking/singapore_fintech_payments_guide.md) for the franchise: ASEAN corporate and institutional clients, a real-time payments franchise, a MAS-regulated universal-banking model) — as **Head of Technology**, hired from outside to lead the technology division of ~40 engineers across payments, core banking, and data. Her predecessor left after a regulator-adjacent incident: a settlement-reporting submission was late, and the bank's technology estate is a patchwork of a modern real-time payments platform and legacy batch systems. Nadia has 90 days to reach break-even faster than her predecessor did — and to convince the CEO, the COO, and the CRO that technology is the answer, not the problem. (The persona follows the repo convention: Cymbal Bank is the only bank persona here, in the style of [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13's worked example.)

### 17.1 The STARS Diagnosis (days 1–14)

Nadia runs the STARS portfolio exercise (Ch.3) in week one, before proposing anything:

| Part of her new responsibility | STARS situation | Why | The imperative |
|-------------------------------|-----------------|-----|----------------|
| Real-time payments platform (FAST-style rails, ISO 20022 messages) | **Realignment** | Fundamentally sound, but drifted: deployment pipelines are slow, and the platform team has been firefighting for a year | Diagnose the drift, create urgency without a crisis, fix the alignment (structure vs. delivery model) |
| Digital onboarding and payments innovation lab | **Accelerated growth** | The bank's fintech bets are winning — usage doubles yearly — and the team is drowning in demand | Build structure and capacity before quality collapses; delegate |
| Legacy batch settlement engine | **Turnaround** | The source of the late regulatory submission; fragile, under-documented, single points of failure | Act decisively, stabilize first, restore credibility with MAS-facing stakeholders |
| Retail core banking (maintained, stable) | **Sustaining success** | Works; the team is competent and proud of it | Honor the past, refresh selectively, do not dismantle what works |

Her conclusion, checked with her boss in the Ch.4 situational-diagnosis conversation: this is a **realignment-with-a-turnaround-pocket** portfolio. The biggest risk is misdiagnosing it as a turnaround and applying slash-and-burn to a fundamentally sound platform team — which would destroy the real-time payments franchise while fixing the settlement engine. The turnaround pocket (settlement) gets speed; the realignment core (payments) gets diagnosis and alignment; the growth pocket gets structure.

### 17.2 The Learning Agenda (days 1–30)

Written before day one, refined weekly (Ch.2). A sample of Nadia's questions:

- **Business:** Which three products drive Cymbal Bank's P&L? What is the payments franchise's margin, and what are the top customer complaints? What did the late MAS submission actually cost (fines, notices, relationship damage)?
- **People:** Who holds the real knowledge in each platform team? Who is listened to regardless of title? Who on the leadership team is a technology ally, and who quietly resents it?
- **Culture:** Why did the late submission happen *culturally* — was it a tooling gap, a silo, or a "don't escalate" norm? What gets celebrated (heroics vs. reliability)?
- **Technology:** Where are the single points of failure in the settlement chain? What is the real deployment cadence of the payments platform? What is the actual state of runbooks, monitoring, and incident practice?

Her learning plan: two documents (the last MAS submission, the platform's architecture review) before day one; interviews with her direct reports, the CRO, the head of payments product, the COO's operations lead, and two vendors in weeks 1–3; and "follow the money" — she sits in on the settlement batch window once, at 2 a.m., because that is where the truth lives (the p. 50 lesson: extract maximum insight from the best sources).

### 17.3 The Early Wins (days 30–60)

From the learning agenda, Nadia picks a focus area that is both *business-central* and *credibility-building* (Ch.5): **regulatory-reporting reliability on the payments and settlement chain**. Three wins, sequenced in waves:

1. **Wave 1 (day ~35): the visibility win.** A one-page "settlement chain health" dashboard — batch completion, exception counts, submission-readiness — shown weekly to the CRO and the COO. Low risk, fully in her control, and it makes the previously invisible chain visible. *Achieve: ship the dashboard. Overdeliver: include the payments platform's own submission-readiness. Specify: agreed metric definitions in writing.*
2. **Wave 2 (day ~55): the stabilization win.** Harden the settlement batch: add a checkpoint alert and a documented runbook for the top three failure modes, and run a blameless rehearsal of the MAS submission cycle with the operations team. Measurable: zero missed internal submission deadlines for 30 consecutive days.
3. **Wave 3 (day ~75): the alignment win.** A "reliability charter" for the payments platform team — the alignment artifact (Ch.6): explicit SLOs, a deployment pipeline backlog item, and one structural change (a platform reliability squad) — the first piece of the realignment.

Each win is tied to the negotiated success metrics (§17.4), so it is visible to the people who matter.

### 17.4 Negotiating Success and Building Alliances (days 1–60)

The **five conversations** (Ch.4) are booked in weeks 1–2 with her boss, the COO: situational diagnosis (the STARS check above — and the COO's own view, which differs: he thinks it is a pure turnaround; Nadia negotiates the diagnosis before the strategy), expectations (day-90 success = "no missed regulatory submissions, a visible reliability plan, and a technology roadmap the CEO can defend"), style (the COO wants weekly written one-pagers; she adapts), resources (two new settlement-engineer hires and the authority to pause a non-critical legacy project), and personal development (a monthly skip-level with the CEO and a mentor on the bank's risk committee).

The **alliance map** (Ch.8): the CRO is a natural ally (reliability is her mandate) — *supporter*; the head of payments product is *neutral-but-winnable* (she wants the platform's roadmap to serve clients, not just compliance — Nadia makes the reliability charter serve both); the head of retail technology is a *mild opponent* (fears Nadia will raid his budget — managed around with a transparent resource agreement); the MAS relationship manager (the bank's liaison for regulatory engagements) is a *supporter* who is brought into the dashboard review from week one. Nadia spends her political capital on the winnable neutral, not on converting the opponent.

| Stakeholder | Position (Ch.8) | Nadia's strategy |
|-------------|-----------------|------------------|
| COO (her boss) | Supporter, but wrong diagnosis (thinks "pure turnaround") | Five conversations; negotiate the STARS diagnosis before the strategy |
| CRO | Supporter (reliability is her mandate) | Weekly dashboard review; make her look good to MAS |
| Head of payments product | Neutral-but-winnable | Reliability charter that serves clients, not just compliance |
| Head of retail technology | Mild opponent (budget fear) | Transparent resource agreement; no raids |
| MAS relationship manager | Supporter | Briefed week one; invited into the dashboard review |
| Settlement team lead | Neutral, defensive | Hard conversation (day ~40); keep/develop/replace decision by day 60 |

### 17.5 The 90-Day Plan (30/60/90)

| Phase | Focus | Key actions | Success measures |
|-------|-------|-------------|------------------|
| **Days 1–30: Learn and diagnose** (Ch.1–4) | Learning agenda; STARS portfolio; five conversations; alliance map | Draft learning agenda pre-start; interviews across bank and vendors; STARS diagnosis agreed with COO; expectations + style + resources conversations; settlement batch observed live; MAS liaison briefed | Learning agenda shrinks to hypotheses; written 90-day plan agreed with COO; stakeholder map complete |
| **Days 31–60: Early wins and stabilization** (Ch.5–7) | Ship wins; stabilize settlement; assess team | Wave-1 dashboard live and in weekly CRO/COO review; batch checkpoint alerts + runbooks; 30-day zero-missed-deadline streak; team assessment of all 8 direct reports and 2 key squads; reliability charter drafted with the payments team | Dashboard used in the weekly review; zero missed internal deadlines; team assessment completed with keep/develop/replace triage |
| **Days 61–90: Alignment and acceleration** (Ch.6, Ch.8–10) | Lock in alignment; consolidate alliances; launch acceleration | Reliability charter signed off; platform reliability squad stood up; resource agreement with retail tech; skip-level with CEO; onboarding/transition support for her own new reports (the Ch.10 system, started small); personal advisory board recruited | Day-90 review against negotiated metrics: no missed regulatory submissions; roadmap presented to the board technology committee; successor named for one key role |

**Self-management throughout (Ch.9):** Nadia tells her family about the 2 a.m. batch-window visits and the first-90-days intensity *before* starting; protects Friday evenings; recruits her personal advisory board (a retired bank CTO, a peer from her previous firm, and the risk-committee mentor); and watches her own work-avoidance tell — she catches herself polishing the dashboard instead of having the hard conversation with the settlements team lead about the missed deadline, and does the conversation anyway. At day 90 her cumulative contribution has crossed the break-even line (the p. 3–4 chart, made real): the organization now needs her as much as she needs the job — and the settlement chain is no longer the reason anyone loses sleep.

## 21. The Glossary

**Break-even point** — the moment in a transition when your net contribution reaches zero: the organization gets back as much as it puts in; the book's central metric and the definition of "getting up to speed" (Intro; §2.2, §5).

**STARS** — the acronym for the five transition situations: **s**tart-up, **t**urnaround, **a**ccelerated growth, **r**ealignment, **s**ustaining success (Ch.3; §2.1, §8). Verified verbatim, p. 71.

**STARS portfolio** — the mix of situations within one role; most senior jobs contain several, and each part needs its own strategy (Ch.3; §2.1).

**Learning agenda** — the written list of questions about your new organization that you draft before starting and refine as you learn; "defines what you want to learn" (Ch.2; §2.3, §7).

**Learning plan** — how you will learn it: sources, people, and methods; "defines how you will go about learning it" (Ch.2; §2.3, §7).

**Early wins** — specific, measurable, business-central improvements secured in the first 90 days to build credibility and momentum (Ch.5; §2.4, §10).

**AOS** — achieve something specific, overdeliver on it, specify expectations concretely; the early-wins rule of thumb (⚠ not captured verbatim this run; Ch.5; §10).

**Five conversations** — the negotiated relationship with your new boss: situational diagnosis, expectations, style, resources, personal development (⚠ labels per standard summary; Ch.4; §2.7, §9).

**STARS-aware alliance map** — the stakeholder diagnosis (supporters, opponents, winnable neutrals) you build before you need the allies (Ch.8; §2.5, §13).

**Personal advisory board** — the small group of trusted truth-tellers, inside and outside the organization, who counsel you through the transition (⚠ per standard summary; Ch.9; §14).

**Work avoidance** — the busywork that lets a new leader dodge the genuinely hard transition work; indexed in the 2013 edition at p. 226 (Ch.9; §14).

**30/60/90 plan** — the repo's operationalization of the book's phased 90 days: days 1–30 learn and diagnose, days 31–60 win, days 61–90 consolidate and scale (⚠ naming is repo/industry standard, not a printed book table; §16.4; [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.3).

**Beyond sink or swim** — the 2003 conclusion's title; in the 2013 edition, the closing section of Ch.10 on organization-level transition support (p. 247) (§3, §15).

**Virtuous / vicious cycles** — credibility compounding into resources and wins, or missed expectations compounding into isolation (Intro, Ch.5; §5, §10).

**Wave** — a sequenced tranche of early wins: each wave is low-risk and credibility-building so the next, higher-value wave is possible; the 2013 index confirms "waves of" early wins at pp. 117–118 (Ch.5; §2.4, §10).

**Start-up** — the STARS situation of building something new (a unit, product, or company) from nothing; the imperative is to move fast, define the plan, and attract talent (Ch.3; §2.1, §8).

**Turnaround** — the STARS situation of saving a unit or company in serious trouble; the imperative is to act decisively, cut fast, and restore credibility with stakeholders (Ch.3; §2.1, §8).

**Accelerated growth** — the STARS situation of rapidly scaling a successful business; the imperative is to build systems and structure before they are needed (Ch.3; §2.1, §8).

**Realignment** — the STARS situation of a fundamentally sound organization that has drifted out of alignment with its environment; the hardest situation to see because there is no crisis, and the imperative is to create urgency despite complacency (Ch.3; §2.1, §8).

**Sustaining success** — the STARS situation of a successful organization that must stay on top; the imperative is to honor the past and refresh selectively without dismantling what works — the successor's trap territory (Ch.3; §2.1, §8).

**Onboarding** — the organizational discipline of accelerating a new hire's path to the break-even point: structured ramps, coaching, and just-in-time support (Ch.10; §15, §16.2; [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md) §3).

**Transition** — the period of maximum leverage and minimum information that starts a new role; the book's subject, and the moment when the ten principles apply (Intro; §5).

**Alliance / coalition** — the network of stakeholders (boss, peers, other units, customers, regulators) recruited before you need them; "Create Coalitions" (2003) became "Create Alliances" (2013) (Ch.8; §2.5, §13).

**Successor's trap** — the sustaining-success failure mode: a successor who dismantles what the predecessor built, or who fails to refresh what must change; avoided by honoring the past while building the future (Ch.3, Ch.9; §2.1, §8).

## 22. The Closing
