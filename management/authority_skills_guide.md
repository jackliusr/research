## 1. The Concept — What Authority Is

### 1.1 The Short Answer

**Authority is legitimate power** — power that the people subject to it accept as rightful. ✅ The standard reference statement is that authority "is commonly understood as the legitimate power of a person or group over other people," and that "the term *authority* is often used for power that is perceived as legitimate or socially approved by the social structure" ([Wikipedia, *Authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority); [Wikipedia, *Power (political science)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_(social_and_political))). The distinction that matters for a practitioner is the one word: *legitimate*. Power is the capacity to produce an effect; authority is the capacity that others recognise as *entitled* to produce it. A manager can compel attendance and still command no authority; an expert can hold no position and be deferred to anyway. Authority is therefore relational and perceptual — it lives in the follower's recognition, not only in the leader's chair.

The practical claim of this guide is that authority is not a trait you are born with or a badge the org chart hands you. It is a **skill set** — earned through competence and credibility, exercised through decision rights and delegation, maintained through consistency and trust, repaired through named moves after overreach, and lost through neglect. That claim is what the human-skills/leadership genre is about, and this guide verifies what stands behind it.

### 1.2 The Definitional Lineage — Legitimate Domination

The definitional root is **Max Weber**. Weber distinguished *Macht* (power) from *Herrschaft* — a term usually translated as authority, domination, or (literally) "mastership." ✅ Weber "distinguished three ideal types of legitimate political leadership/domination/authority (German: *Herrschaft*, lit. 'mastership')" and wrote that "authority (as distinct from power [German: *Macht*]) is power accepted as legitimate by those subjected to it" ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority), citing Weber 1922/1978: 215–216 and Weber 1919/2015: 137–138). The three types — charismatic, traditional and rational-legal — are treated in §3 below.

Two consequences follow for the practitioner. First, **legitimacy is the load-bearing element**: the same instruction lands differently depending on whether the person receiving it believes the giver is entitled to give it. Second, **legitimacy has sources** (tradition, law, personal gift), and a modern organisation leans overwhelmingly on the rational-legal source — office, rule, role — which is precisely why rational-legal authority feels impersonal, transferable, and (crucially) **renewable only by continuing to satisfy the rules and the role's competence expectations**.

### 1.3 The Acceptance Turn — Barnard, Simon and the Zone

Chester Barnard, the AT&T executive whose 1938 book *The Functions of the Executive* founded the acceptance theory of authority, pushed the definition one step further toward the follower. ✅ Barnard's account holds that "what makes a communication authoritative rests with the subordinate, rather than with his superior," and that managers should "obtain authority by treating subordinates with respect and competence" ([Wikipedia, *Chester Barnard*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_Barnard)). This is the **acceptance theory**: an order is authoritative only when the person receiving it accepts it as within the scope of what they owe the organisation.

Barnard's related concept — the **"zone of indifference"** (the range of orders a person will accept without conscious questioning, because they fall inside what the person treats as their job) — is standard in the management literature and is claimed in §13 as ⚠-knowledge, not ✅: the phrase is the canonical Barnard teaching, but this pass verified the acceptance theory against the reference page and did not verify the exact phrase against *The Functions of the Executive* itself. Herbert Simon, who built on Barnard, refined the same idea as a **"zone of acceptance"** and folded it into bounded rationality; that attribution is likewise ⚠-knowledge here.

The practical upshot of the acceptance turn is a test: **an authority you must enforce on every occasion is not authority — it is coercion with paperwork.** Authority is the condition in which most people, most of the time, do the right thing without being compelled, because they see the request as legitimate. That is why "authority skills" are teachable: the zone is not fixed. It widens when the leader's competence, fairness and consistency build, and it narrows — sometimes catastrophically — after a breach.

### 1.4 Authority in the Organisational-Behaviour Canon

The organisation-behaviour (OB) canon treats authority as the legitimate base *within* a taxonomy of power. ✅ The repository's [organizational_behavior_guide.md](organizational_behavior_guide.md) §4.4 carries the canonical five-base table (coercive, reward, legitimate, expert, referent — French and Raven 1959) and the standard research findings: people respond best to expert and referent power and worst to coercive power, and the bases are substitutable (an expert can lead without legitimate position). The OB definition of **power** — "the capacity of A to influence B's behavior despite B's resistance" — is the necessary foil to authority: authority is the *legitimate* slice of that capacity, and it is the slice that does not require resistance to be overcome each time. This guide does not re-derive the OB treatment; it verifies the primary sources under it and builds the skills layer on top.

### 1.6 The Practical Stakes — Why the Distinction Earns Its Keep

The definitional apparatus is not academic throat-clearing; it decides behaviour in four recurring situations.

First, **when the instruction is refused.** A manager whose order is declined can respond in one of two ways: raise the coercive pressure (a power move: sanctions, escalation, performance process) or repair the legitimacy (an authority move: find out whether the request is seen as within the person's zone of indifference, whether the competence or fairness of the decision is doubted, and address that). The first wins the moment and spends the relationship; the second is slower and compounds. Knowing that authority rests on acceptance tells you which lever you are actually pulling.

Second, **when authority must be borrowed.** In every matrix or cross-functional setting (§11.1), the senior professional must act on authority they do not hold — invoking another's mandate, another's standard, another's expertise. This is legitimate *only* if the borrowing is honest and repaid; invoking a mandate you do not have, or an approval you have not received, converts borrowed authority into a credibility withdrawal that is very hard to reverse.

Third, **when authority is contested by an equal.** Two peers with overlapping remits resolve most disputes not by rank (there is none) but by whose legitimacy is stronger on the specific matter — whose expertise is more relevant, whose track record is more credible, whose interests are more evidently aligned with the organisation. That is the power taxonomy (§2) in daily use.

Fourth, **when the leader must lead change.** Change is where authority is most tested, because it requires people to accept direction that departs from custom (tradition, §3.2) and often from their own interest. A leader who has only position power can change a process; a leader with earned authority can change a culture. The difference between the two is exactly the subject of this guide.

## 2. The Sources — French and Raven's Bases of Power

### 2.1 The 1959 Paper and the Five Bases

The canonical taxonomy of where social power comes from is **John R. P. French Jr. and Bertram Raven, "The Bases of Social Power" (1959)**, originally published as a chapter in **Dorwin Cartwright (ed.), *Studies in Social Power* (University of Michigan Press)**. ✅ The American Psychological Association's PsycNet record cites it precisely — "French, J. R. P., Jr., & Raven, B. (1959). The bases of social power. In D. Cartwright (Ed.), *Studies in social power* (pp. 150–167). Univ. Michigan" — and abstracts the five bases as "**referent power, expert power, reward power, coercive power, and legitimate power**" ([APA PsycNet record 1960-06701-004](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1960-06701-004)). ✅ The original paper's own summary states: "We have distinguished five types of power: referent power, expert power, reward power, coercive power, and legitimate power" ([archived copy of the 1959 chapter](https://ia800601.us.archive.org/21/items/15341_Readings/15341_Readings/Power/French_&_Raven_The_Bases_of_Social_Power_ch32_pp607-623_text.pdf), surfaced this pass).

French and Raven's definitional move is important and often lost: ✅ they defined **social influence** as "a change in the belief, attitude, or behavior of a person (the target of influence) which results from the action of another person (an influencing agent)," and **social power** as *the potential* for such influence — the agent's available resources for producing it ([Wikipedia, *French and Raven's bases of power*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Raven%27s_bases_of_power), citing Raven 2012). Power, in other words, is capacity; influence is realised change. Authority is a *kind* of power — the legitimate kind — and its exercise is a kind of influence.

### 2.2 Raven's Informational Addition (1965)

The five-base taxonomy did not stay at five. ✅ "This was followed by Raven's subsequent addition in 1965 of a sixth separate and distinct base of power: **informational power**" ([Wikipedia, *French and Raven's bases of power*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Raven%27s_bases_of_power), citing Raven 1965). Informational power is the power of having and transmitting *information* — the argument, the fact, the explanation — that changes what the target believes, as distinct from the legitimate power of the office, the referent power of the relationship, the expert power of the skill, and the reward/coercive powers of the incentive. The distinction between **expert power** (the target defers because the agent is expert) and **informational power** (the target is persuaded by the information regardless of the agent's expertise) is subtle and practically crucial for the knowledge worker: the first is a reputation, the second is a message.

This sixth base is the honest bridge to the modern knowledge organisation. In a bank or a technology function, most day-to-day influence is informational and expert, not legitimate: a well-evidenced architecture recommendation moves a decision that no one is formally obliged to accept.

### 2.3 The Restatements — 1992/1993 and the Six Bases

The taxonomy has been repeatedly elaborated, and the elaborations matter because they are what the modern leadership literature actually cites. ✅ The reference account records that "since then, the model has gone through very significant developments: coercion and reward can have **personal as well as impersonal forms**; expert and referent power can be **negative or positive**; legitimate power, in addition to **position power**, may be based on other **normative obligations: reciprocity, equity, and responsibility**; information may be utilized in **direct or indirect** fashion" ([Wikipedia, *French and Raven's bases of power*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_and_Raven%27s_bases_of_power), citing **Raven 1992**). The same source traces the further differentiation into impersonal/personal coercion and reward; formal legitimacy (position power) plus legitimacy of reciprocity, equity and dependence; positive/negative expert and referent; and direct/indirect information.

The practical value of the restatements is that they convert a flat list into a **design space**. "Legitimate power" is not one thing: formal position power is the weakest and least renewable of the legitimate obligations, while reciprocity-based and equity-based legitimacy — the sense that you have given as much as you ask, and that you treat people as they deserve — are far more durable. A leader who leans only on position power is using the most brittle base in the taxonomy; a leader who builds reciprocity and equity legitimacy is compounding.

### 2.4 The Power/Interaction Model and Raven 2008

Raven's mature framework is the **Power/Interaction Model of Interpersonal Influence**. ✅ Bertram Raven (1926–2020), a UCLA psychologist and Leon Festinger's doctoral student, is described as developing his work on interpersonal influence and social power into "a broader reaching **Power/Interaction Model of Interpersonal Influence**," which "has been applied to organizational power relationships," health psychology, close relationships and educational settings, with a Power/Interaction Inventory developed for cross-cultural comparison across collaborating researchers in 14 nations ([Wikipedia, *Bertram Raven*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertram_Raven), citing the UCLA obituary and Raven's professional profile). The 2008 paper — "The Bases of Power and the Power/Interaction Model of Interpersonal Influence," published in *Analyses of Social Issues and Public Policy* — is the standard citation for the mature model and is flagged ⚠ in §13 because this pass verified Raven's Power/Interaction line of work and the 1992 restatement through the reference page but did **not** verify the 2008 paper's exact journal, volume and pages against a primary record.

### 2.5 The Practitioner Additions and Yukl's Taxonomy

Beyond the academic line, the leadership-training world added its own bases. The most-cited practitioner addition is **connection power** — power derived from whom you know and who owes you — usually attributed to the Hersey–Blanchard situational-leadership tradition, which (in various editions) presents a seven-base set alongside the French–Raven five. These additions are ⚠ **practitioner-origin** and are flagged as such: they are useful teaching devices and they recur across the training literature, but they are not part of French and Raven's verified taxonomy, and attributions of exactly who added what, in which edition, vary across sources. A second family is the **influence-tactic taxonomies** in the organisational-behaviour research tradition — most associated with Gary Yukl and colleagues — which classify *behaviours* (rational persuasion, inspirational appeals, consultation, ingratiation, exchange, personal appeals, coalition tactics, legitimating tactics, pressure) rather than *bases*. ⚠-knowledge: Yukl's taxonomy is standard in the OB literature, but the specific tactic lists and their labels vary by study and were not re-verified this pass. The point of the two additions is not to expand the canon but to mark the boundary: the five (then six) bases are the verified backbone; the three-to-seven-base practitioner variants sit on top of it as teaching tools.

| **Legitimate** | Recognised right to influence (position, role, norm) | Medium via position, high via reciprocity/equity | The formal chair: necessary, brittle if used alone (§9) |
| **Reward** | Ability to give valued outcomes | Conditional on resources | Works while the reward is contingent and credible |
| **Coercive** | Ability to punish | Low — breeds resistance | The most fragile and most resented base |
| **Expert** | Knowledge and skill | High while current | The staff-engineer default; decays with staleness |
| **Referent** | Identification with, liking of, respect for the agent | High, but slow to earn | The strongest relational base |
| **Informational** | The content of the message itself | High, but message-specific | Added 1965; the knowledge worker's everyday lever |

✅ Bases and definitions per the 1959 paper and the 1965 addition (APA PsycNet; Wikipedia *French and Raven's bases of power*). Durability ordering is the standard research account carried in [organizational_behavior_guide.md](organizational_behavior_guide.md) §4.4 (expert and referent beat coercive), ⚠-knowledge for the finer ordering.

### 2.7 The Contested Additions — Flagged, Not Smoothed

Three things must not be smoothed over. First, ✅ the **number itself is not fixed**: the 1959 paper is a five-base paper and the informational sixth is a 1965 addition, so a source that says "French and Raven's five bases" and a source that says "the six bases of power" can both be citing the same tradition at different dates. Second, ⚠ the **practitioner additions** (connection power, and the "7-base" and "9-base" variants) are training-industry elaborations, not part of the verified taxonomy; their attributions are inconsistent across sources and one should not cite them as if French and Raven authored them. Third, ⚠ **Raven 2008's exact bibliographic details** are flagged — the mature model is real and the Power/Interaction title is verified, but the publication record was not confirmed against a primary page this pass.

### 2.8 The Bases in Combination

Points of taxonomy matter because real influence is rarely one base at a time; the skilled operator knows which base is doing the work and which is being leaned on past its limit.

- **Expert plus referent is the durable pair.** Expertise earns deference on the matter; referent regard earns the benefit of the doubt on the person. Together they are what §9 calls personal authority, and they are the only bases that survive a change of employer intact.
- **Legitimate plus informational is the everyday pair.** In a bank or a technology function, most decisions move because someone with the right office puts a well-evidenced proposal in front of a group. Remove the office and the proposal still often carries (informational power); remove the evidence and the office must push (position power), which is the more expensive mode.
- **Reward plus coercive is the brittle pair.** These are the "compliance" bases: they produce action that lasts as long as the surveillance or the incentive, and they generate the counter-behaviours (gaming, concealment, exit) that §11.4 catalogues. They are not unusable — deadlines exist — but a leader who lives on them is renting, not owning, their authority.
- **The substitution effect.** Because the bases are substitutable (✅ [organizational_behavior_guide.md](organizational_behavior_guide.md) §4.4), a leader weak on one base can compensate with another: the manager with little referent warmth but high expert credibility will be consulted even when not liked; the beloved manager with thin expertise will be liked and quietly bypassed on the technical call. Diagnosis means asking *which base is short* before deciding which to build.

The practical warning is **overloading**. A leader who uses referent warmth to cover a competence gap, or position power to cover a relationship deficit, is spending a base on a purpose it was never meant to serve — and the base depletes faster than it would in its intended role.

## 3. The Types — Weber's Three Types of Legitimate Authority

### 3.1 The Three Ideal Types

Weber's tripartite classification is the second pillar of the definitional foundation. ✅ Weber "distinguished three ideal types of legitimate political leadership/domination/authority" — **charismatic**, **traditional** and **rational-legal** — and wrote about them both in his essay "The Three Types of Legitimate Rule" and in his 1921 masterwork *Economy and Society* (see Weber 1922/1978: 215–216), as well as in the 1919 speech *Politics as a Vocation* (Weber 1919/2015: 137–138) ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority)). ✅ The same source records Weber's claim that "authority (as distinct from power) is power accepted as legitimate by those subjected to it," and that the three forms appear in a "hierarchical development order" from charismatic, to traditional, to rational-legal.

### 3.2 Traditional Authority

**Traditional authority** rests on the sanctity of established custom and the legitimacy of those who rule under it — the patriarch, the patrimonial lord, the inherited office. ✅ The reference account associates traditional authority with "patriarchs, patrimonialism, feudalism" ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority)). In organisational form, traditional authority survives as seniority, "the way we've always done it," founder authority and family-firm hierarchy — legitimate because it is old rather than because it is efficient. It is stable and slow-moving, and it is the form most threatened by rapid change, because change is precisely a claim that custom is no longer binding.

### 3.3 Rational-Legal Authority

**Rational-legal authority** rests on rules and offices: the person has authority because the position is defined by law or procedure, not because of who they are. ✅ Associated with "modern law and state, bureaucracy" ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority)), and described by Weber as "the most advanced" form, toward which societies evolve because charismatic authority is unstable and must "routinize" into a structured form. Every corporate org chart, delegation-of-authority matrix and approvals policy is a rational-legal instrument. Its virtues are impersonality and transferability — the role can be handed over — and its characteristic weakness is that it authorises the *office* but not the *person*: a new holder of a rational-legal office inherits the authority of the position and none of the earned authority of the predecessor (§5, §9).

### 3.4 Charismatic Authority

**Charismatic authority** grows out of "the personal charm or the strength of an individual personality" — Weber described it in a lecture as "the authority of the extraordinary and personal gift of grace (charisma)," and distinguished it with: "Men do not obey him [the charismatic ruler] by virtue of tradition or statute, but because they believe in him." ✅ Because followers must keep legitimising the leader, it is "particularly difficult for charismatic leaders to maintain their authority," and when the leader loses charisma or dies the system tends to transform into traditional or legal-rational authority ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority)). This is the purest form of **personal** authority: legitimate because of the person, not the office — the ancestor of §9's personal-versus-positional distinction. It is also the least durable and the hardest to institutionalise, which is why the mature move is to convert personal authority into the organisation's rules, roles and successors.

### 3.5 The Translation and Terminology Debates

⚠ The terminology here is genuinely contested, and the honest guide says so. Weber's German word is ***Herrschaft***, whose literal sense is closer to "mastership" or "lordship" than to the English "authority"; standard translations render it "authority," "domination" or "rule," and the choice changes the reading. ✅ The reference account itself glosses *Herrschaft* as "lit. 'mastership'" ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority)). The widely used **Roth and Wittich** English edition of *Economy and Society* (1978) is the source of the page cites above; a separate English translation of the "Three Types of Legitimate Rule" essay by Hans Gerth appeared in 1958. ⚠ A further reading in the scholarly literature treats Weber's *Herrschaft* through an "**imperative coordination**" lens (authority as the probability that a command will be obeyed in a given group) rather than a purely normative one — a contested interpretation, flagged rather than asserted. The practitioner consequence is modest but real: when you say "authority," you may mean the *right* to command (normative), the *fact* of command (sociological), or the *legal office* (juridical) — and the listener may mean a different one.

### 3.6 The Ideal-Type Caveat

✅ The three types "are ideal types and rarely appear in their pure form" ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority)). No real leader is purely rational-legal; every functioning office is animated by some mixture of position, tradition, personal credibility and the charisma of competence. The ideal types are analytic lenses, useful for diagnosing which kind of legitimacy a leader is currently drawing on — and therefore which kind is at risk. The leader whose only legitimacy is the office is one restructuring away from having none; the leader whose legitimacy is purely personal is one departure away from leaving none behind.

### 3.7 The Three Types in a Modern Organisation

Weber's types were built for states, but they map cleanly onto the modern bank, and the mapping is diagnostic.

| Weberian type | Modern organisational form | What it buys | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** | Founder authority, seniority, "the way we do things," inherited client relationships | Stability, continuity, deference | Change initiatives; new-entrant competition |
| **Rational-legal** | The org chart, delegation of authority, policy, grade, committee mandate | Transferable, impersonal, scale | Personal followership; a new holder's first year; matrices (§11.1) |
| **Charismatic** | The respected expert or leader whose personal reputation commands the room | Speed, discretionary effort, loyalty | Succession; the leader's absence; scale beyond the room |

The diagnostic use is simple: **when authority is not working, ask which type is being relied on and whether the situation still supports it.** A manager leaning on rational-legal authority to drive a change that requires discretionary effort is using a compliance tool for a commitment problem. A respected expert leaning on charismatic authority to run a governance process is using a personal tool for a rule-bound task. The mature leader blends the three deliberately — the office for the accountability, the custom for the continuity, the person for the energy — and knows that only the rational-legal one can be handed over.

### 3.8 A Note on the "Imperative Coordination" Reading

⚠ Beyond the three-type framework, a distinct reading of Weber treats *Herrschaft* as **imperative coordination** — the probability that a command within a given group will be obeyed — which is a more sociological, less normative definition than "legitimate authority" (§3.5). This reading tends to be used in organisation theory to define "authority" as *de facto* obedience to commands rather than *de jure* legitimacy. It is flagged as an interpretation rather than asserted as Weber's own settled meaning; the practitioner can use it as the reminder that authority is measured **by whether the command is obeyed**, not by whether the org chart says it should be.

## 4. The Distinction — Authority, Power and Influence

### 4.1 Dahl's A→B Formulation (1957)

The conceptual foundation of modern power analysis is **Robert A. Dahl, "The Concept of Power" (1957)**, which proposed the intuitive relational formulation that **A has power over B to the extent that A can get B to do something B would not otherwise do.** ⚠-knowledge: this formulation is the canonical textbook statement and the near-universal way the concept is taught, but this pass did not fetch a primary record for the 1957 article (published in *Behavioral Science*); it is stated here as standard knowledge and flagged in §13. Its analytic virtue is that it forces the analyst to specify **who** (A), **over whom** (B), **about what scope**, and **with what observable change** — and its limitation, which the next subsections supply, is that it only captures power that is *exercised and visible*.

### 4.2 Macht versus Herrschaft

The distinction that anchors this guide is Weber's. ✅ Weber separated **power (*Macht*)** from **authority (*Herrschaft*)**, with authority being "power accepted as legitimate by those subjected to it" ([Wikipedia, *Tripartite classification of authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tripartite_classification_of_authority); [Wikipedia, *Authority*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority)). Power is the ability; authority is the ability *plus* the recognition of the right. In practice the distinction shows up as two different currencies: raw power (including coercion and resource control) can *win the moment* and can *lose the relationship*; authority can win the moment **and** carry forward, because it does not consume legitimacy in the winning. The distinction between power and authority, and the third term **influence**, is best seen as a chain: *power* is the capacity, *authority* is legitimate capacity, and *influence* is the actual production of change — sometimes by authority, sometimes by power, sometimes by argument alone.

### 4.3 Wrong's Forms and Bases

**Dennis H. Wrong's *Power: Its Forms, Bases and Uses*** (1979; later editions) is the standard scholarly systematisation of the forms (force, manipulation, persuasion, authority) and the bases of power, and it is the work that most cleanly separates **authority** (including coercive, legitimate and personal varieties) from the other forms. ⚠-knowledge: Wrong's framework is standard and widely cited, but this pass did not verify the book's exact contents, edition or pagination; it is presented as the standard reference rather than as freshly verified. For the practitioner its value is the reminder that **persuasion** and **manipulation** are distinct forms of influence — one discloses its aim, the other does not — and that a leader who drifts from persuasion into manipulation has changed the *kind* of power being used, not merely its strength.

### 4.4 The Two Faces (Bachrach and Baratz 1962)

**Peter Bachrach and Morton S. Baratz, "Two Faces of Power" (1962, *American Political Science Review*)**, added the second face: power is not only getting B to do what B would not otherwise do (the first face, Dahl's), but also **keeping issues off the agenda** so that the conflict never becomes visible. ⚠ The "faces" framing is flagged as a **contested-adjacent interpretation** rather than a settled doctrine: the two-faces article is real and canonical, but this pass did not verify it against a primary record, and the "faces/dimensions" vocabulary is sometimes presented as a level-by-level hierarchy when the original arguments are more tangled. The practitioner translation is real, though: **agenda control is authority's quietest form.** Whoever sets the agenda, the criteria and the options has exercised authority before anyone speaks.

### 4.5 Lukes' Three Dimensions (1974)

**Steven Lukes, *Power: A Radical View* (1974)**, generalised the insight to a third dimension: power can shape the *preferences themselves* — so that B does not even recognise a conflict of interest, having internalised A's view of what is desirable. ✅ Lukes (born 1941) is a British political and social theorist, formerly professor at the University of Siena, the European University Institute and the LSE, and at NYU until 2021; his work includes "**the three dimensions of power**" ([Wikipedia, *Steven Lukes*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Lukes)). ⚠ **Flagged:** the three-dimensional model is a canonical scholarly framework, but the "faces versus dimensions" lineage (Dahl → Bachrach–Baratz → Lukes) is a **contested-adjacent interpretation** — a useful synthesis that flattens three different arguments into one ladder, and one that some political theorists dispute. Used carefully, it is a diagnostic: at which level is this decision *really* being made — the visible one (who won), the semi-visible one (whose issue never got onto the agenda), or the invisible one (whose interests are not seen as a conflict at all)?

### 4.6 The Distinction Table

| Term | Short definition | Verified citation | Practice signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Power** | Capacity to produce an effect on another's behaviour | ✅ Weber's *Macht* (Wikipedia *Tripartite*); ⚠ Dahl 1957 A→B | The ability, whether or not it is accepted |
| **Authority** | Power accepted as legitimate | ✅ Wikipedia *Authority*; Weber (*Herrschaft*) | Compliance without each-time enforcement |
| **Influence** | Actual production of change in belief, attitude or behaviour | ✅ French & Raven 1959 definition of social influence | The realised effect, whatever the source |
| **Coercion** | Influence by threat or force | ✅ French & Raven base (coercive power); Wrong (form) | Legitimacy-consumptive; breeds resistance |
| **Persuasion** | Influence by argument, disclosed | ⚠ Wrong (form); ✅ informational/expert bases | Legitimacy-neutral or -building |
| **Manipulation** | Influence that conceals its aim | ⚠ Wrong (form) | Legitimacy-destroying if discovered |

### 4.7 The Contested-Adjacent Flag

⚠ Two honest caveats close this section. First, the "faces" (Bachrach–Baratz) and "dimensions" (Lukes) vocabulary is **not a single verified hierarchy**; it is a scholarly synthesis that is broadly accepted and frequently oversimplified, and this guide flags it as contested-adjacent rather than ✅. Second, the neat definitions above are *stipulative* — they fix usage for this guide — and different literatures draw the lines differently (some treat authority as a form of power, others as analytically distinct; some treat persuasion as a form of power, others as outside it). The skills argument in the rest of the guide uses the stipulations in §4.6 and names its sources, so a reader who prefers a different drawing of the lines can still follow the practical claims.

### 4.8 A Worked Distinction — Same Act, Different Legitimacy

The distinction becomes concrete when the *same observable act* is classified by its source. Consider a manager telling a team member to redo an analysis by Friday. The act is identical; the classification is not:

| Reading | The source of the influence | The failure mode if it is wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **An exercise of authority** | The role's right to set the deadline, accepted as legitimate | Resentment if the acceptance was assumed and not present |
| **An exercise of power** | The ability to withhold approval or escalate | Compliance, then concealment; the relationship is spent |
| **An exercise of influence** | A well-evidenced reason the rework matters | The reason is not believed; the instruction has no weight |
| **An exercise of coercion** | An implicit or explicit threat | Resentment, exit, sabotage; the most expensive way to get it done |
| **An exercise of manipulation** | A concealed aim — the real reason is not disclosed | Legitimacy destroyed if discovered (§4.6) |

The practical value of the table is diagnostic, not moralistic. All five can produce the Friday deadline. Only the first three leave the leader's authority intact or better; coercion spends it, and manipulation can obliterate it. A leader who cannot tell which one they are doing is flying blind — and the tell is usually the *reason* the other person gave for complying, which the leader only learns if they ask.

## 5. The Establishment — How Authority Is Earned

### 5.1 The Earned-versus-Granted Distinction

Position can be *granted*; authority must be *earned*. The rational-legal office (§3.3) arrives with the appointment letter; the acceptance theory (§1.3) says the acceptance that makes it real is not in the letter. This is why two people can hold the same grade and command very different degrees of compliance, and why a new holder of an old office can find the authority does not transfer. The skills question is therefore: what earns acceptance? Across the leadership literature and the power research, three currencies recur — **competence** (you know what you are doing), **credibility** (you tell the truth and your word is reliable) and **consideration** (you are seen to have the interests of others and the organisation at heart). The first two are what the leadership-credibility literature calls the foundation; the third is what the trust literature (§8.1) calls benevolence.

### 5.2 The Leadership-Credibility Literature

The practitioner anchor is **James Kouzes and Barry Z. Posner, *The Leadership Challenge***. ✅ The book was first published in 1987 by Wiley; its seventh edition was released in 2023, and it presents "The Five Practices of Exemplary Leadership" — **Model the Way, Inspire a Shared Vision, Challenge the Process, Enable Others to Act, and Encourage the Heart** — developed from surveys beginning in 1983, in which the authors asked people "What do you do as a leader when you're performing at your personal best?" and gathered thousands of interviews and roughly 75,000 written responses ([Wikipedia, *The Leadership Challenge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Leadership_Challenge)). ⚠ The frequently quoted line that "**credibility is the foundation of leadership**" is Kouzes and Posner's signature claim — the central thesis of the earlier *Credibility* (1993) and a recurring framing of *The Leadership Challenge* — but the exact wording, edition and page were not verified this pass; it is flagged ⚠-knowledge. What is verified is that the framework exists, has been revised through seven editions, and places credibility and modelling-the-way at the front.

**Warren Bennis and Burt Nanus, *Leaders: Strategies for Taking Charge* (1985)**, is the second pillar of the credibility literature, built on interviews with a large sample of leaders and distilled into the claim that leaders manage attention, meaning, trust and self. ✅ Warren Bennis (1925–2014) was a pioneer of the contemporary field of leadership studies, University Professor at USC, founding chairman of its Leadership Institute, and earlier provost at SUNY-Buffalo and president of the University of Cincinnati; he published "Revisionist Theory of Leadership" in *Harvard Business Review* in 1961 and helped establish leadership as a field ([Wikipedia, *Warren Bennis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_Bennis)). ⚠ The specific four-part framework and the study details of *Leaders* (1985) are flagged ⚠-knowledge — the book and its standing are standard, but the interview count and the exact phrasing were not re-verified.

### 5.3 Kotter and the Leadership-versus-Management Divide

**John Kotter's "What Leaders Really Do" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1990)** is the canonical articulation of the distinction that the whole "authority skills" genre leans on: **management copes with complexity** (planning, budgeting, organising, controlling) while **leadership copes with change** (setting direction, aligning people, motivating and inspiring) — two different, both necessary systems. ✅ John P. Kotter is the Konosuke Matsushita Professor of Leadership, Emeritus, at Harvard Business School (teaching there from 1972), founder of Kotter International, author of many books including *Leading Change* (1996) and its eight-step model, and an early writer on power and management (*Power in Management*, 1979) ([Wikipedia, *John Kotter*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Kotter)). ⚠ The exact 1990 article citation and wording are flagged ⚠-knowledge.

⚠ **The dichotomy is itself contested, and the guide flags it in place.** Henry Mintzberg — the McGill management scholar and author of *The Nature of Managerial Work*, *Structure in Fives* and *The Rise and Fall of Strategic Planning* ([Wikipedia, *Henry Mintzberg*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Mintzberg)) — argued that the sharp separation of "leaders" from "managers" is a false and damaging dichotomy: real managers *do* lead, and isolating a leadership caste from the work of managing produces the very disconnection (and hubris) that the separation was meant to cure. The practical reading is therefore not "managers plan, leaders inspire" but: **the same person must be able to do both** — and authority is earned in the doing of the mundane managerial work (delivering, deciding, protecting the team) at least as much as in the inspirational set-piece. A leader who cannot manage loses authority in the details; a manager who cannot lead loses it in the change.

### 5.4 Competence, Credibility and the Three Currencies

| Currency | What earns it | French-and-Raven mapping | Failure signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Competence** | Delivering results; being right often enough; knowing the domain | Expert power (§2.6) | Authority erodes the moment decisions fail |
| **Credibility** | Truth-telling, promise-keeping, consistency, owning mistakes | Informational + referent (partial) | Nobody believes the forecast or the assurance |
| **Consideration** | Fairness, defending the team, giving credit, sharing credit | Referent + reciprocity legitimacy (§2.3) | Compliance without commitment; quiet quitting |

The three currencies map onto different French–Raven bases (✅ bases verified §2; the mapping is this guide's synthesis, ⚠). The point of separating them is diagnostic: when authority feels thin, the leader can ask *which currency is short* — is it that people doubt my competence (results problem), my honesty (credibility problem), or my care (relationship problem)? The repair move differs sharply by currency, and treating a credibility problem as a competence problem (or vice versa) usually makes it worse.

### 5.5 The Establishment Sequence

The practical sequence for earning authority in a new role — consistent with the first-90-days literature and cross-referenced rather than duplicated here ([the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md)) — is: (1) **listen and diagnose** before asserting; (2) **land one visible, uncontested win** to establish competence; (3) **keep the small promises** to establish credibility; (4) **protect people from avoidable pain** to establish consideration; (5) only then **spend** the accumulated legitimacy on a hard decision. The sequence matters because authority is a *stock*, not a flow: it is accumulated by consistent small deposits and spent on the decisions that require it. A leader who spends before depositing must fall back on position power (§9) — and position power, used to cover an overdraft, depreciates fast.

### 5.6 The Common Missteps in Earning Authority

Five missteps recur, and each has a signature and a correction.

1. **Asserting before diagnosing.** The new leader arrives with a plan and announces it in week two, before the listening is done. The signals are visible (the org chart reorganised before anyone was asked) and the cost is that every subsequent idea is read as *mine* rather than *ours*. Correction: the §5.5 front-load of listening, and a written "what I heard" that demonstrates it.
2. **Confusing visibility with authority.** The leader whose moves are all announcements — town halls, memos, all-hands — accumulates *profile* and very little *acceptance*. Authority is built in small private deposits (a promise kept to one person) at least as much as in public set-pieces. Correction: keep the small promises; count them.
3. **Consuming the credibility reserve on trivia.** Correcting a minor point in a meeting to be right, when it does not matter, spends the same currency that the next genuinely contested decision will need. Correction: ask whether being right here is worth the future cost of being believed there.
4. **Letting competence and title diverge.** The Peter problem (§11.4) in slow motion: the leader stops learning the domain while the title keeps growing, and authority follows the domain down. Correction: keep a hands-on, first-hand exposure that is visible to the people whose respect matters.
5. **Treating the mandate as the ceiling.** The leader who only ever does what the delegation matrix permits never earns the discretionary following that authority is for. Correction: spend a calibrated amount of earned legitimacy on something the role does not strictly require (§5.5 step 5) — the protected colleague, the contested-but-right call — and let the acceptance grow.

The through-line is that authority is **accumulated in small units and spent in large ones**, and most missteps are errors of sequencing — spending before depositing, or depositing in public where the currency does not accrue.

## 6. The Exercise — Decision Rights, Delegation and Accountability

### 6.1 The Decision-Rights Economics

Authority in an organisation is operationalised as **decision rights**: who is entitled to decide what. The classic economics-of-organisation claim is that decision rights should be **co-located with the knowledge** needed to make the decision — if the specific knowledge lives at the front line and the general knowledge lives at the centre, the design choice is to move the rights to the knowledge or the knowledge to the rights, and the cost of doing neither is bad decisions plus information games. ⚠-knowledge: this is the argument of **Michael C. Jensen and William H. Meckling, "Specific and General Knowledge, and Organizational Structure"** (a foundational paper of the property-rights/organisational-economics literature, appearing in a 1992 volume and later in the *Journal of Applied Corporate Finance*). The attribution and the argument are standard, but this pass did not verify the paper against a primary record — it is flagged ⚠ in §13. The practitioner consequence is robust regardless: **a mismatch between where knowledge sits and where rights sit is the structural cause of most "the wrong people are deciding" complaints** — and one of the most durable ways a leader earns authority is to move rights to where the knowledge actually is (delegate down) or to move knowledge toward the decision (consulting up).

### 6.2 The Practitioner Decision Frameworks

The decision-rights literature has a practitioner layer, and it should be presented as such:

- **RACI / the responsibility assignment matrix** — ✅ verified: a project-management technique that assigns each stakeholder one of four roles for a deliverable: **Responsible** (does the work), **Accountable** (ultimately answerable and signs off; conventionally only one per task), **Consulted** (two-way input, e.g. subject-matter experts) and **Informed** (one-way updates). It is also called a linear responsibility chart, and it appears in the PMI's *PMBOK Guide* (7th ed., 2021) ([Wikipedia, *Responsibility assignment matrix*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsibility_assignment_matrix)).
- **DACI** — ✅ verified as a listed alternative: **Driver, Approvers, Contributors, Informed** ([Wikipedia, *Responsibility assignment matrix*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Responsibility_assignment_matrix)). ⚠ The attribution of DACI to Intuit and its internal training contexts is practitioner lore and is flagged.
- **RAPID** — ⚠-knowledge: **Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide**, associated with Bain & Company's decision-rights practice. It is standard in the consulting literature and the most decision-centric of the three (it names a single *Decider*), but this pass did not verify it against a primary Bain source; it is flagged ⚠.

The frameworks are not rules; they are **language for negotiating rights explicitly rather than discovering them by collision**. Their common core — one accountable owner, explicit consulted parties, explicit informed parties, and a named decider — is the operational definition of a working authority structure.

### 6.3 Delegation and Accountability

**Delegation** is where authority is transferred along with work; **accountability** is where it is answered for. Two principles recur. First, **delegate the decision, not just the task** — a delegation that hands over the work but retains every decision has not delegated authority at all, and produces the micromanagement failure mode of §11.4. Second, **accountability follows authority**: if someone is held accountable for an outcome while being denied the authority to act on it, the structure is not accountability but scapegoating, and it destroys the credibility currency of §5.4.

⚠ **The accountability literature.** The standard scholarly treatment is **Jennifer S. Lerner and Philip E. Tetlock, "Accounting for the Effects of Accountability" (*Psychological Bulletin*, 1999)**, a review arguing that accountability is not uniformly good or bad: it improves accuracy, self-critical processing and complexity of judgement when the decision-maker does not know the audience's views (pre-decisional, "pre-emptive" accountability) but can produce defensive, self-justifying or conformity-driven behaviour when the audience is known or the process is post-hoc ("retrospective" accountability). ⚠-knowledge: this review is genuine and much-cited, but this pass did not verify the paper against a primary record; the two-paradigm reading (pre- vs post-decisional) is the standard account and is flagged ⚠ in §13. The practical consequence is sharp: **accountability that is announced in advance and invites the decision-maker to reason openly builds authority; accountability that appears after the fact as a search for someone to blame destroys it.** A leader who is seen to weaponise accountability — to punish with hindsight — loses the informational honesty of their team first, and their authority next.

### 6.4 The Authority Exercise Checklist

| Question | What a good answer sounds like |
|---|---|
| Where is the knowledge? | Named; and the rights sit with it, or the consultation path is explicit |
| Who is the single decider? | One named person, not a committee's fog |
| Who is consulted, and when? | Named, before the decision, with their input actually weighed |
| Who is accountable? | The same person who has the authority to act |
| Is the delegation real? | The delegate can decide; the delegator does not re-decide |
| Is accountability announced in advance? | Yes — and it invites reasoning, not blame (§6.3) |

### 6.5 Decision-Rights Anti-Patterns

The frameworks fail in predictable ways, and naming the anti-pattern is most of the fix.

| Anti-pattern | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Accountability without authority** | Someone is blamed for an outcome they had no right to act on | Move the right to the accountable person, or move the accountability to the right-holder (§6.3) |
| **Authority without accountability** | Someone can decide but never answers for it | Attach the outcome and the review to the right, in advance |
| **The committee decider** | Every meeting ends with "let's discuss further"; nobody can say yes | Name the single Decider (RAPID's D; RACI's single A) and record it |
| **Delegation theatre** | Work is handed over; every decision is re-made by the delegator | Delegate the decision, and let the delegate be wrong within the bounds (§6.3) |
| **The self-appointed decider** | A strong personality decides on matters reserved to a colleague | Re-publish the rights map; escalate the boundary, not the outcome (§12.4) |
| **Criteria drift** | The basis of a decision changes after the fact | Write the criteria before the decision, and hold them through it |

The through-line is the same as §6.1: **rights, knowledge and accountability should line up.** Every anti-pattern above is a misalignment, and most are resolved not by a reorganisation but by the leader having the conversation that names the misalignment out loud — which is itself an exercise of authority (a legitimate one) rather than an exercise of power.

## 7. The Hard Moments — Difficult Conversations and Command Presence

### 7.1 The Authority Gradient

The most useful verified concept in the human-factors literature for the authority skills is the **authority gradient**: the perceived difference in power or expertise between a leader and their team, and the extent to which that difference silences the team. Its home discipline is **crew resource management (CRM)**. ✅ CRM is "a set of training procedures for use in environments where human error can have devastating effects," focused on interpersonal communication, leadership and decision-making in cockpits; its founder is described as **David Beaty** (a former RAF and BOAC pilot who wrote *The Human Factor in Aircraft Accidents*, 1969), and CRM in the US formally began with a National Transportation Safety Board recommendation by air-safety investigator and aviation psychologist **Alan Diehl** during his investigation of the 1978 United Airlines Flight 173 crash near Portland ([Wikipedia, *Crew resource management*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_resource_management)). ✅ Crucially, "the term 'cockpit resource management' was later amended to 'crew resource management'… The term was coined in 1979 by NASA psychologist **John Lauber**," and "**while retaining a command hierarchy, the concept was intended to foster a less-authoritarian cockpit culture in which co-pilots are encouraged to question captains if they observed them making mistakes**" ([Wikipedia, *Crew resource management*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_resource_management)). United Airlines was the first airline to launch a comprehensive CRM programme, starting in 1981, and by the 1990s CRM was a global standard.

That last sentence is the whole authority-skills lesson in miniature: **retain the hierarchy, remove the fear.** Aviation did not flatten the cockpit; it trained captains to *invite* and *receive* challenge, and co-pilots to *give* it. The named failure mode is the **steep authority gradient** — where the leader is so remote or punitive that the safety-critical information never travels up. CRM's relevance to medicine, surgery and firefighting (§7.2) is precisely that command presence and psychological safety are not opposites: the trained leader holds the command structure and simultaneously lowers the gradient that would silence it.

⚠ **The "authority gradient" coinage and the Cosby–Croskerry citation are flagged.** The term is standard in aviation and medical human-factors writing, and **K. S. Cosby and Pat Croskerry's "Authority gradients and medical error" (2003, *Quality & Safety in Health Care*)** — or "Profiles in patient safety: Authority gradients in medical error" (*Academic Emergency Medicine*, 2003) — is the commonly cited medical application. ⚠ This pass did **not** verify who coined "authority gradient" or the exact Cosby–Croskerry bibliographic record; the phrase is well-attested in the CRM literature, but its first use and the exact article details are flagged in §13 and in "What Could Not Be Verified." The honest statement is: *the concept is verified through CRM; the coinage is not.*

### 7.2 Command Presence in a Crisis

Command presence — the capacity to be believed and followed when things are on fire — is what the CRM literature calls the leadership and assertiveness competencies, and it decomposes into behaviours rather than mystique. ✅ The U.S. Navy's seven critical CRM skills include decision-making, **assertiveness** ("willingness to participate and state a given position until convinced by facts that another option is more correct"), mission analysis, communication, leadership and adaptability ([Wikipedia, *Crew resource management*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_resource_management)). The practical behaviours that build command presence are: **say the situation plainly**, **name the decision and the decider**, **state the next action and timing**, **invite dissent explicitly** ("what am I missing?"), and **be visibly calm and consistent** — because the team reads the leader's affect as data about the real state of things. In a crisis, authority is spent fastest by ambiguity and rebuilt fastest by a clear, checkable plan.

### 7.3 Difficult Conversations

The authority skills involved in a difficult conversation are three: **the courage to initiate it**, **the skill to conduct it without destroying the relationship**, and **the fairness to be changed by it**. The practitioner literature here is large and should be named with a ⚠ practitioner tag:

- ***Difficult Conversations: How to Discuss What Matters Most*** (Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen, from the **Harvard Negotiation Project**; first edition 1999) — ⚠ practitioner. Its core move is to separate the three conversations that run at once — the "What Happened?" conversation (facts, intent, blame), the Feelings conversation, and the Identity conversation (what this says about me) — and to move from blame to *joint contribution* and from certainty about intent to curiosity.
- ***Crucial Conversations: Tools for Talking When Stakes Are High*** (Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan and Al Switzler; first edition 2002) — ⚠ practitioner. Its core move is to maintain the "pool of shared meaning," make it safe for the other party to speak, and separate facts from stories.
- ***Radical Candor*** (Kim Scott, 2017) — ⚠ practitioner. Its two-axis model (care personally *and* challenge directly) names the failure modes of ruinous empathy (care without challenge) and obnoxious aggression (challenge without care), and supplies the "guidance, team, results" frame.

All three are flagged ⚠ **practitioner** — enormously influential, taught in leadership programmes, but not academic research findings, and the guide does not present their claims as verified. What *is* verified is the human-factors scaffold underneath: the CRM finding that the fix for a steep authority gradient is trained, structured challenge-giving and challenge-receiving (✅ §7.1). The practitioner books are useful heuristics for *how* to run the conversation; the CRM evidence is the reason the conversation must be run at all.

### 7.4 The Hard-Moments Rehearsal

Authority in hard moments is a **rehearsable behaviour**, and the rehearsals that matter are: (1) **the dissent invitation** — practise saying "I'm going to state my view and then I want the strongest case against it" until it is natural; (2) **the bad-news delivery** — the short, plain, no-hedging opening sentence, delivered early; (3) **the performance conversation** — facts first, impact second, expectation third, and an explicit invitation to be corrected; (4) **the decision announcement under dissent** — "I've heard the case, here is the decision, here is why, here is what would make me revisit it"; and (5) **the apology that repairs** — named wrongdoing, named impact, changed behaviour, no defence (§8.4). §12 gives these a worked place in a 90-day track.

### 7.5 The Post-Incident Authority Check

The hard moment does not end when the meeting does. A short post-incident check converts the moment into authority rather than leaving it to chance. Five questions, answerable privately:

1. **Did I say the situation plainly, or did I manage the room?** Reassurance that turns out to be false is the most expensive comfort a leader can offer.
2. **Did I keep the decision and the decider clear?** Ambiguity under pressure is read as the leader not knowing what they are doing.
3. **Did I invite the strongest dissent, and did I visibly weigh it?** The dissent invitation is worthless if the answer was decided before it was asked (§7.1).
4. **Did I let the person who was right be right?** A leader who cannot be visibly changed by a subordinate's better argument has just taught the team not to bring better arguments.
5. **Did anyone get blamed for a system failure?** Blame under crisis is the fastest way to convert a steep authority gradient (§7.1) into a permanent one.

The check is the difference between a leader who *survives* a crisis and one who *emerges from it with more authority*. The first is grateful it is over; the second has banked a deposit (§8.2) that the next hard decision will draw on.

## 8. The Maintenance and the Repair — Trust, Consistency and Overreach

### 8.1 The Trust Literature

The canonical integrative model of organisational trust is **Roger C. Mayer, James H. Davis and F. David Schoorman, "An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust" (*Academy of Management Review*, 1995)**. ✅ Its distinctive move — verified through the trust literature's own summary — is to separate **the trustworthiness factors that give rise to trust** (a trustee's perceived **ability, benevolence and integrity**) from **trust itself**, and to place risk-taking ("trusting behaviour") downstream of both ([Wikipedia, *Trust (social science)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_(social_science)), citing the Mayer reference). ✅ The same source notes the parallel paradigm distinguishing **cognition-based trust** (rational calculation of capability) from **affect-based trust** (emotional attachment) ([Wikipedia, *Trust (social science)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trust_(social_science))).

The three factors map cleanly onto the leader's authority: **ability** is the competence currency of §5.4, **integrity** is the credibility currency, and **benevolence** is the consideration currency. That mapping is this guide's synthesis (⚠), but it is a useful diagnostic: a leader who is seen as able and honest but cold (low benevolence) will be obeyed and not followed; a leader seen as warm but unable (low ability) will be loved and not followed; a leader seen as able and warm but slippery (low integrity) will be followed until the first audit. ⚠ Any popularisations that reduce trust to a single "trust equation" or a one-line checklist are flagged — the verified model is a three-factor, context-dependent one, not a slogan.

### 8.2 Consistency and the Deposit Model

Trust is built by **consistency**: the same response to the same situation, the promise kept when keeping it is inconvenient, the standard applied to the leader as well as the team. This is why authority behaves like a **stock** (§5.5): each kept promise, each fair call, each time the leader absorbs blame they could have deflected, is a deposit; each broken promise, each rule applied to others and not themselves, each public humiliation, is a withdrawal. The asymmetry matters more than the balance: trust is built in small increments over time and destroyed in single events, and the repair is not symmetric with the breach.

### 8.3 Power and Its Corruptions

Two research lines supply the honest account of the corrupting risk that authority carries.

- **"Does power corrupt?"** ⚠-knowledge: the classic study is **David Kipnis, "Does Power Corrupt?" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1972)**, which found that subjects given power over others evaluated those others more negatively and were more likely to attribute outcomes to their own actions, and less likely to attribute them to the subordinates' effort. The paper is standard in the OB canon, but this pass did not verify it against a primary record; it is flagged ⚠.
- **Approach/inhibition theory.** ✅ "Developed by D. Keltner and colleagues, approach/inhibition theory assumes that having power and using power alters psychological states of individuals," built on the distinction between *approach* (action, self-promotion, seeking rewards, increased energy) and *inhibition* (self-protection, vigilance, loss of motivation), and holds "that power promotes approach tendencies, while a reduction in power promotes inhibition tendencies" ([Wikipedia, *Power (political science)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_(social_and_political))). ✅ The same source lists the documented tendencies of the powerful: they "take action," "are more proactive, more likely to speak up, make the first move," "experience more positive emotions," and — on the negative side — "are prone to take risky, inappropriate, or unethical decisions and often overstep their boundaries"; when individuals gain power their self-evaluation becomes more positive while their evaluations of others become more negative. ⚠ The underlying paper is **Dacher Keltner, Deborah H. Gruenfeld and Cameron Anderson, "Power, Approach, and Inhibition" (*Psychological Review*, 2003)**; the theory and its direction are ✅ via the reference page, but the exact 2003 citation details are ⚠ flagged.

The practical synthesis: **power does not so much corrupt character as change attention.** The powerful become more action-oriented, more optimistic, more self-referential, and (crucially) **less likely to take the perspective of the less powerful.** Authority skills therefore include deliberate counter-measures: soliciting the view from below, keeping a dissent channel open (§7.1), rotating exposure to the front line, and having someone whose job it is to tell you what you do not want to hear.

### 8.4 Overreach and the Repair Moves

Overreach is the failure to see that the authority you hold has a boundary. Its forms are familiar: spending position power on something that required persuasion; treating dissent as disloyalty; using a crisis to make permanent structural grabs; making a decision reserved for a colleague; insisting on being right after being shown wrong. The repair moves, in order, are: (1) **name it** — "I overstepped, and here is specifically what I did"; (2) **name the impact** — what it cost the person and the team; (3) **restore the right** — return the decision to its owner, or its credit to its author; (4) **change the behaviour visibly** — the next occasion is the proof, not the apology; and (5) **accept the slower clock** — like trust, authority repairs slowly, and a second breach during repair is close to terminal. A leader who repairs well often ends with *more* authority than before, because the team learns that the leader can be wrong and can own it — which makes the leader's other claims more, not less, believable.

### 8.5 The Trust Ledger — A Maintenance Practice

The deposit-and-withdrawal model (§8.2) is useful only if it is turned into a habit. A simple ledger practice, run monthly, keeps the account visible:

| Ledger line | Question | Typical deposits | Typical withdrawals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Promises** | Did I do what I said, on time, without being chased? | The small kept promise; the deadline met | The quietly dropped commitment; the "I'll get back to you" that never came |
| **Fairness** | Did the same standard apply to me as to the team? | Owning a mistake in public; applying the rule to yourself | The exception for the leader; the rule for others |
| **Protection** | Did I absorb the pressure I could have passed down? | Taking the difficult escalation; shielding the team from a needless demand | Passing blame down; letting a colleague take a hit that was yours |
| **Voice** | Did dissent get heard and weighed? | Acting on a subordinate's better argument | Repeatedly asking for input and then not using it |
| **Presence** | Was I there when it was hard, not just when it was fun? | The incident call at 2 a.m.; the difficult conversation held | The absence under pressure; the credit taken after the fact |

The ledger is not a scoreboard for anxiety; it is a maintenance schedule. The point is the **asymmetry** — a single withdrawal in the Protection or Fairness row can outweigh months of deposits — and the fact that trust is *path-dependent*: the deposits that matter most are the ones made when it would have been easier not to. A leader who reviews the ledger monthly catches the drift before it becomes a credibility crisis rather than a credibility repair.

## 9. The Personal and the Positional — Authority Beyond the Chair

### 9.1 The Distinction

**Positional authority** is the authority of the office: the role, the title, the delegation matrix, the power to approve. **Personal authority** is the authority of the person: the regard, credibility and trust that travel with the individual and persist when the office is vacated. The distinction is one of the most useful practical tools in the genre, and it is also — honestly — one of the least rigorously sourced. ⚠ **The phrasing "positional versus personal authority" is largely practitioner-origin and its coinage is contested**; it is used across the leadership-training literature with different labels ("position power" vs "personal power," "formal" vs "informal" authority), and no single scholarly source owns it. This guide flags that provenance rather than dressing it up.

### 9.2 The Honest Lineage

The distinction is nonetheless **a re-labelling of verified older concepts**, and it should be traced honestly:

- **Weber's charismatic authority (§3.4)** is personal authority in its purest form — legitimacy from the person, not the office, and unstable precisely for that reason.
- **French and Raven's separation of legitimate power (position) from referent and expert power (person)** (§2.6) is the same distinction in taxonomy form: ✅ the bases are verified, and referent/expert are the personal bases.
- **Barnard's acceptance theory (§1.3)** explains *why* the personal base is the durable one: ✅ because "what makes a communication authoritative rests with the subordinate," an office cannot supply acceptance — only the person's perceived competence, credibility and consideration can ([Wikipedia, *Chester Barnard*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chester_Barnard)).

So the honest statement is: the *label* is practitioner, the *content* is centuries old. A leader with only positional authority has borrowed their legitimacy from the org chart and must give it back at the next reorganisation; a leader with personal authority can carry it to the next role — and, more importantly, can exercise it without invoking the office at all, which is what makes it the higher and more durable form.

### 9.3 Personal Authority Without Position

The senior individual contributor, the staff engineer, the subject-matter expert and the respected programme lead all run on personal authority, and their practical tool-kit is the **base-substitution** the OB research describes. ✅ [organizational_behavior_guide.md](organizational_behavior_guide.md) §4.4 carries the standard finding that the bases are substitutable — a credible expert can lead without legitimate power (the "staff engineer effect"). The mechanics of influence without authority — exchange, coalition, escalation, reciprocity, the stakeholder map — belong to [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) and [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md); this guide's §10 states only the condensed influence core and points there. The one addition worth naming here is **borrowed authority**: a leader without position can borrow legitimacy by citing the decision-owner's mandate ("the CTO has asked for this"), and the skilled practitioner treats borrowed legitimacy as a loan that must be repaid with results, not a resource to spend freely.

### 9.4 The Conversion Problem

The mature leadership task is to **convert** personal authority into durable structures before it walks out of the door: written standards, delegated rights, trained successors, and a culture that does not depend on a single charismatic figure. ⚠ This is the practitioner reading of Weber's "routinisation of charisma" and of the founder-succession problem; it is sound, but it is an interpretation, flagged as such. Its authority-skills moral: an organisation whose authority rests only in persons is fragile; an organisation whose authority rests only in offices is inert; the skilled leader builds both and makes them reinforce each other.

### 9.5 The Personal-Authority Audit

For a leader who wants to know how much of their authority is personal rather than positional, five questions cut through:

1. **If the title were removed tomorrow, who would still take my call?** The names are the referent base, and their count is the personal-authority balance.
2. **Do people seek my judgement on matters outside my remit?** Being consulted beyond the box is the clearest everyday signal of personal authority.
3. **When I disagree with a peer, do they engage the argument or invoke their rank?** An equal who argues with you, rather than around you, is treating your authority as real.
4. **After a decision goes against someone, do they still bring me the next problem?** Losers who keep coming back are the surest sign that authority is personal, not merely held.
5. **What have I converted into structure?** If the answer is "nothing," the personal authority is a single point of failure for the organisation and a career risk for the leader (§9.4).

The audit is uncomfortable by design. Most leaders discover they are stronger on the positional questions (mandate, budget, headcount) than on the personal ones — which is precisely why the genre exists: **the positional answers are given to you, and the personal ones have to be earned.**

## 10. The Influence Skills That Complement Formal Authority

### 10.1 Why This Section Is Condensed

Formal authority is a *lever*, not a complete tool-kit. Most of what a senior professional needs to get done happens outside their decision rights — across functions, upwards, sideways, in matrix and network settings (§11.1). The influence canon for that work is covered in depth elsewhere in the repository ([managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md); [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md)), and this section is deliberately **condensed**: it states the verified core, flags the contested parts, and points rather than re-derives.

### 10.2 Cialdini Condensed

Robert B. Cialdini's ***Influence*** is the most-cited popular work on compliance and persuasion. ✅ The book was originally published as ***Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (1984)** and republished as ***Influence: Science and Practice* (1985)**; it examines the ways people are influenced by "compliance professionals," and "a seventh lever on 'unity' has been added to the most recent edition" ([Wikipedia, *Influence: Science and Practice*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence:_The_Psychology_of_Persuasion)). ✅ The seven principles are **reciprocation, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity and unity** ([Wikipedia, *Influence: Science and Practice*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influence:_The_Psychology_of_Persuasion)). ⚠ The commonly used subtitle for the 2021 revision — ***Influence, New and Expanded: The Psychology of Persuasion*** — and the exact framing of the unity addition are flagged: the seventh principle is verified; the exact 2021 edition title and release details were not verified against a publisher page this pass.

Read against this guide, the Cialdini principles are **influence tactics that substitute for or amplify authority**: *authority* leans on the legitimate/expert base; *liking* on the referent base; *reciprocation* on the reciprocity legitimacy of §2.3; *commitment and consistency* on the trust deposit model of §8.2; *social proof* on the norm-following that Barnard's acceptance theory presumes; *scarcity* on the economics of choice; and *unity* on identity — the "we" that makes the request an in-group obligation. Used honestly, they are the everyday levers of a leader without enough formal power; used manipulatively, they cross the line from persuasion to manipulation drawn in §4.6, and the discovery costs the leader the credibility currency.

### 10.3 The Replication Debates — Flagged

⚠ **The influence principles are not uniformly replication-proof, and the guide flags this rather than smoothing it.** Several social-psychology findings on which the popular principles rest have been through replication scrutiny, and some — especially in the social-priming and ego-depletion regions — have contested or weakened evidence bases. The practical statement is: **the phenomena are real in field settings and weakly-to-moderately supported in controlled ones**, and the tag ⚠ applies to any claim that a specific principle produces a specific effect size on demand. Treat the principles as **well-attested heuristics**, not as deterministic levers.

### 10.4 The Milgram Base of the Authority Principle

The "authority" principle traces to **Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments at Yale**. ✅ Beginning in August 1961, Milgram ran a series of experiments in which participants were instructed by an authority figure to administer what they believed were increasingly painful electric shocks to a "learner"; "in the first version of the study… every participant went up to 300 volts, and **65% went up to the full 450 volts**." Milgram first described the research in a 1963 article in the *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* and later in the 1974 book *Obedience to Authority: An Experimental View* ([Wikipedia, *Milgram experiment*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milgram_experiment)). The experiment is the canonical demonstration of "the power of authority and situation over disposition" — and the repository's [organizational_behavior_guide.md](organizational_behavior_guide.md) carries the OB reading (subjects were not sadists; the situation made refusal structurally hard).

⚠ **The 65% figure is contested.** The commonly cited "65% of participants went to 450 volts" rests on Milgram's own reporting of a subset of his conditions; the archival re-analysis by **Perry (2012) and Perry, Gibson and colleagues (2013)** of Milgram's original papers is widely cited as showing that the obedience rate depends heavily on which of the many conditions is counted, and that the headline figure is not a stable universal constant. ⚠ This pass did **not** verify the Perry/Gibson re-analysis against a primary record; it is flagged ⚠ and listed in "What Could Not Be Verified." The practical consequence is important for authority skills: **the "people blindly obey authority" story is overstated**; obedience was highly sensitive to *proximity* (how close the authority was), *the presence of dissenting peers*, and *the framing* — which is a more useful finding, because those are exactly the variables a leader can influence.

### 10.5 Cohen and Bradford's Exchange Model

The complement to Cialdini's psychology is **Allan R. Cohen and David L. Bradford's *Influence Without Authority***, whose central model is **exchange**: influence without formal authority works like a barter economy — you identify what the other party values, you offer something genuine in return (resources, information, support, visibility, a future favour), and you build a durable relationship of reciprocal credibility. ⚠-knowledge: *Influence Without Authority* (first edition 1990; later editions) and its exchange model are standard practitioner references, and the "currencies of exchange" taxonomy is widely taught, but this pass did not verify the book against a primary record; it is flagged ⚠. It is the natural bridge between this guide and [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md), which carries the applied "influencing without authority" toolkit and the stakeholder-mapping discipline.

### 10.6 The Complement, Stated Plainly

Formal authority and the influence skills are complements, not substitutes. Authority decides **when you may decide alone**; influence decides **what you can get done when you may not**. A leader with authority but no influence skills is eventually resented and worked around; a leader with influence skills but no authority is effective but shadowy, and eventually hits the ceiling of a decision they cannot make. The mature profile is a leader who **uses authority sparingly and influence constantly**, and who knows which of the two a given situation actually requires.

### 10.7 The Influence-to-Authority Mapping

The practical link between the two literatures is a mapping table. Each influence principle is a tactic that draws on a specific authority base, and each has a boundary past which it becomes manipulative (§4.6).

| Principle | Authority base it draws on | Where it earns its keep | The manipulation boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Authority** | Legitimate + expert | Invoking a mandate, a credential, a validated standard | Citing authority you do not have, or expertise you have not got |
| **Liking** | Referent | Building the rapport that makes hard messages land | Building a false rapport to extract compliance |
| **Reciprocation** | Reciprocity legitimacy (§2.3) | The fair exchange that makes a request reasonable | Manufacturing an obligation the other did not choose |
| **Commitment/consistency** | Informational + referent | Aligning a request with the person's own stated goals | Trapping someone in a commitment they no longer hold |
| **Social proof** | Informational + normative | Showing what respected peers have already done | Fabricated or cherry-picked consensus |
| **Scarcity** | Reward (indirect) | The genuine deadline and the genuine limit | Manufactured urgency and false scarcity |
| **Unity** | Referent (identity) | The "we" of a shared team, profession or cause | Claiming kinship that does not exist to lower defences |

Read with §10.2's warning, the table's point is that the same tactic can be **statecraft or manipulation** depending on whether the thing it invokes is real. Liking built on genuine regard is referent power; liking performed to extract a favour is manipulation. The test is not the tactic but the truth of its premise — which is why the influence skills and the integrity currency (§5.4) are inseparable.

## 11. The Modern Context

### 11.1 Flattened, Networked and Matrix Organisations

The twentieth-century hierarchy is being overlaid — not replaced — by flatter, networked and matrix structures, and each overlay changes where authority lives.

- **Mechanistic versus organic.** ⚠-knowledge: **Tom Burns and G. M. Stalker's *The Management of Innovation* (1961)** distinguished the **mechanistic** form (high specialisation, vertical control, rules and hierarchy — suited to stable environments) from the **organic** form (lateral communication, fluid roles, expertise over position — suited to changing environments). The distinction is canonical, but this pass did not verify the book against a primary record; it is flagged ⚠.
- **Matrix management.** ✅ Matrix management is "an organizational structure in which some individuals report to more than one supervisor or leader," was "developed in U.S. aerospace in the 1950s," and "achieved wider adoption in the 1970s"; types include strong, weak and balanced matrices ([Wikipedia, *Matrix management*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_management)). ⚠ The classic treatment **Davis and Lawrence, *Matrix* (1977)**, and the influential **Bartlett and Ghoshal HBR article "Matrix Management: Not a Structure, a Frame of Mind" (1990)**, are the standard references but were not verified against primary records this pass. ⚠ Jay Galbraith's information-processing/star-model work on matrix design is likewise ⚠-knowledge.
- **Networks.** ⚠-knowledge: **Nohria and Eccles (eds.), *Networks and Organizations* (1992)**, is the standard scholarly statement that organisations are as much networks of relationships as they are hierarchies of authority; flagged ⚠.
- **The counter-literature that hierarchy persists.** Authority did not dissolve; it was redistributed and made more contestable. ✅ **Elliott Jaques** (1917–2003), a Canadian psychoanalyst and management consultant, originated **requisite organization** as "a total system of managerial organization," along with time-span of discretion, fair pay, corporate culture and the "midlife crisis" ([Wikipedia, *Elliott Jaques*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliott_Jaques)). Requisite organisation is the strongest statement that hierarchy is *necessary* — that there is a right number of layers and a right span, derivable from the time-span of the work, and that flattening beyond the requisite level does not remove authority, it merely makes it informal and therefore less accountable. ⚠ Thomas Leavitt's *Top Down: Why Hierarchies Are Here to Stay* (2005) makes the empirical case that hierarchy is resilient; flagged ⚠-knowledge.
- **The practitioner futurism.** ⚠ **Gary Hamel and Michele Zanini's *Humanocracy*** and **Frédéric Laloux's *Reinventing Organizations* (2014)** are flagged as practitioner/futurist rather than verified research. ✅ Laloux's book describes five stages of organisational structure (Red, Amber, Orange, Green, Teal) drawn from screening over fifty organisations, proposing "Teal" built on self-management, wholeness and evolutionary purpose ([Wikipedia, *Reinventing Organizations*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinventing_Organizations)). These are vision statements, not controlled findings; they usefully describe what authority might become, and should be read with the flag attached.

**The authority-skills consequence:** in a matrix or network, authority is **negotiated and borrowed** rather than merely held, and the leader's task shifts from exercising rights to **clarifying them**. A matrix without explicit decision rights (§6) turns every cross-functional decision into an informal power contest, and the leader who can name the decider, the consulted and the informed — out loud, early — accumulates authority that the structure itself fails to provide.

### 11.2 The Expert Organisation and Professional Authority

The professional organisation is a distinct authority regime. ✅-adjacent: **Henry Mintzberg's** five structural configurations — including the **professional bureaucracy** (his 1983 *Structure in Fives*) — hold that in hospitals, universities, law and accounting firms and (partly) banks, the **operating core holds the authority**, exercised through trained expertise and professional standards, while managers coordinate and buffer rather than command. ⚠ The professional-bureaucracy concept is standard, but this pass verified Mintzberg as a scholar ([Wikipedia, *Henry Mintzberg*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Mintzberg)) and not the 1983 book's contents; it is flagged ⚠-knowledge.

The characteristic friction is the **professional–managerial tension**: the professional's authority derives from expertise and external professional norms; the manager's derives from the office and the budget. ⚠-knowledge: **Amitai Etzioni's** work on the **semi-professions** (e.g. nursing, teaching, social work) — ✅ Etzioni (1929–2023) was an Israeli-American sociologist known for socioeconomics and communitarianism, and a prolific organisational theorist ([Wikipedia, *Amitai Etzioni*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amitai_Etzioni)) — holds that occupations whose authority is shorter on training and weaker on professional mandate are more vulnerable to managerial encroachment. The authority-skills reading: **in an expert organisation, position alone buys almost nothing.** A leader of highly trained professionals earns authority by respecting and extending the professionals' autonomy, by being the one who protects their time and standards, and by being demonstrably competent in a domain the professionals respect — the rational-legal chair is a meeting invitation, not a mandate.

### 11.3 Authority and Culture

Culture changes the meaning of authority, and the guide states the findings with the generalisations flagged.

- **Hofstede's power distance.** ✅ Geert Hofstede developed a framework of national cultural dimensions from "a worldwide survey of employee values by IBM between 1967 and 1973," including **power distance** — "the strength of social hierarchy" — measured by the **Power Distance Index**; the framework was later refined with a fifth dimension, long-term orientation, and a sixth, indulgence ([Wikipedia, *Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofstede%27s_cultural_dimensions_theory)). ✅ **Power distance** itself is "the extent to which power is unequally distributed between parties, and the level of acceptance of that unequal distribution, whether it is in the family, workplace, or other organizations," introduced by Hofstede in the 1970s ([Wikipedia, *Power distance*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_distance)). ⚠ **Singapore's position**: Singapore is widely cited as a **high-PDI** society (frequently reported around the 70s on the index), and the low-power-distance-democracy correlation is a standard (though popularised) reading — the specific Singapore score is flagged as ⚠ approximate and index-edition-dependent, and the guide does not assert a precise figure it did not verify.
- **The GLOBE study.** ✅ **House, Hanges, Javidan, Dorfman and Gupta (eds.), *Culture, Leadership, and Organizations: The GLOBE Study of 62 Societies* (Sage, 2004)** — the GLOBE project's cross-cultural study of leadership across 62 societies, with cultural clusters and culturally endorsed implicit leadership theories ([Wikipedia, *Global leadership*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_leadership), citing House et al. 2004). GLOBE extends and partly challenges Hofstede's dimensions and adds leadership-specific expectations.
- **Paternalistic leadership in Chinese/Asian organisations.** ⚠-knowledge: **Jling-Lih Farh and Bor-Shiuan Cheng's "A Cultural Analysis of Paternalistic Leadership in Chinese Organizations" (2000)** is the standard reference for paternalistic leadership — a style combining strong discipline and authority with fatherly benevolence and moral leadership, treated as indigenous to Chinese organisational contexts. The concept is well-attested; the 2000 chapter was not verified against a primary record this pass, so it is flagged ⚠.

⚠ **Two flags that must not be dropped.** First, **all of these are generalisations with large within-culture variance**: national-dimension scores describe distributions, not individuals, and using them as stereotypes about a colleague is both statistically illiterate and professionally corrosive. Second, ⚠ **the "Asian values" debate is itself contested**: the term was "codified and promoted in the Bangkok Declaration of 1993," associated with leaders including Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew, and invoked to argue that Asian societies prioritise "social harmony," "loyalty and respect towards figures of authority," and "collectivism" over Western individual rights ([Wikipedia, *Asian values*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_values)). ⚠ **Amartya Sen's critique** — that "Asian values" is a selective and politically convenient construction that ignores Asia's own traditions of dissent and reasoning, and that it is used to justify authoritarianism — is the standard counter, flagged ⚠-knowledge here. The authority-skills reading is therefore **contextual, not culturalist**: respect expectations about deference and hierarchy where they are real and helpful, but treat the "we are hierarchically deferential" story as a claim to be tested with the individual in front of you, not a licence to flatten people into a dimension score.

### 11.4 The Failure Modes

Authority fails in characteristic ways, each with a literature.

| Failure mode | What it looks like | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| **Abdication / laissez-faire** | The leader withdraws from decisions, leaves the team unguided, calls it empowerment | ⚠-knowledge: **Lewin, Lippitt and White (1939)** — the classic leadership-climate studies finding laissez-faire leadership the least effective and least satisfying. Standard, not re-verified. |
| **Abusive / destructive supervision** | Sustained hostile verbal and non-verbal behaviour: ridicule, silent treatment, blame, temper | ✅ The construct is defined as "subordinates' perceptions of the extent to which their supervisors engage in the sustained display of hostile verbal and nonverbal behaviors" ([Wikipedia, *Abusive supervision*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abusive_supervision), citing Tepper). ⚠ **Bennett Tepper's 2000 *Academy of Management Journal* paper** is the standard source; ⚠-knowledge: **Einarsen, Aasland and Skogstad (2007)** on destructive leadership; not re-verified. |
| **Micromanagement** | Excessive monitoring and detail-control; delegation without decision rights | ⚠ Mostly practitioner. ✅ The definition (excessive control/attention to detail, "lack of freedom and trust") is standard ([Wikipedia, *Micromanagement*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micromanagement)); ⚠-knowledge: the practitioner literature (e.g. Robert Sutton's writing on bad bosses) is the main treatment. |
| **The Peter Principle** | Promotion to the level of incompetence, so authority outruns competence | ✅ **Laurence J. Peter and Raymond Hull's *The Peter Principle* (1969, William Morrow)** — "in a hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to his level of incompetence"; the book was intended as **satire** though it made a serious organisational point ([Wikipedia, *Peter principle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_principle)). Flagged ⚠ as satire/practitioner rather than empirical finding. |
| **The steep authority gradient** | The leader's distance silences the bad news (§7.1) | ✅ via CRM (see §7.1) |

The **structural** failure mode underneath all of them is the one this guide keeps returning to: **authority without matching competence produces the Peter problem; authority without matching consideration produces the abuse problem; authority withheld (abdication) produces the vacuum that politics fills.** Authority skills are the discipline of holding the level right — neither absent nor overbearing — and of keeping the three currencies (§5.4) funded.

### 11.5 The Hybrid Organisation in Practice

Most real organisations are now hybrids: a rational-legal hierarchy overlaid with product lines, a matrix overlaid with communities of practice, a network overlaid with a compliance regime. The authority skills that work in a hybrid are the ones this guide has been building, applied in combination:

- **Map the rights, do not assume them.** In a hybrid, the official map and the working map differ; the leader who asks "who actually decides this?" before asserting saves a turf war (§6).
- **Build legitimacy horizontally.** Because lateral relationships carry half the work, reciprocity legitimacy (§2.3) — the record of having helped before asking — is the currency that buys lateral action.
- **Use the hierarchy deliberately, not defensively.** Escalation is a legitimate tool when a boundary is genuinely contested (§12.4); it becomes a withdrawal of trust only when it is used as a substitute for the conversation.
- **Keep the personal network warm.** In a network organisation, referent and informational power flow along relationships that no org chart draws; a leader who only talks to their reporting line is structurally weak.
- **Write things down.** The more matrixed the organisation, the more authority depends on the shared written artefact — the rights map, the decision record, the standard — because there is no single chain of command to remember it for you.

The hybrid does not abolish authority; it **spreads and privatises** it. The leader who can navigate that spread — naming rights, banking reciprocity, escalating honestly and writing it all down — holds more effective authority than the one who only ever commands their own box.

## 12. The Cymbal Bank Worked Example — An Authority-Skills Development Track (ILLUSTRATIVE)

### 12.1 The Framing and the Scenario

**This worked example is ILLUSTRATIVE.** It is a constructed scenario for a fictional Cymbal Bank technology leader, built to show how the verified concepts of this guide become a concrete plan. It is not a case study, not a claim about any real bank, and not a report of an outcome; the figures, names and dates are invented for teaching, and only the **frameworks** they instantiate are sourced. It is the **authority-skills track** of a first-90-days plan and deliberately cross-references, rather than duplicates, the STARS diagnosis, early-wins machinery and alliance-building of [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) (which carries the full Cymbal Bank Head-of-Technology worked example) and the stakeholder-mapping and influence-without-authority toolkit of [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md).

**Scenario (illustrative):** *Priya Nadar* joins Cymbal Bank (Singapore) as **Head of Platform Engineering**, a new-ish role (the previous incumbent left after eight months) leading 34 engineers across three teams — Payments, Data and Core Banking — that previously reported to two different directors. She owns the platform roadmap and the budgets of three teams, but the **architecture decisions are formally reserved to a cross-functional Architecture Review Board**, the **production change window is controlled by the Change Advisory Board**, and the **Neptune core-banking migration** is jointly run with a business-side programme director who has been in the bank eleven years and treats the platform as a delivery resource. Her mandate is real but thin: she has the box on the org chart, no personal authority with this organisation, and a team that has watched two leaders in two years and defaults to scepticism.

The authority-skills problem for Priya is precisely this guide's problem: **she has legitimate power (§2) and almost nothing else.** The plan below is her 90-day authority-building track.

### 12.2 The 90-Day Track — Fortnight by Fortnight

| Window | Authority-skill focus | Concrete moves | Guide anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Days 1–14** | Diagnose, do not assert (§5.5 step 1) | 25 one-to-one listening sessions across the three teams and the adjacent functions; a written "what I heard" memo circulated back; no reorganisation, no public commitments; begin the stake map (below) | §5.5; [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md); [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) |
| **Days 15–28** | Name the decision rights (§6) | Draft a one-page decision-rights map for the three teams (what Priya decides, what the team decides, what the ARB decides, what the programme decides); circulate for correction before adoption; negotiate the two contested edges (architecture sign-off, change-window priority) with the board chairs | §6.1–6.2; RACI/DACI |
| **Days 29–45** | Land one visible, uncontested win (§5.5 step 2) | Pick the payments incident-recurrence problem: it is painful, measurable, inside her authority, and needs no one's permission — halve the repeat incidents in one release cycle; publish the numbers | §5.5; [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) early-wins |
| **Days 46–60** | Build the consideration currency (§5.4) | Protect the migration team's on-call load by reallocating two engineers; publicly credit the engineer who found the root cause at the monthly town hall; hold the line on a scope demand that would have forced weekend work | §5.4; §8.1 benevolence |
| **Days 61–75** | Run the hard conversation (§7.3–7.4) | Prepare and hold the conversation with the business programme director about the platform being a delivery *partner*, not a resource — facts first, impact second, expectation third, with an explicit invitation to be corrected; rehearse the dissent invitation in the migration steering meeting | §7.3–7.4 |
| **Days 76–90** | Convert personal authority into structure (§9.4) | Publish the decision-rights map as the operating standard; stand up a fortnightly architecture forum that the ARB consumes rather than replaces; agree a named successor for each of the three team leads' critical responsibilities; write the "how we decide here" one-pager | §6; §9.4 |

### 12.3 The Stakeholder and Authority Map (Illustrative)

The map is not a list of org-chart boxes; it is a list of **who holds which authority and which currency**, so the leader knows where she is borrowing and where she is depositing.

| Stakeholder | Authority held | Currency to align | Priya's approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| The two former supervising directors | Legitimate (residual), referent with the teams | Their endorsement of the new structure | Respect their history publicly; ask them to co-present the new decision-rights map — turning potential resistance into sponsorship |
| The Architecture Review Board chair | Legitimate (architecture sign-off) | Expert + informational | Bring evidence, not opinion; offer a per-domain fast-path so the ARB keeps the exceptions and she keeps the routine — a reciprocity trade (§2.3) |
| The Change Advisory Board | Legitimate (production windows) | Informational + process | Demonstrate a lower-incident track record (§12.2 win), then ask for a risk-based fast path for low-risk platform changes |
| The business programme director | Legitimate (programme mandate), referent (11 years' standing) | Reciprocity + credibility | The difficult-conversation target (§7.4); supply reliable delivery first, then negotiate the partnership framing |
| The Payments team lead | Expert + referent (team trust) | Recognition and real delegation | Give real decision rights (not just tasks); make her the visible owner of the incident win |
| The Data team (sceptical, watched two leaders leave) | Informational (they know the systems) | Consistency over time | Keep every small promise; publish the decision-rights map and actually follow it |

### 12.4 The Early Win, the Escalation Protocol, and the Repair

**The early win (§12.2).** The payments incident-recurrence problem is chosen by the §5.5 test: *visible, uncontested, measurable, and inside Priya's own decision rights.* It establishes competence (and a little expert power) without requiring anyone's permission — which is exactly why it is the right first move for a leader with thin authority. The published numbers are the deposit; the way she credits the team is the consideration deposit.

**The escalation protocol (illustrative).** Priya writes, and negotiates, an explicit escalation protocol so that escalation is a *process*, not a *relationship failure*: (1) try first within the team, with the decision rights as written; (2) if the decision crosses a right boundary, raise it with the right-holder directly, with the evidence and the options; (3) escalate to the ARB or the programme steering only when the boundary is genuinely contested or the risk is bank-level; (4) every escalation records the decision and the decider. She then uses it in the days 15–28 negotiation as the *thing she is asking them to agree to* — turning a potential turf fight into a shared process improvement (§6), which is how a leader without personal authority converts a rule into a relationship.

**The repair moves (§8.4).** Priya makes at least one overreach in the first 90 days, and the guide's model says to plan for it, not deny it. Illustrative: in the pressures of month two she tells a business-side prioritisation meeting that the platform "will" absorb a scope increase without checking with her own leads first — a positional grab that costs her the team's credibility. The repair is the §8.4 sequence: name it to the team ("I committed your capacity without asking you — that was wrong"), name the impact (they had planned their quarter around it), restore the right (the scope decision goes back to the team leads, and she takes the renegotiation with the programme), change the behaviour (a standing rule that she never commits team capacity without the leads in the room), and accept the slower clock (the next two months are the proof).

### 12.5 The Lessons

Six takeaways, each tracing to a section of this guide: (1) **position alone buys a seat, not a mandate** — Priya's legitimacy was rational-legal (§3.3) and thin, so the plan front-loads earning (§5); (2) **name the decision rights early and in writing** — the matrix and the ARB meant the *real* authority was negotiated, not held (§6, §11.1); (3) **the first win must be inside your own authority** — requiring no one's permission is what makes it a deposit rather than a debt (§5.5); (4) **the consideration currency is the one leaders skip** — the scope protection and the public credit were the cheapest, highest-yield deposits she made (§5.4, §8.1); (5) **the hard conversation is rehearsed, not improvised** — facts, impact, expectation, invitation to be corrected (§7.4); and (6) **the end state is structure, not personality** — decision-rights map, architecture forum, named successors, and a written "how we decide here," so that the authority outlives the leader (§9.4). The same shape applies to any authority-building mandate in the bank — a new Head of Data, a programme director inheriting a troubled migration, a staff engineer made a team lead — because the skills, not the scenario, are what transfer.

### 12.6 The Anti-Example — The Same Mandate, Unearned

The mirror image shows what Priya's first 90 days look like when the authority-skills disciplines are skipped — a cautionary walk through §5.6 and §11.4 in one mandate. **Diagnosis:** she skips the listening and opens with a reorganisation in week two, so every later idea is read as self-interested. **Rights:** she never negotiates the decision-rights map; the ARB and the programme keep deciding informally, and she learns the boundaries only by losing the arguments — each loss read by her team as a loss of standing. **Early win:** she picks the Neptune migration as her first win — the highest-visibility, highest-dependency problem on the estate — and when it slips, her competence is in question rather than proven. **Consideration:** she passes the migration's weekend load onto her teams to protect a relationship upstairs, and the teams conclude, accurately, that she will spend them to look good. **Hard conversation:** she avoids the conversation with the programme director and escalates instead, teaching the organisation that she cannot handle a peer; the escalation is won and the credibility is lost. **Repair:** after a public overstatement of capacity, she defends the statement rather than repairing it, and the team stops bringing her the information she now needs. **End state:** ninety days in, she holds the same rational-legal box she was given on day one and nothing more — a position without authority, in an organisation that has already watched two previous holders leave.

The two columns are the same mandate. The difference is entirely the skill set this guide has described: diagnose before asserting, name the rights, win where you have authority, protect the people, rehearse the hard conversation, repair the overreach honestly, and convert the personal into the structural. Nothing in the anti-example required bad intent — only the absence of the disciplines, which is precisely why the disciplines are worth practising.

### The Glossary

- **Abdication (laissez-faire leadership)** — withdrawal from decisions and guidance, often mislabelled empowerment; a failure mode in which authority is not exercised at all (§11.4).
- **Acceptance theory of authority** — Barnard's claim that a communication is authoritative only when the subordinate accepts it as within the scope of what they owe the organisation (§1.3).
- **Accountability** — the state of being answerable for an outcome; for a structure to be legitimate it must be paired with the authority to act (§6.3).
- **Approach/inhibition theory** — Keltner and colleagues' account of how holding power shifts psychological state toward action and self-promotion, and reduced power toward inhibition and vigilance (§8.3).
- **Authority** — legitimate power; power accepted as rightful by those subject to it (Weber's *Herrschaft*) (§1.1–1.2).
- **Authority gradient** — the perceived difference in power or expertise between a leader and those below, and the extent to which it silences challenge (§7.1).
- **Bases of social power** — French and Raven's taxonomy: reward, coercive, legitimate, referent and expert (1959), plus informational (1965) (§2.1–2.2).
- **Charismatic authority** — legitimacy grounded in the person's personal gift or following, rather than office or custom; the purest personal authority and the least stable (§3.4).
- **Coercive power** — power based on the ability to punish; the most fragile and most resented base (§2.6).
- **Credibility** — the currency of truth-telling, promise-keeping and consistency; the trust model's integrity factor (§5.4, §8.1).
- **Decision rights** — the entitlements to decide particular matters; the operational form of authority in an organisation (§6.1).
- **DACI** — Driver, Approvers, Contributors, Informed; a decision-rights framework listed as an alternative to RACI (§6.2).
- **Delegation** — the transfer of authority along with work; real delegation transfers decisions, not just tasks (§6.3).
- **Expert power** — power grounded in knowledge and skill; the personal base that substitutes for position (§2.6).
- **Herrschaft** — Weber's German term for authority/domination, literally "mastership"; the term whose translation is contested (§1.2, §3.5).
- **Informational power** — Raven's 1965 addition: power in the content of the message itself, independent of the agent's office or expertise (§2.2).
- **Influence** — the actual production of change in another's belief, attitude or behaviour; realised power (§2.1, §4.6).
- **Legitimate power** — power grounded in a recognised right to influence, whether from position or from reciprocity, equity and responsibility norms (§2.3, §2.6).
- **Machiavellianism** — the personality dimension of cynical manipulation; referenced from the OB corpus and noted in the abusive-supervision literature (§11.4).
- **Macht** — Weber's German term for power, distinguished from *Herrschaft* (§4.2).
- **Matrix organisation** — a structure in which people report to more than one leader (solid and dotted lines), developed in US aerospace in the 1950s (§11.1).
- **Micromanagement** — excessive monitoring and detail-control; delegation of tasks without delegation of decisions; mostly a practitioner construct (§11.4).
- **Overreach** — exercising authority beyond its legitimate boundary, e.g. a positional grab or treating dissent as disloyalty (§8.4).
- **Personal authority** — authority carried by the person; the referent and expert bases; persists across roles (§9.1).
- **Peter Principle** — Peter and Hull's (1969) satirical observation that people rise to their level of incompetence, so authority can outrun competence (§11.4).
- **Positional authority** — authority derived from office, role and the formal delegation matrix (the legitimate base) (§9.1).
- **Power** — the capacity to influence another's behaviour, with or without resistance; the wider category of which authority is the legitimate slice (§1.4, §4.6).
- **Power distance** — Hofstede's dimension describing the inequality of power and the acceptance of that inequality (PDI) (§11.3).
- **Referent power** — power grounded in identification with, liking of, or respect for the person; the strongest relational base (§2.6).
- **Rational-legal authority** — legitimacy grounded in rules and offices; the modern organisational form, impersonal and transferable but weak in personal attachment (§3.3).
- **Requisite organisation** — Jaques's system holding that there is a necessary number of hierarchical layers and spans derived from the time-span of the work (§11.1).
- **Reward power** — power based on the ability to give valued outcomes; works while the reward is contingent and credible (§2.6).
- **RACI** — Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed; the responsibility assignment matrix (§6.2).
- **RAPID** — Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide; a decision-rights framework associated with Bain & Company (§6.2).
- **Social influence** — French and Raven's definition: a change in belief, attitude or behaviour caused by another person (§2.1).
- **Traditional authority** — legitimacy grounded in the sanctity of custom, inheritance and seniority (§3.2).
- **Trust** — willingness to be vulnerable to another based on positive expectations; in the integrative model, downstream of perceived ability, benevolence and integrity (§8.1).
- **Zone of indifference / zone of acceptance** — the range of orders a person accepts without conscious questioning; Barnard's (and Simon's) concept (§1.3).

### The Closing Summary

Authority is legitimacy made operational. Weber gave us the three types and the one word that carries the whole idea — accepted; Barnard turned the concept toward the follower, where it actually lives; Simon placed it inside the bounded rationality of real organisations; French and Raven gave us the map of where the legitimacy can come from; and a half-century of trust, power and human-factors research gave us the failure modes and the repair moves. Read together, the literature says something unfashionably simple: **position is a starting balance, not a standing balance.** It can be spent down by inconsistency, by overreach, by a competence that stops keeping up with the title; and it can be built up by the everyday disciplines this guide has named — the kept promise, the protected colleague, the decision named clearly and owned honestly, the bad news delivered early, the dissent invited and weighed, the mistake named before someone else has to.

The skills are learnable, and that is the point. The leader who never receives a mandate does not get to skip the work — the senior individual contributor runs the same programme on referent, expert and informational power instead of legitimate power. The leader who does receive a mandate gets a shorter runway and a longer memory: everyone will watch whether the authority was earned or merely granted, and the two are told apart by what happens the first time it is inconvenient to act with integrity. Held well, authority compounds and outlives the holder; held badly, it depreciates in public; withheld entirely, it becomes the vacuum that politics fills. There is nothing mystical in the middle path — only the unglamorous, repeatable practice of being competent, credible and considerate while deciding clearly and repairing honestly.

*End of guide — the authority earned.*
