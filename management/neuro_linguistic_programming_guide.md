## How to Use This Guide

This guide is a reference manual and a due-diligence file, built around an **honest claims-vs-evidence structure** so you never mistake a marketed technique for a proven fact. **For a quick orientation**, read Section 1 (scope, the disambiguation, the honesty framing) and Section 8 (the verdict). **For the history and the people**, read Section 2 — it separates the verifiable facts (Bandler, Grinder, Pucelik, the 1970s, the books) from the movement's own mythology. **For the models and techniques**, read Section 3 — each model is presented as NLP teaches it, with an evidence tag, and the summary table in 3.12 gives the bottom line. **For where NLP is actually sold and used**, read Section 4 (therapy, coaching, sales, communication, corporate training, self-help). **For the scientific evidence**, read Section 5 — the critiques (Sharpley, Einspruch & Forman, Witkowski), the failed replications, and the evidence-based overlaps. **For what to actually do**, read Section 6 (practical responsible use), and **to see it assembled**, Section 7 walks a stakeholder meeting end-to-end. **For definitions**, the glossary in Section 9 covers every term in one place; Section 10 lists the verified references.

Three reading notes. First, the **verification discipline**: every major claim in this guide carries an evidence tag — ✅ *supported by research*, ⚠️ *contested / mixed evidence*, or ❌ *not supported / folklore*. These tags are the point of the guide. Second, the **two-layer reading of NLP**: like most pseudoscience-adjacent systems, NLP is best understood as a *wrapper and a payload* — a set of genuinely useful communication habits (listening, precise questioning, perspective-taking) wrapped in an unsupported theory of mind (representational systems, eye-accessing cues, "programming"). The guide keeps those two layers separate so you can keep the payload and discard the wrapper. Third, **this is not an attack piece**: NLP techniques are described accurately as taught, the small number of positive studies (e.g., the 2017 acrophobia trial) are reported fairly, and the evidence-based overlaps with coaching and CBT are acknowledged — the goal is calibration, not polemic.

## 1. Topic, Disambiguation, and the Honesty Framing

### 1.1 What NLP is: the scope

**Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP)** is a communication, self-development, and psychotherapy-adjacent modality created in the early-to-mid 1970s. Its founders defined it as **"the study of the structure of subjective experience"** — the idea that human experience is built from sensory representations (the "neuro"), language (the "linguistic"), and learned behavioural sequences (the "programming"), and that by modelling how excellent performers think and speak, anyone can replicate their results.

In practice, NLP is best described as **an eclectic toolbox**: rapport-building techniques (mirroring, pacing), language-pattern systems (the meta-model, the Milton model), internal-state change techniques (anchoring, reframing, the swish pattern, the fast phobia cure), and a descriptive vocabulary for subjective experience (representational systems, submodalities, eye-accessing cues). It is taught in short training courses (a "practitioner" certificate can take as little as a few days to a few weeks), sold to corporations as communication training, and popularised in self-help books.

Three things NLP is **not**, stated up front:

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| NLP is a scientific therapy | ❌ No — it is a commercial training system with a contested evidence base (Section 5) |
| NLP is one unified school | ⚠️ No — it fragmented early; dozens of competing lineages, brands, and certification bodies exist with no shared standard (Section 2.7) |
| NLP is a branch of psychology | ❌ No — it originated outside academic psychology (a linguistics professor and a psychology student at UC Santa Cruz), and mainstream psychology departments do not teach it (Section 2.8) |

### 1.2 The disambiguation: which NLP?

**In this guide, "NLP" = Neuro-Linguistic Programming**: the Bandler & Grinder self-development modality from the 1970s — rapport, anchoring, reframing, eye-accessing cues, VAK, the meta-model. The subject of this guide is *people*: how they talk, influence, and change.

**"NLP" does NOT mean Natural Language Processing** — the computer-science field of machines processing human language (transformers, tokenization, RAG, LLMs), which is the subject of the repo's [../technology/ai_llm/](../technology/ai_llm/) guides. The two share only an acronym, and confusing them is a genuine hazard: search for "NLP courses" and you will get both sales-training seminars and machine-learning bootcamps. If you came here expecting transformers and tokenizers, you want [../technology/ai_llm/llm_optimization_complete_guide.md](../technology/ai_llm/llm_optimization_complete_guide.md) instead. This guide is about the 1970s self-development modality, full stop.

### 1.3 The honesty framing: claims vs evidence

This topic is a minefield of confident claims with weak evidence, so the organising discipline of the guide is a **claims-vs-evidence structure**:

- **Sections 2–4 present NLP as its proponents teach and market it** — the history as the movement tells it, the models, the techniques, the applications. No straw-manning: the claims are described accurately, because an accurate account of an unsupported claim is the most useful thing a guide can give you.
- **Section 5 presents the scientific evidence** — the peer-reviewed reviews (Sharpley 1984, 1987; Einspruch & Forman 1985; Witkowski 2010; Sturt et al. 2012), the direct tests (Wiseman et al. 2012), and the academic consensus.
- **Section 6 presents practical guidance built on what the evidence supports**, with the contested and unsupported material clearly flagged.
- **Sections 7–8 assemble it** into a worked example and an honest verdict.

The honest bottom line, stated up front so there is no suspense: **NLP's specific models — representational systems, eye-accessing cues, the "programming" metaphor — have repeatedly failed empirical testing and are widely classified as pseudoscience; but NLP packages several genuinely useful communication habits (rapport, precise questioning, reframing) that have solid support from other traditions.** The value of this guide is learning to separate those two layers, so you can use the useful habits without paying for, or propagating, the unsupported theory.

### 1.4 Why this matters in business

The stakes for a solution architect and manager are practical, not academic:

- **Sales and persuasion training** — "NLP selling" courses are a staple of corporate training budgets; knowing what they actually deliver (and what they can't) protects the budget and the team's time.
- **Coaching and leadership** — "NLP coaching" overlaps with mainstream coaching frameworks (GROW and its relatives, see [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md)); the overlap is where the value is, and the NLP-specific extras are where the claims inflate.
- **Stakeholder communication** — rapport, pacing, and precise questioning are real skills (see [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md)); NLP is one of many places they are taught, and far from the most evidence-grounded.
- **Due diligence on training** — the NLP certification industry is unregulated (Section 6.6); a manager who can spot the red flags of extraordinary claims (Section 6.7) is a manager who does not waste money on cargo-cult training.
- **Lie-detection folklore** — the popular "watch the eyes" claim circulates in negotiation and hiring contexts; the evidence is covered in [body_language_lie_detection_guide.md](body_language_lie_detection_guide.md), and the short version is in Section 5.5.

**The business value of this guide is calibration, not magic:** knowing which NLP-derived habits are real communication skills and which are unsupported theory lets you take the useful 20% and skip the marketed 80% — without either over-credulity or reflexive dismissal.

### 1.5 The NLP presuppositions: the movement's belief system

Every NLP course opens with a list of **presuppositions** — "beliefs that NLP practitioners hold to be useful" — which function as the movement's operating philosophy. They are worth knowing because (a) they are the emotional engine of NLP's appeal and (b) they are a perfect demonstration of the claims-vs-evidence gap:

| Presupposition (as taught) | The claim | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|
| "The map is not the territory" | Our experience of reality is a filtered model, not reality itself | ✅ Compatible with mainstream psychology (perception is constructive); also the basis of reframing |
| "People are not their behaviour" / "behind every behaviour is a positive intention" | Separate the person from the act; find the useful intent | ✅ Good practice (matches CBT's separation of self from thought; restorative-justice thinking); the "positive intention" universal is ⚠️ overstated |
| "There is no failure, only feedback" | Outcomes are information for adjustment | ✅ Good resilience habit (matches growth-mindset research, though the slogan oversimplifies) |
| "If what you're doing isn't working, do something else" | Behavioural flexibility | ✅ Trivially good advice |
| "Everyone has all the resources they need" | No one is broken; change is about access, not capacity | ⚠️ Empowering but empirically dubious in its strong form (people differ in capacities; some need clinical help, not "resource access") |
| "You cannot not communicate" | All behaviour communicates something | ⚠️ Pragmatically useful in communication training; philosophically contestable |
| "The meaning of your communication is the response you get" | Responsibility for the outcome lies with the communicator | ⚠️ Useful accountability device; false as a universal (listeners contribute meaning too) |
| "The unconscious mind is benevolent and serves you" | Unconscious processes work in your favour | ❌ Unsupported; a metaphysical claim, not a psychological finding |

The pattern to notice: the presuppositions are **useful heuristics dressed as psychological laws**. The heuristics (flexibility, feedback, intent-separation) are genuinely helpful in management; the laws ("the unconscious is benevolent") are unfalsifiable beliefs. NLP training typically asks you to *adopt* the presuppositions as a condition of learning — a classic marker of a belief system rather than a scientific finding.

## 2. The History of NLP

> **Reading note.** NLP's origin story is partly verifiable fact and partly movement mythology (the two founders have told inconsistent versions, including of who invented what). This section presents the facts that are independently verifiable — dates, books, people, institutions — and flags the parts that rest on the movement's own accounts.

### 2.1 Origins: Santa Cruz in the 1970s

NLP emerged at the **University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC)** in the **early 1970s**, out of a collaboration between **Richard Bandler**, then a psychology undergraduate, and **John Grinder**, an assistant professor of linguistics. The documented sequence:

- **1972–73:** Bandler, working with the Gestalt therapist and writer Robert Spitzer, began transcribing and editing audio tapes of the late **Fritz Perls**' Gestalt therapy sessions (material later published as *The Gestalt Approach* and *Eye Witness to Therapy*). Bandler started running Gestalt-style groups and became fascinated with what Perls did that made his work effective.
- **~1973–74:** Grinder — a linguist trained in **Noam Chomsky's transformational grammar** — and Bandler began systematically analysing transcripts of Perls and of the family therapist **Virginia Satir**, looking for the *linguistic patterns* underlying their therapeutic effectiveness. This is the documented origin of the **meta-model** (Section 3.2): the claim that therapeutic language change could be described as a formal grammar, in the same way Chomsky described syntax.
- **~1975:** The first book, **The Structure of Magic I: A Book About Language and Therapy**, was published (Science and Behavior Books, 1975), presenting the meta-model. A second volume, *The Structure of Magic II*, followed in 1976 and extended the model to the client's representational systems.
- **1976 (per practitioner accounts):** the work was named "Neuro-Linguistic Programming" — the "neuro" for the sensory/neurological channel, the "linguistic" for the language patterns, the "programming" for the behavioural sequences. ⚠️ *The exact naming date rests on the founders' own accounts; what is verifiable is that the term was in use by 1979, when the book title* Frogs into Princes: Neuro Linguistic Programming *made it public.*

The intellectual stew is worth noting, because NLP's claims are often judged against its ingredients: **Chomsky** (transformational grammar), **Korzybski**'s general semantics ("the map is not the territory"), **Bateson** (systems thinking; Bateson was a UCSC colleague and wrote the introduction to *The Structure of Magic*), plus the clinical pragmatism of Perls, Satir, and Erickson. NLP's theories of mind were thus assembled from linguistics, semantics, and therapy folklore — not from experimental psychology or neuroscience, which is why the field's later empirical failures (Section 5) surprised its practitioners more than its critics.

### 2.2 The modelling project: Satir, Perls, Erickson

The core NLP method is **modelling** (Section 3.10): study an expert, extract the structure of what they do, and teach that structure to others. The original modelling subjects were three master therapists:

| Subject | Speciality | What NLP claims to have extracted |
|---|---|---|
| **Virginia Satir** (1916–1988) | Family therapy / communication | Rapport, pacing, "congruence", the meta-model's precision questioning; Satir's communication patterns (e.g., placating, blaming, computing, distracting) were a documented source for the early models |
| **Fritz Perls** (1893–1970) | Gestalt therapy | Attention to present-moment sensory experience, confrontation of vague language ("I feel bad" → *what exactly? where in the body?*), the use of language patterns to intensify experience — a documented source for the meta-model |
| **Milton H. Erickson** (1901–1980) | Hypnotherapy / indirect suggestion | The **Milton model** (Section 3.3): artfully vague, indirect, presupposition-heavy language; Erickson's hypnotic patterns were analysed in the two *Patterns of the Hypnotic Techniques of Milton H. Erickson* volumes (1975, 1976) |

⚠️ **Verification note:** that Bandler and Grinder studied Satir and Perls transcripts and Erickson's work is verifiable (the books are the evidence). What is *not* verifiable is the stronger NLP claim that modelling yields a complete, teachable replica of an expert's skill — no controlled study has ever demonstrated that an NLP-modelled performer replicates the modelled expert's outcomes (Section 5).

### 2.3 The founding texts

| Book | Year | Content and status |
|---|---|---|
| **The Structure of Magic I** (Bandler & Grinder, Science and Behavior Books) | 1975 | The meta-model: language as surface structure, "deletions, distortions, generalisations", precision questioning. The foundational NLP text. |
| **The Structure of Magic II** | 1976 | Extends the model to representational systems (VAK) and therapist-client matching. |
| **Patterns of the Hypnotic Techniques of Milton H. Erickson, Vols I–II** (Bandler & Grinder, 1975/1976) | 1975–76 | The Milton model; the linguistic analysis of Erickson's hypnotic language. |
| **Frogs into Princes** (Bandler & Grinder, Real People Press) | 1979 | Edited transcripts of NLP workshops; the book that took NLP from an academic-adjacent project to a popular movement. The title itself ("frogs into princes") signals the fairy-tale marketing register that critics later seized on. |

### 2.4 The co-founders and the early team

- **Richard Bandler** (b. 1950) — psychology student at UCSC; the charismatic, often abrasive "wizard" of the pair; primary author of the technique literature (e.g., *Using Your Brain for a Change*, 1985, which introduced the swish pattern and submodalities). Later legal history with Grinder over the NLP trademark is part of the movement's lore.
- **John Grinder** (b. 1940) — linguist; ex-military; brought the Chomskyan grammar machinery that gave NLP its pseudo-formal language of "surface structure", "deep structure", and "transformations".
- **Frank Pucelik** (b. 1949) — UCSC student and Vietnam veteran who worked with Bandler and Grinder in the earliest days; often called "the third man" or third co-founder of NLP. ⚠️ *His exact contribution is documented mainly through practitioner accounts and his own website; the founding texts list Bandler and Grinder as authors.*
- **Supporting cast:** Gregory Bateson (intellectual patron; wrote the foreword to *The Structure of Magic I*), Leslie Cameron-Bandler (Grinder's then-wife; early trainer and author), Robert Dilts (early developer of modelling theory and submodality work), Judith DeLozier, and later developers such as **Tad James** (Timeline Therapy, 1980s — an NLP *offshoot*, not part of the original canon; see 3.11).

### 2.5 The 1980s: popularity, the movement, and the split

The 1980s turned NLP from a Santa Cruz project into a global movement — and simultaneously fragmented it:

- **Popularisation:** *Frogs into Princes* (1979) and the flood of workshop books that followed made NLP a staple of the human-potential circuit. By the mid-1980s NLP was being taught in the US, UK, Europe, and Australia.
- **The split:** Bandler and Grinder's partnership broke down acrimoniously around 1981, followed by years of lawsuits over the NLP name and trademark. The result was a permanent schism: Bandler-branded NLP, Grinder-branded NLP (he later co-developed "New Code NLP" with Carmen Bostic St. Clair), and a proliferation of independent schools, each claiming the true lineage.
- **Therapy fad and backlash:** NLP was marketed as a fast therapy (the "one-session phobia cure") at exactly the moment the academic reviews (Sharpley 1984, 1987) were concluding there was little empirical support — the divergence between commercial success and academic verdict dates from this decade.
- **Fragmentation:** no central body, no curriculum standard, no licensing — a structural feature that persists to this day (2.7).

### 2.6 Business NLP and Anthony Robbins

The single most important populariser of NLP was **Anthony Robbins** (b. 1960), the American self-help author and infomercial star, whose books **Unlimited Power** (1986) and **Awaken the Giant Within** (1991) and whose "Date with Destiny" seminars repackaged NLP (plus fire-walking, neuro-associative conditioning, and motivational showmanship) for a mass audience. Robbins is frequently described as **"NLP-based"**: his core vocabulary — anchoring, reframing, submodalities, "state management", rapport — is straight from the Bandler & Grinder canon, even though Robbins later downplayed the NLP label and built his own brand ("Neuro-Associative Conditioning").

The business consequence: NLP entered the corporate world largely **through the Robbins door** — as motivational training and sales psychology rather than as therapy. "NLP sales", "NLP for leaders", and "NLP communication" workshops in banks, insurers, and consultancies descend from this lineage, which is why Section 4 treats corporate NLP separately from therapeutic NLP. ⚠️ *Note that Robbins' claims (e.g., the fire-walking as a metaphor for "the power of belief") are anecdotes, not data; his transformation claims have never been subjected to controlled study.*

### 2.7 The current status: the 2020s industry and certification landscape

As of the 2020s, NLP is a **thriving but unregulated training industry** with a small research footprint:

- **Scale:** estimates of NLP practitioners run into the tens of thousands worldwide; in the UK alone, over 100,000 people had attended NLP training courses by the late 2000s (Tosey & Mathison 2007/2009). ⚠️ *No reliable census exists; figures come from the industry itself.*
- **Certifications:** a pyramid of **Practitioner → Master Practitioner → Trainer** levels, offered by dozens of competing bodies (ANLP, ABNLP, INLPTA, the Society of NLP, NLP University, countless local schools). **There is no unified structure, no accreditation standard, and no licensing requirement** — anyone can set up a school and issue certificates. Training length ranges from a few days to a few months; costs range from hundreds to tens of thousands of dollars.
- **Corporate penetration:** NLP remains a common offering in corporate training catalogues (communication, sales, leadership) and is still purchased by public bodies — UK NHS organisations spent over £800,000 on NLP-related training between 2006 and 2009 (Sturt et al. 2012) — a fact the industry cites as validation and the critics cite as evidence of weak procurement governance.
- **Academic footprint:** near zero. NLP is not part of mainstream psychology curricula, has no dedicated peer-reviewed journal of record, and its research base is a thin collection of small studies, most of them from the 1980s (Section 5).

### 2.8 The academic status: the pseudoscience designation

The academic verdict on NLP is unambiguous and long-standing:

- **Wikipedia** (which reflects published sources) opens its NLP article: *"Neuro-linguistic programming (NLP) is a pseudoscientific approach to communication, personal development, and psychotherapy that first appeared in Richard Bandler and John Grinder's book The Structure of Magic I (1975)."*
- **The Skeptic's Dictionary** (Robert Todd Carroll) carries an NLP entry documenting the pattern of extraordinary claims with absent evidence — a standard reference in the skepticism literature.
- **Peer-reviewed reviews** — Sharpley (1984, 1987) in the *Journal of Counseling Psychology*, Witkowski (2010) in the *Polish Psychological Bulletin*, and the NHS-commissioned systematic review by Sturt et al. (2012) in the *British Journal of General Practice* — converge on the same conclusion: **the core NLP models lack empirical support, and NLP functions as pseudoscience** (details in Section 5).
- **The critics' characterisation** is captured in phrases like "cargo cult psychology" and "a pseudoscience masquerading as psychology" — the argument being that NLP mimics the *appearance* of science (technical jargon, "modelling", "research data bases") without its substance (controlled tests, falsifiable predictions, replication).

⚠️ **A fairness note before Section 5:** the pseudoscience designation is about NLP's *theoretical core and evidence base*, not about every person who practises it. Many NLP trainers are sincere communicators who teach useful listening and questioning habits; the dispute is about the *models and claims*, not the goodwill of the people involved. Section 5 lays out exactly which parts are unsupported and which parts overlap with evidence-based practice.

### 2.9 How NLP spread: geography and channels

NLP's diffusion is itself instructive — it spread through **commercial channels, not academic ones**:

- **The human-potential circuit (1970s–80s):** workshop tours, cassette tapes, and the Esalen-style growth movement carried NLP far beyond Santa Cruz before any journal article had tested it.
- **The self-help bestseller channel (1980s–90s):** Robbins' books and infomercials took NLP vocabulary to millions; the vocabulary survived in the culture even after the theory was discredited.
- **The corporate training channel (1990s–2020s):** "communication", "sales", and "leadership" workshops packaged NLP for organisations; this is the channel a manager is most likely to meet (Section 4.5).
- **The therapy-adjacent channel:** NLP is bundled with hypnotherapy and "energy" modalities in the complementary-therapy market; in some countries (notably Germany and the UK) it has a foothold in coaching and counselling-adjacent practice.
- **Geographic spread:** strongest in the English-speaking world (US, UK, Australia) plus continental Europe; in Asia, NLP is present in the coaching and sales-training market (including Singapore), usually imported via US/UK franchise schools.

⚠️ **Honest note on scale:** every figure in circulation (numbers of practitioners, "millions trained") originates from the NLP industry itself; there is no independent census. Treat "NLP is used by X people" as marketing data, not as evidence of efficacy.

## 3. The Core Models and Techniques

> **Reading note.** This section describes the NLP models **as taught by NLP practitioners** — the claims, the vocabulary, the techniques. Each subsection ends with an evidence tag; the full evidentiary treatment is in Section 5. Read them as a pair: the models here, the verdicts there.

### 3.1 Representational systems: the VAK model

**The claim.** NLP holds that humans process experience through sensory channels ("modalities" or **representational systems**): primarily **Visual** (V), **Auditory** (A), and **Kinesthetic** (K — feeling, both emotion and body sensation), plus **Olfactory/Gustatory** (O/G, smell/taste) and **Auditory-Digital** (Ad — internal self-talk, "digital" language). Three sub-claims follow:

1. **Preference:** each person has a *preferred* representational system — a dominant channel that shapes how they think, learn, and remember ("I'm a visual person").
2. **Predicates:** the preferred system is revealed in speech — the sensory words a person uses ("I *see* what you mean" = visual; "that *sounds* right" = auditory; "I *feel* that's wrong" = kinesthetic). NLP calls these **predicates**, and training teaches you to listen for them and match them.
3. **Matching:** you can build rapport and influence by matching the other person's predicates ("I see your point" to a visual person, "let's get a feel for it" to a kinesthetic person).

**The evidence.** ❌ **Not supported.** The empirical tests of the 1980s — most systematically reviewed by Sharpley (1984) — found **no reliable relationship between a person's stated preferred system, their spoken predicates, and their actual cognitive processing**; "eye-movement-plus-predicate" tests of the model failed (e.g., Elich, Thompson & Miller 1985 found no support for the combined eye-movement/predicate prediction). The preferred-representational-system construct does not survive controlled measurement. What *is* real: people do use sensory metaphors in speech, and matching a counterpart's vocabulary is a mild rapport aid — but that works through similarity/verbal mirroring, not through any validated "processing channel" mechanism. The VAK learning-styles variant ("visual/auditory/kinesthetic learners") popularised from NLP is itself a documented educational myth.

### 3.2 The meta-model: language patterns and precision questioning

**The claim.** The **meta-model** (from *The Structure of Magic*, 1975) is a system for interrogating vague language. Borrowing Chomsky's distinction between "surface structure" (what is said) and "deep structure" (the fuller meaning), NLP claims that vague or distressing statements arise from three families of linguistic distortion, and that precision questions ("meta-model challenges") restore clarity:

| Pattern family | Examples | Meta-model challenge |
|---|---|---|
| **Deletions** (something left out) | "They don't respect me" / "I'm stuck" | *Who, specifically, doesn't respect you? Stuck in what way, according to whom?* |
| **Distortions** (meaning changed) | "He makes me angry" / "You never listen" | *How, specifically, does he make you angry? Never — not once?* |
| **Generalisations** (one case → all cases) | "All managers are out of touch" / "I always fail at this" | *All? Always? What would count as a counter-example?* |

**The evidence.** ⚠️ **The technique is useful; the theory is unsupported.** There is no evidence for the Chomsky-derived claim that "deep structure" is a psychological reality or that therapy works by "completing" deleted deep structure — that part is pseudo-linguistics. But as a *practical questioning discipline*, the meta-model is close to well-supported practice: clarifying vague complaints, challenging over-generalised self-talk, and asking "who specifically / how exactly / what would count as" are core techniques of **cognitive therapy**, **Socratic questioning**, and good management communication (see [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md)). Treat the meta-model as *a structured reminder of what good clarifying questions look like*, not as a theory of mind.

### 3.3 The Milton model: hypnotic language

**The claim.** The **Milton model** (from the Erickson *Patterns* volumes, 1975–76) is the mirror image of the meta-model: instead of precision, it deliberately uses **"artful vagueness"** — the indirect, presupposition-heavy, ambiguity-laden language that Erickson used in hypnosis. Typical devices: unspecified verbs ("you can relax..."), lack of referential index ("people find..."), presuppositions ("as you notice how comfortable you're becoming..."), and embedded suggestions. The claim is that vague language bypasses conscious resistance and speaks directly to the "unconscious mind", creating trance-like receptivity.

**The evidence.** ❌ **The mechanism is unsupported; the technique is real but mundane.** There is no evidence of an "unconscious mind" that processes artful vagueness differently from ordinary speech. What is real: (a) Erickson was a genuinely influential clinician whose indirect-communication ideas fed into modern clinical hypnosis and (b) vague, presupposing language is simply a persuasive-communication device — politicians, advertisers, and managers use it constantly. Whether it "bypasses the critical faculty" is untestable marketing; whether it is a rhetorical skill you can learn is trivially true. ⚠️ Note also the ethical asymmetry: the Milton model is fundamentally about influencing without the other person's explicit awareness, which raises consent issues in business use (see Section 6).

### 3.4 Eye-accessing cues (summary; cross-reference)

**The claim.** NLP's most famous chart: eye-movement direction reveals which representational system a person is using — up-left = visual *remembered*, up-right = visual *constructed* (imagined), sideways = auditory, down-left = internal dialogue, down-right = kinesthetic. In popular form: **liars look up and to their right** (they are "constructing" a story), truth-tellers look up-left ("remembering") — the basis of countless "spot the liar" workshops and of the NLP-branded lie-detection products sold to law enforcement and corporate clients.

**The evidence.** ❌ **Not supported — and directly tested.** The most famous test is **Wiseman et al. (2012)**, *"The Eyes Don't Have It: Lie Detection and Neuro-Linguistic Programming"* (PLOS ONE): in a controlled lie-detection study, eye-movement direction was unrelated to truthfulness, and trained NLP practitioners did no better than chance. The full treatment — the eye chart, the VAK system, the lateral-eye-movement research, and the deception-science alternative — is in **Section 2–3 of [body_language_lie_detection_guide.md](body_language_lie_detection_guide.md)**; do not duplicate it here. The one-line summary for this guide: **eye-accessing cues are folklore; do not use them for lie detection** (Section 6.5).

### 3.5 Anchoring

**The claim.** **Anchoring** is NLP's version of classical conditioning: an external stimulus (a touch, a word, a gesture) becomes associated with an internal state (confidence, calm, motivation), so that re-presenting the stimulus ("firing the anchor") re-triggers the state. NLP teaches: (1) recall a peak experience of the desired state, (2) apply a unique stimulus at the peak ("set the anchor"), (3) break state, (4) test by firing the anchor. Sales training uses it as "state control" — anchoring yourself into confidence before a pitch.

**The evidence.** ⚠️ **The mechanism is thin; the practice is real but weaker than claimed.** That stimuli can become associated with emotional responses is basic, well-established psychology (Pavlovian conditioning, evaluative conditioning). What is *not* established: that a single quick exercise produces durable, reliable state control in real situations — the NLP "anchor" is a simplification of conditioning that ignores extinction, context-dependence, and individual differences. Controlled studies of NLP anchoring are essentially absent. Practical verdict: as a *self-regulation ritual* (pair a gesture with a calm memory; use it before a meeting) it is harmless and may help via simple attention and expectation effects — but it is not the "neuro-linguistic technology" the marketing claims.

### 3.6 Rapport: mirroring, pacing, leading

**The claim.** **Rapport** — a state of mutual attention and responsiveness — is NLP's foundational social skill. The model: **pacing** (matching the other person's behaviour: posture, gestures, tone, tempo, vocabulary — i.e., mirroring), then **leading** (once rapport is established, the other person unconsciously follows your changes — you slow down, they slow down; you move, they move). The claim is that pacing creates unconscious rapport and leading creates influence.

**The evidence.** ✅/⚠️ **Partially supported — the strongest part of NLP.** The *outcome* (rapport matters) is heavily supported: the therapeutic-alliance literature shows the relationship between practitioner and client predicts outcomes across therapy modalities (Horvath & Symonds 1991), and similarity-attraction research supports the general "we like people like us" effect. What is weaker: the specific **pacing-and-leading mechanism** — the claim that mirrored movements produce measurable influence is supported mainly by small, mixed studies; the "unconscious" choreography claims are overstated. Practical verdict: mirroring is a real, modestly-supported rapport habit (use it lightly and sincerely — obvious mirroring backfires), and it is better understood through the mainstream communication literature ([communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md)) than through NLP's mechanistic story.

### 3.7 Reframing

**The claim.** **Reframing** changes the *meaning* of an experience by changing the frame in which it is viewed — "the map is not the territory" (Korzybski via Bandler & Grinder). Two classic forms: **content reframing** (the situation is the same, the interpretation changes — "the project was delayed" → "the delay let us fix the data model before go-live") and **context reframing** (a behaviour that is a problem in one context is a resource in another — "stubbornness" → "persistence under pressure"). NLP also has a "six-step reframe" for internal conflicts.

**The evidence.** ✅ **Supported — because it is cognitive restructuring.** Reframing is, in substance, what cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) calls **cognitive restructuring**: identifying an automatic interpretation and deliberately generating alternative interpretations. CBT's efficacy for anxiety, depression, and stress is among the best-evidenced results in clinical psychology (Hofmann et al. 2012). NLP's reframing is CBT-adjacent technique wrapped in different jargon — and this is the clearest case of "borrowed technique that works" in the whole system. Note the honest caveat: it works because *interpretation shapes emotion* (well-established), not because of "parts" or "unconscious programming" (unsupported).

### 3.8 Submodalities

**The claim.** **Submodalities** are the fine-grained sensory qualities of internal representations — the "qualia" of the VAK system: for images, *brightness, size, distance, colour, motion, focus*; for sound, *loudness, pitch, tempo, location*; for feelings, *intensity, location, temperature*. NLP claims that changing submodalities changes the *meaning and emotional charge* of a memory or belief — e.g., "push the image further away, drain the colour, shrink it" to defuse a distressing memory, or "make the goal image big, bright, close" to intensify motivation.

**The evidence.** ❌/⚠️ **Technique exists; mechanism unsupported.** That mental imagery has sensory qualities is trivially true (visualisation research confirms imagery varies in vividness). But the specific causal claims — that a given submodality change (e.g., shrinking an image) reliably produces a given emotional change — have no controlled support; the research base is practitioner anecdote. What likely happens is the same thing that happens in any imagery-based intervention: vivid, emotionally-engaged mental rehearsal changes feelings via well-known mechanisms (imagery rescripting in CBT uses the same principle with better evidence). Use imagery techniques if they help; do not buy the "neuro-linguistic" precision story.

### 3.9 The TOTE model

**The claim.** NLP's "strategy" model — how a person runs a behaviour — is the **TOTE** loop: **Test** (compare current state against a goal), **Operate** (act to close the gap), **Test** again, **Exit** when the goal criterion is met. NLP claims that expert behaviours can be described as sequences of TOTE loops over representational systems, and that modelling means reverse-engineering someone's TOTE strategy.

**The evidence.** ⚠️ **Real origin, borrowed frame.** Unlike most NLP models, TOTE has a legitimate academic origin: **Miller, Galanter & Pribram, *Plans and the Structure of Behavior* (1960)**, a foundational cognitive-science book that introduced the Test-Operate-Test-Exit loop as a unit of behavioural organisation. What NLP added — mapping TOTE steps to VAK systems and claiming to "install" strategies — is unsupported. The TOTE loop itself is a reasonable, if coarse, way to describe goal-directed behaviour and is compatible with modern feedback-control views of action; NLP's specific "strategy installation" claims have never been validated.

### 3.10 Modelling: the core NLP method

**The claim.** **Modelling** is NLP's founding method and its distinctive claim: study an expert (in vivo or via transcripts), extract the structure of their excellence — their beliefs, state, strategies, language — and produce a transferable model others can learn. NLP presents itself not as a theory but as a *meta-skill*: "we don't know what's true, we model what works."

**The evidence.** ❌ **The flagship claim has no demonstrated results.** After fifty years, there is **no controlled demonstration that NLP modelling produces expert-level performance in a novice** — no study shows that NLP-modelled salespeople outsell controls, NLP-modelled therapists outperform trained peers, or NLP-modelled leaders replicate the modelled leader's outcomes. The method's output is *models as judged by modellers* (subjective, non-falsifiable) rather than *replication as measured by outcome*. By contrast, evidence-based skills training (deliberate practice, structured feedback, simulation) has strong support — which is why the honest recommendation (Section 6) is to treat "modelling" as a metaphor for "study good people and imitate them", a practice as old as apprenticeship, and skip the pseudo-technical apparatus.

### 3.11 The techniques: swish, fast phobia cure, parts integration, timeline therapy

| Technique | What it claims to do | How it works (per NLP) | Evidence tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Swish pattern** (Bandler, 1985) | Replace an unwanted habitual response (a craving, a nervous habit) with a new one | Visualise the cue image, then "swish" it with a desired-self image, rapidly, several times; submodality change (shrink/dim the cue) | ❌ No controlled support; essentially an imagery-rehearsal technique; any benefit plausibly from attention/expectation |
| **Fast phobia cure** (V/K dissociation) | Cure a phobia in one session | "Dissociate": watch yourself in the phobic memory from outside your body (visual-kinesthetic dissociation), then run the memory backwards/fast to defuse the emotional charge | ⚠️ One small positive RCT: **Arroll et al. 2017** (fear of heights, *International Journal of Psychiatry in Medicine*) used an NLP-style imaginal intervention and reported short-term improvement; otherwise no evidence base. The dissociation principle overlaps with imagery-based treatments (EMDR lineage) — but exposure therapy remains the heavily-evidenced standard for phobias |
| **Parts integration / visual squash** | Resolve internal conflicts ("a part of me wants X, a part wants Y") by personifying the parts and negotiating integration | Represent conflicting "parts" as images, dialogue between them, then "squash" them together into a new integrated image | ❌ No controlled support; "parts" are a metaphor with no psychological mechanism; overlap with cognitive conflict-resolution is only metaphorical |
| **Timeline therapy** (Tad James, 1980s) | Release negative emotions and limiting beliefs by "revisiting" events along an imagined personal timeline | Hypnotic regression along a spatialised timeline; a trademarked offshoot of NLP, **not** part of the Bandler & Grinder canon | ❌ No controlled support; hypnotic "regression" produces confabulated memories (a documented risk); trademarked commercial package |

### 3.13 Meta-programmes: NLP's personality system

**The claim.** **Meta-programmes** are NLP's answer to personality: habitual filters ("programmes about programmes") that shape how people process information — e.g., *towards/away* motivation (moving toward goals vs away from problems), *internal/external* reference (self-judged vs other-judged), *sameness/difference* (noticing what's the same vs what's different), *global/specific* (big picture vs detail). Business NLP sells "meta-programme profiling" as a way to read colleagues, customers, and candidates quickly ("he's an away-from, internal-reference, specific person — here's how to sell to him").

**The evidence.** ❌ **Not supported as a measurement system.** The meta-programme taxonomy is intuitively plausible (the underlying dimensions resemble real individual-difference constructs studied in psychology — regulatory focus, locus of evaluation, field dependence) but the NLP version has **no validated psychometrics**: no reliability data, no validation studies, no evidence that untrained observers can reliably classify people, and no evidence that profiling improves selling, hiring, or management outcomes. In psychology, personality measurement works only with validated instruments (e.g., the Big Five questionnaires, which have decades of reliability and validity research behind them). NLP's meta-programme profiling is astrology-with-jargon by comparison: plausible categories, confident readings, zero measurement science. **Practical position:** if you want to understand individual differences at work, use validated tools and good questions (see [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) and [360_management_guide.md](360_management_guide.md)); treat meta-programme labels as conversation shorthand at best.

## 4. The Applications

### 4.1 Therapy

**What NLP claims:** fast treatment for phobias, trauma, anxiety, depression, and even physical conditions — the "one-session phobia cure", "trauma release" via dissociation, and the famous claim that NLP can cure in hours what takes therapy months.

**What the evidence says:** ❌ **Insufficient.** The NHS-commissioned systematic review (**Sturt et al. 2012**, *British Journal of General Practice*) searched the literature and concluded there was **insufficient evidence of NLP's effectiveness for health outcomes**, with few studies and quality too low to draw conclusions. The single often-cited positive trial — **Arroll et al. 2017** for fear of heights — is small, single-site, and reports short-term self-reported improvement. Meanwhile the evidence-based treatments for the same problems are strong: **exposure therapy** for phobias, **CBT** for anxiety and depression, **EMDR** and trauma-focused CBT for PTSD. **Responsible position:** if a colleague or client is considering NLP "therapy" for a clinical condition, steer them to regulated, evidence-based care — and note that anyone presenting NLP as a treatment for trauma, depression, or physical illness is making claims the literature does not support (red-flag list, Section 6.7).

### 4.2 Coaching

**What NLP claims:** "NLP coaching" accelerates goal achievement, removes limiting beliefs, and installs empowering strategies; certification paths (NLP coach, Master NLP coach) are sold as career credentials.

**What the evidence says:** ⚠️ **The coaching overlap is real; the NLP-specific claims are not.** Mainstream coaching — structured conversations using frameworks like **GROW** (Goal, Reality, Options, Will) — is a well-established practice, and its effectiveness rests on supported mechanisms: goal-setting (Locke & Latham), structured questioning, accountability, and the coaching alliance. See the coaching frameworks in depth in [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md), which covers GROW and its relatives. NLP coaching is *coaching with NLP jargon and techniques bolted on*: the GROW skeleton does the work, the "limiting-belief removal" and "strategy installation" add the unvalidated layer. **Practical position:** use evidence-based coaching frameworks; treat "NLP coach" certification as an unregulated marketing credential, not a professional qualification (Section 6.6).

### 4.3 Sales and marketing

**What NLP claims:** NLP selling — "read the customer's representational system, mirror their predicates, anchor positive states, and close more deals"; NLP persuasion is marketed as a predictable influence technology, sometimes with explicitly manipulative framing ("weapons of influence", "hypnotic selling").

**What the evidence says:** ❌ **No demonstrated sales effect.** There are no controlled field studies showing NLP-trained salespeople outperform untrained ones. The specific mechanisms (predicate matching, eye-cue reading) have failed laboratory tests (Section 5.5). What *is* real in good selling — listening, rapport, questioning, framing value, handling objections — is ordinary, well-evidenced communication skill (covered in [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) and [vendor_management_guide.md](vendor_management_guide.md)). **Practical position:** the honest version of "NLP sales" is "professional communication skills"; the manipulative version (covert anchoring, "hypnotic" closes) is both unsupported and an ethics and conduct risk in a regulated bank environment.

### 4.4 Communication

**What NLP claims:** the meta-model for precise questioning, rapport for connection, the Milton model for influential language, reframing for handling objections — marketed as a complete communication system.

**What the evidence says:** ✅/⚠️ **The useful core is real communication skill.** The meta-model's precision questions, rapport behaviour, and reframing map onto supported practices: clarifying questioning, active listening, and perspective-taking are staples of evidence-based communication training; reframing is CBT cognitive restructuring (Section 3.7). This is the *legitimate face of NLP* — and note that you can get all of it from mainstream sources (the communication and stakeholder guide in this series covers it without the pseudo-science). **Practical position:** take the communication habits, skip the theory, and prefer evidence-grounded training vendors.

### 4.5 Corporate training

**What NLP claims:** "NLP for leaders", "NLP negotiation", "NLP presentation skills" — corporate workshops promising better influence, emotional intelligence, and performance.

**What the evidence says:** ⚠️ **A market reality with a thin evidence base.** NLP training is genuinely widespread in corporates and public bodies (the UK NHS's £800k+ spend, 2006–2009, documented by Sturt et al. 2012; 100,000+ UK attendees per Tosey & Mathison). But corporate NLP research consists of small, self-report, practitioner-run studies; there is no rigorous evidence that NLP training improves leadership or business outcomes beyond what general communication training delivers. Notably, the only academic book-length treatment of NLP in management — **Tosey & Mathison, *Neuro-Linguistic Programming: A Critical Appreciation for Managers and Developers* (2009)** — is sympathetic but explicitly critical: it treats NLP as a *useful set of practices and a language for reflecting on experience*, not as validated science. **Practical position for a manager:** if NLP training is on the table, ask what *specific, measurable* skills it will install, compare it against evidence-based communication training, and treat "certified NLP trainer" claims as marketing (6.6).

### 4.6 Self-help

**What NLP claims:** the Robbins channel — "rewrite your programming", "take control of your state", "model success" — personal transformation marketed through books, seminars, and infomercials; NLP submodalities and anchoring as "life-hacking" tools.

**What the evidence says:** ⚠️ **Self-help value with zero causal evidence.** Self-help can work through well-known mechanisms (goal-setting, structured reflection, social support, expectancy). But the specific NLP claims of "reprogramming" and "state control" have no controlled support, and the motivational-seminar industry's testimonials are anecdotes, not data. **Practical position:** read Robbins and Bandler as *motivational rhetoric with useful communication habits inside* — not as a science of the mind; the vocabulary ("anchoring", "reframing", "state") is a handy language for talking about communication and emotion, which is exactly how Section 8 will frame it.

### 4.7 Applications table: domain, claim, evidence

| Domain | Typical NLP claim | Evidence | Honest bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Therapy** | Fast cures for phobias, trauma, depression | ❌ Insufficient (Sturt et al. 2012); one small positive RCT (Arroll 2017) | Use exposure/CBT/EMDR — the evidence-based treatments |
| **Coaching** | Accelerated goals via "strategy installation" | ⚠️ Coaching works; NLP-specific layer unvalidated | Use GROW and the coaching alliance ([team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md)) |
| **Sales/marketing** | "NLP selling" closes more deals; read the customer | ❌ No field evidence; mechanisms fail in lab tests | Good selling = good communication skill, nothing more |
| **Communication** | Meta-model, rapport, reframing as a complete system | ✅/⚠️ Core habits supported via other traditions | Take the habits; skip the theory |
| **Corporate training** | "NLP for leaders" improves influence and performance | ⚠️ Widespread market, thin evidence; critical academic treatment (Tosey & Mathison 2009) | Benchmark against evidence-based communication training |
| **Self-help** | Reprogram yourself, model success | ⚠️ Self-help mechanisms real; NLP-specific claims unsupported | Enjoy the rhetoric; discount the "programming" |

### 4.8 Hiring and recruitment

**What NLP claims:** NLP-based interviewing — "read the candidate's representational system", "spot lies with eye cues", "use meta-programme profiling to predict fit" — sold to HR as a shortcut to better hires; some NLP-branded pre-employment screening products claim to assess "honesty" or "personality" from language or eye patterns.

**What the evidence says:** ❌ **A liability, not an asset.** Every component is unsupported: eye-cue lie detection failed its direct test (Wiseman 2012), meta-programme profiling has no validated psychometrics (3.13), and there is no evidence NLP screening improves hiring outcomes. Worse, using them creates real risk: confident-but-wrong judgments, possible discrimination exposure (profiling candidates on unvalidated categories), and legal exposure if a rejected candidate challenges the method. **Responsible position:** hire on structured interviews, validated assessments, and work-sample tests — the evidence-based toolkit (see [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) for interviewing craft). If a vendor proposes NLP-based screening, treat it as a red flag (Section 6.7) and decline.

## 5. The Scientific Evidence

> **Reading note.** This section is the evidentiary core of the guide: the peer-reviewed record on NLP, from the early reviews of the 1980s through the direct tests of the 2010s, then the evidence-based overlaps. The verdict is not "NLP is a lie" — it is the more precise finding that NLP's *specific models* are unsupported while some of its *practices* are supported under other names.

### 5.1 The early critiques: Sharpley (1984, 1987)

The first systematic assessment of NLP's empirical claims came from the counselling-psychology literature. **Christopher Sharpley**, reviewing the research on the preferred representational system (PRS) — the VAK/predicate-matching model — concluded that studies failed to support NLP's claims:

- **Sharpley (1984)**, *"Predicate Matching in NLP: A Review of Research on the Preferred Representational System"* (*Journal of Counseling Psychology*, 31(2)): reviewed the empirical literature on PRS and predicate matching and found **little support for the construct** — the claimed link between a person's preferred sensory system, their language, and their behaviour did not hold up in controlled studies.
- **Sharpley (1987)**, *"Research Findings on Neurolinguistic Programming: Nonsupportive Data or an Untestable Theory?"* (*Journal of Counseling Psychology*, 34(1)): extended the review with new studies and posed the key question — the data were not merely *unsupportive*, the theory was in danger of being **untestable** (claims stated so vaguely that no experiment could refute them). His conclusion: NLP's core assumptions lacked empirical support.

These two papers became the standard citation for "NLP has no evidence", and they remain the scholarly baseline today.

### 5.2 The counter-critique: Einspruch & Forman (1985)

NLP practitioners and their academic sympathisers did not accept the early verdict. **Eric Einspruch and Bruce Forman** — themselves NLP-aligned — published *"Observations Concerning Research Literature on Neuro-Linguistic Programming"* (*Journal of Counseling Psychology*, 32(4), 589–596) as **a critique of the critiques**: they examined 39 empirical studies of NLP and argued that most were methodologically flawed — researchers had not been properly trained in NLP, had tested NLP poorly (e.g., inferring PRS from a single cue), and had therefore produced invalid null results. Their conclusion: the case against NLP was **premature**, and properly-conducted research might yet vindicate it.

⚠️ **Verification note (dates):** Einspruch & Forman's *Journal of Counseling Psychology* article is **1985** (32(4), 589–596); some NLP sources miscite it as 1988 (a 1988 publication of theirs exists in the NLP bibliography, but the famous counter-critique is the 1985 one). This guide uses the verified 1985 citation.

**How the dispute resolved:** the Einspruch & Forman critique was legitimate methodological pushback, and Sharpley (1987) engaged with it directly. But the practical outcome was decisive against NLP: two decades of subsequent research *did* use better methods (properly-trained NLP practitioners in the Wiseman 2012 lie-detection study, for example), and the null results persisted. The methodological excuses have run out: **the later, better studies still failed to support NLP** (5.3, 5.5).

### 5.3 The later reviews: Witkowski (2010) and the systematic review

- **Witkowski (2010)**, *"Thirty-Five Years of Research on Neuro-Linguistic Programming. NLP Research Data Base. State of the Art or Pseudoscientific Decoration?"* (*Polish Psychological Bulletin*, 41(2), 58–66): the most comprehensive review of the NLP research base. From **315 articles in the NLP Research Data Base**, Witkowski selected **63 studies published in journals from the ISI Master Journal List** (i.e., the best-indexed, most defensible subset) and analysed them against the field's own claims. His conclusion: even the *best* NLP research does not support NLP's models and claims — the research base functions as **"pseudoscientific decoration"**: it gives the *appearance* of a scientific literature while containing no convincing evidence. This paper is the definitive statement of the pseudoscience characterisation in the peer-reviewed literature.
- **Sturt et al. (2012)**, *"Neurolinguistic Programming: A Systematic Review of the Effects on Health Outcomes"* (*British Journal of General Practice*, 62(604), e757–e764): commissioned in an NHS context (following the NHS's own NLP training spend), this systematic review searched for trials of NLP interventions with health outcomes. It found **few studies, of poor quality, with high risk of bias** — and concluded there was **insufficient evidence** that NLP improves health outcomes. The one notable positive trial it could point to is the acrophobia study later published as **Arroll et al. (2017)** — small and short-term.

Both reviews converge with Sharpley: **the empirical case for NLP's specific models and techniques has not been made in fifty years of opportunity.**

### 5.4 The academic consensus: pseudoscience and fringe status

Beyond individual studies, the *institutional* treatment of NLP is telling:

- **Textbooks and curricula:** NLP is absent from mainstream psychology, psychotherapy, and communication textbooks; it is not taught in university psychology departments (it appears occasionally in business schools as a *topic of study* — e.g., Tosey & Mathison's critical appreciation — not as a validated method).
- **Reference works:** Wikipedia's lead sentence classifies NLP as **pseudoscientific**; **The Skeptic's Dictionary** (Carroll) documents the pattern of extraordinary claims without evidence; skeptical and science-based-medicine outlets have repeatedly covered NLP's failure to produce evidence.
- **Funding and journals:** NLP has no presence in mainstream psychology journals' regular pages (its papers appear in practitioner-run or low-tier venues), and no major funding body has supported its research programme — a consistent marker of scientific fringe status.
- **The "extraordinary claims" asymmetry:** NLP claims extraordinary things (one-session phobia cures, reading minds via eye movements, installing expert strategies) — under the standard evidentiary bar, extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and NLP's evidence is, at best, ordinary anecdote. The failure of the *claimants* to produce it — despite a commercial industry with every incentive to do so — is itself informative.

⚠️ **Fairness note:** "fringe" and "pseudoscience" are verdicts about the *evidence and epistemology* of NLP, not about individual practitioners. They mean: NLP's models are not established by the standards of empirical science, and its marketing overstates its basis. They do not mean every NLP technique is harmful or every trainer is dishonest (Section 6 separates the useful habits from the unsupported theory).

### 5.5 Failed replications: eye-accessing cues and representational systems

The two most testable NLP claims — eye-accessing cues and the VAK representational system — have both been directly tested and have failed:

- **Eye-accessing cues — Wiseman et al. (2012)**, *"The Eyes Don't Have It: Lie Detection and Neuro-Linguistic Programming"* (*PLOS ONE*, 7(7), e40259): participants told truth/lies on video; eye-movement direction was coded; **eye movements did not differ between truth and lie conditions**, and — crucially — **trained NLP practitioners who used the eye chart judged truthfulness no better than chance**. The study was designed with NLP practitioners' input, addressing the "you tested it wrong" objection. Full treatment in [body_language_lie_detection_guide.md](body_language_lie_detection_guide.md), Sections 2–3. Related research on **lateral eye movements** (Ehrlichman & Weinberger 1978, and later) likewise failed to find the claimed hemispheric-processing link.
- **Representational systems / predicate matching:** the PRS construct failed the 1980s tests reviewed by Sharpley (1984); later combined tests (eye movement + predicate, e.g., Elich, Thompson & Miller 1985) also found no support; no subsequent well-controlled study has resurrected it. The **VAK learning-styles** variant is independently documented as an educational myth (there is no evidence that matching instruction to a "learning style" improves outcomes).
- **Anchoring and submodality rules:** no controlled studies demonstrate the specific causal claims (that firing an anchor reliably reinstates a state, or that shrinking an image reliably reduces its emotional charge). The absence of positive evidence is consistent across the fifty-year window.

The pattern is the signature of a pseudoscience: **specific, testable predictions, repeatedly tested, repeatedly null — while the marketing continues unchanged.**

### 5.6 The evidence-based overlaps: what actually works

The honest counterpart to Section 5.5: several NLP practices *work* — because they were borrowed from, or coincide with, evidence-based traditions:

| NLP practice | Evidence-based equivalent | The actual evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Rapport** | **Therapeutic alliance** (Horvath & Symonds 1991) | The quality of the practitioner-client relationship predicts outcomes across therapy modalities — one of the most replicated findings in psychotherapy research; similarity-attraction supports "we like people like us" |
| **Meta-model precision questions** | **Socratic questioning / clarifying questions** | Core of cognitive therapy and of every evidence-based communication-training curriculum; asking "who specifically / how exactly" is textbook good practice |
| **Active listening / pacing** | **Active listening** (Rogers 1957; motivational interviewing) | Reflective listening and non-judgmental attention are validated components of counselling and coaching |
| **Reframing** | **Cognitive restructuring (CBT)** (Hofmann et al. 2012) | CBT's efficacy for anxiety, depression, and stress is among the best-evidenced results in clinical psychology; reframing is restructuring by another name |
| **Goal-directed coaching** | **Goal-setting theory** (Locke & Latham 2002); GROW coaching | Specific, challenging, committed goals reliably improve performance — the mechanism behind coaching's effectiveness (see [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md)) |
| **Imagery techniques** | **Imagery rescripting / visualisation** | Mental imagery reliably affects emotion and performance (sport psychology, CBT imagery work); the specific NLP submodality rules remain unsupported |

The pattern is unmistakable: **where NLP works, it works because it is doing something mainstream psychology already understands — under a different name and with added jargon.**

### 5.7 The honest assessment

The fairest one-sentence summary of the evidence: **NLP is an eclectic toolbox of communication techniques — most of them borrowed — wrapped in an unsupported cognitive model.** Concretely:

- **Unsupported:** the theory of mind (representational systems, submodality mechanics, "programming", deep-structure linguistics), the signature claims (eye-accessing cues, one-session cures, strategy installation), and the flagship method (modelling) — ❌ across the board.
- **Supported by other traditions:** rapport/alliance, precise questioning, reframing/restructuring, goal-directed coaching, imagery — ✅ where they overlap with psychology's own validated practices.
- **Untested rather than disproven:** some techniques (e.g., anchoring as a self-regulation ritual) have never been properly studied; the honest label is "no evidence", not "proven false" — though fifty years of commercial incentive to produce evidence, and none appearing, is itself a signal.
- **The business implication:** a corporate buyer should value NLP training at roughly the value of *generic communication-skills training*, and discount the NLP-specific claims to zero — the extra premium buys jargon, not results.

### 5.8 Why NLP persists: the psychology of pseudoscience appeal

Given the evidence record, the interesting question is not "does NLP work?" but "why does NLP keep selling?" — and the answer is well-studied cognitive and social psychology, not mystery:

- **The Barnum (Forer) effect:** meta-programme profiles and "representational system" readings are written vaguely enough that people recognise themselves in them — the same mechanism that powers horoscopes and handwriting analysis. Personality-testing research has documented this for decades; NLP profiling is a textbook case.
- **Confirmation bias:** once a practitioner believes a candidate is "auditory" or "away-from motivated", they notice confirming behaviours and miss disconfirming ones; the technique never gets a fair test in everyday use.
- **Base-rate neglect:** "the eye chart caught him lying" anecdotes ignore that most people are telling the truth most of the time — a "lie detector" that guesses "truth" scores high while detecting nothing (the veracity-effect problem covered in [body_language_lie_detection_guide.md](body_language_lie_detection_guide.md)).
- **The placebo of attention:** any training that makes people listen more carefully, ask better questions, and practise deliberately will produce *some* improvement — and the improvement is attributed to the NLP theory rather than to the generic practice (Section 5.6).
- **Sunk cost and community:** people who paid for certification defend the system to justify the investment; the trainer community provides identity, income, and mutual reinforcement — the classic sociology of a belief community.
- **The untestable escape hatch:** when results fail, NLP's unfalsifiable vocabulary ("resistance", "unconscious sabotage", "you tested it wrong") absorbs the failure, so the theory is never confronted with a disconfirming outcome.

None of this requires NLP trainers to be frauds — it requires only that *people reliably over-attribute results to the system they invested in*. That is why the evidence discipline (Section 6) matters more for NLP than for most topics: the error is built into the way the system is marketed and experienced.

## 6. Practical Responsible Use

> **Reading note.** This section answers the question a manager actually faces: *given everything above, what do I do?* The answer is a triage: use the supported habits, flag the contested ones, avoid the falsified ones — and apply buyer-beware discipline to the industry.

### 6.1 Use: rapport and mirroring ✅

Mirroring is the most supported NLP practice (via similarity-attraction and the therapeutic-alliance literature — Section 5.6). Practical rules for honest use:

- **Match the essentials, not the mannerisms:** energy level, speaking pace, formality, and vocabulary register — not crossed legs and head tilts (obvious physical mirroring reads as mimicry and backfires).
- **Match language lightly:** if a stakeholder says "I want to *see* the roadmap", "here's *what it looks like*" lands better than "here's how it *feels*" — matching sensory vocabulary is harmless and improves perceived connection, whatever the mechanism.
- **Lead rarely and only after rapport:** the pacing→leading claim is overstated, but there is a mundane version that works: once a conversation is flowing, your calm tone and slower pace *do* tend to pull a heated counterpart down — this is emotion regulation through modelling, not "unconscious programming".
- **Be sincere:** rapport techniques work only when the underlying intent is genuine attention. The instant they become manipulation, they stop being rapport and become a conduct risk.

### 6.2 Use: meta-model-style precision questioning ✅

The meta-model, stripped of its pseudo-linguistics, is a structured version of what good questioners do anyway. Use it directly:

- **Deletions:** "The stakeholders are unhappy" → *Which stakeholders, specifically, and unhappy about what, exactly?*
- **Distortions:** "This vendor is making us lose money" → *How, specifically, is the vendor doing that? What is our own process contributing?*
- **Generalisations:** "IT never delivers on time" → *Never? Which deliveries were on time last quarter? What's different about the late ones?*

These are the same moves as CBT's thought-challenging and good stakeholder elicitation — see [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) for the broader questioning toolkit. The discipline is the value: **ask the second question.**

### 6.3 Use: reframing ✅

Reframing is cognitive restructuring, and it is genuinely useful in management:

- **For yourself:** the project delay that "proves I'm a bad planner" reframed as "the plan missed a dependency — what does the data say about which dependencies?" — this is exactly the *decatastrophising* and *evidence-checking* of CBT, applied to work stress.
- **For your team:** a failed release reframed from "we failed" to "we learned where our test coverage was thin" — without being saccharine about it (forced positivity is a different, worse thing).
- **For stakeholders:** a scope cut reframed from "they're killing the project" to "they've re-prioritised toward the compliance deadline — what does that mean for what we *can* deliver?" — perspective-taking is a core stakeholder skill.

The honest caveat: reframing changes *interpretation*, which is real — it does not change the underlying situation. Use it to find better action, not to paper over genuine problems.

### 6.4 Flag: anchoring and imagery techniques ⚠️

Anchoring and submodality work have plausible mechanisms (conditioning; imagery affects emotion) but no NLP-specific evidence. Honest use:

- **Allowed:** as a private self-regulation ritual — pairing a gesture with a calm memory before a difficult meeting; visualising a successful outcome; using imagery to rehearse a presentation. These are harmless and may help via attention, relaxation, and preparation effects.
- **Flag:** any claim that anchoring produces *durable, reliable, externally-observable* state control ("I can switch my team's mood with a touch") — unsupported, and the touch-the-stakeholder version is a professional and cultural landmine (personal-space norms vary; in a banking context, physical anchoring of others is simply unacceptable).
- **Never:** using anchoring or "hypnotic" techniques on other people without their knowledge and consent — that is covert influence, and it is both ethically indefensible and a conduct risk.

### 6.5 Avoid: eye-accessing cues and NLP lie detection ❌

Directly falsified (Wiseman et al. 2012) — do not use:

- **Do not** infer truthfulness or "representational processing" from eye direction — in hiring, negotiation, or stakeholder conversations. You will be wrong at chance levels, and you will be overconfident.
- **Do not** pay for "NLP lie detection" training or tools — the flagship product of this genre failed its most direct test, with trained NLP practitioners at chance accuracy.
- **Do** read [body_language_lie_detection_guide.md](body_language_lie_detection_guide.md) for what actually helps in deception contexts: verbal detail probes, cognitive-load questioning, congruence checking — the evidence-based toolkit.

### 6.6 NLP in business: honest use and buyer beware

**The honest use** of NLP in a corporate setting is indistinguishable from good communication practice: listen actively, ask precise questions, build rapport, take perspectives, set goals. You can take all of this from the evidence-based sources in this series ([communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md), [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md)) without ever touching NLP's theory.

**Buyer beware — the certification industry:** NLP certification is **unregulated**:

- No licensing body, no accreditation standard, no minimum curriculum, no quality control — anyone can found a school and issue "Practitioner / Master Practitioner / Trainer" certificates.
- Certificate quality varies from a weekend workshop to a months-long course, and the certificate's market value is whatever the buyer believes it is — it is **not** a recognised professional qualification in psychology, coaching, or HR.
- If a vendor's credibility rests on "certified NLP trainer" credentials, ask instead: *what skills will my people measurably gain, and what is the evidence?* A vendor who cannot answer beyond NLP jargon is selling jargon (see 6.7).
- If a *contractor or colleague* lists NLP certification as a qualification for a coaching/people role, weigh it as *interest and training attended* — not as validated competence.

**Procurement guidance (the £800k lesson):** the NHS's six-figure NLP spend (Sturt et al. 2012) is the canonical example of what happens when training procurement does not ask for evidence. Before approving NLP training: (1) demand the specific, measurable skills; (2) benchmark against evidence-based communication training (often cheaper); (3) require that any claimed outcomes be testable; (4) be alert to the red flags below.

### 6.7 Red flags: extraordinary claims

The pseudoscience detection checklist — if a trainer, book, or course makes any of these moves, treat the whole pitch with suspicion:

| Red flag | Example (real NLP marketing) | Why it is a red flag |
|---|---|---|
| **Extraordinary outcomes, no data** | "Cure phobias in one session", "read people's minds", "install success strategies" | Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence; none is offered beyond testimonials |
| **Unfalsifiable theory** | "It works at the unconscious level", "you tested it wrong because you weren't NLP-trained" | The theory can explain any outcome, so no outcome can test it (Sharpley's "untestable theory" problem) |
| **Jargon as evidence** | "Neuro-linguistic technology", "representational system analysis", "strategy elicitation" | Technical-sounding vocabulary is the *appearance* of science, not science |
| **Testimonial authority** | "Used by 100,000 people", "trusted by the NHS", celebrity endorsements | Popularity and institutional *purchases* are not efficacy evidence (the NHS bought it — and the NHS-commissioned review found it lacking) |
| **Hostility to testing** | "Double-blind studies can't capture NLP", "Western science is too limited" | A method that cannot be tested by standard science is not above science — it is outside it |
| **Guru mystique** | Founder lore, secret techniques, "only in our lineage" | Cult-ish packaging correlates with unaccountable claims |
| **Cure-all breadth** | Treating phobias *and* sales *and* leadership *and* physical illness with one system | Genuine methods have boundaries; one-size-fits-all is marketing |

### 6.8 A decision framework for NLP material

When NLP material crosses your desk — a course proposal, a book, a colleague's pitch — run this triage:

1. **Name the practice** — strip the jargon: *what concrete behaviour is being taught?* (listening, questioning, mirroring, imagery, goal-setting...).
2. **Check the evidence for that behaviour** — does mainstream psychology support it? (Sections 5.6, 3.12.)
3. **Discount the wrapper** — the VAK theory, the "programming" story, the eye chart add no validated value; price them at zero.
4. **Check the ethics** — does it involve covert influence on others? Then decline.
5. **Check the vendor** — regulated qualifications? testable claims? willingness to be benchmarked? (6.6–6.7.)
6. **Keep what survives** — the supported habits (rapport, precision questions, reframing, goals) are worth keeping regardless of where you learned them.

### 6.9 Quick reference card: keep / flag / avoid

The entire practical argument of this guide, on one card:

| NLP item | Verdict | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Rapport, pacing, mirroring | ✅ Keep | Match energy, register, and sensory vocabulary — lightly and sincerely |
| Meta-model precision questions | ✅ Keep | Ask "who specifically / how exactly / what counts as" — the second question is the skill |
| Reframing | ✅ Keep | Use it as cognitive restructuring: change the interpretation, keep the substance real |
| Goal-directed coaching structure | ✅ Keep | Use GROW and goal-setting theory ([team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md)) — the coaching works, the "strategy installation" doesn't |
| Anchoring, imagery, submodalities | ⚠️ Flag | Fine as private self-regulation rituals; zero NLP-specific evidence; never on others without consent |
| VAK/predicate "reading" of people | ⚠️ Flag | Sensory language is real; using it to "type" people is astrology-with-jargon |
| Meta-programme profiling | ❌ Avoid | No psychometrics; use validated instruments instead |
| Eye-accessing cues / NLP lie detection | ❌ Avoid | Falsified (Wiseman 2012); see [body_language_lie_detection_guide.md](body_language_lie_detection_guide.md) for what actually works |
| NLP "therapy" for clinical conditions | ❌ Avoid | Use exposure/CBT/EMDR; NLP has insufficient evidence (Sturt 2012) |
| NLP certification as a credential | ❌ Avoid paying for | Unregulated; value = generic communication training; treat as interest, not qualification |
| "One-session cures", "mind-reading", "install success" | ❌ Red flag | Extraordinary claims, no data — walk away (Section 6.7) |

## 7. Worked Example: A Difficult Stakeholder Meeting

> **Scenario:** You are a solution architect at a bank, running a design review of a regulatory-reporting platform with the Head of Finance Operations — a stakeholder who is "difficult": they interrupt, dismiss your estimates as "IT fairy tales", and have already threatened to escalate. The meeting is going badly. The sibling guide [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) covers the full stakeholder toolkit; this example shows only the NLP-derived moves that survive evidence review — applied honestly.

### 7.1 The scenario

The stakeholder opens with: *"Every IT project at this bank overruns. Yours will too. I don't have time for another roadmap full of fairy tales."* Three NLP-derived tools — rapport, meta-model-style questioning, reframing — plus the honest frame that these are communication skills, not magic.

### 7.2 Rapport: pacing (the supported part)

**What you do:** match their energy — sit forward, match their clipped tone and pace, drop the slide deck for a moment, and acknowledge the premise before defending anything. *"You're right that delivery has been a problem here, and your time is the scarcest resource in this room, so let me be direct about what I can and can't commit."*

**Why it works (honestly):** you have (a) validated their reality — the fastest route to de-escalation, supported by the negotiation and conflict literature; (b) matched register, which similarity research and the therapeutic-alliance findings say modestly increases connection; (c) NOT mirrored their hostility, crossed your arms, or tried to "pace-and-lead" them like a stage hypnotist. The mundane mechanism — acknowledgment plus calm — is doing the work.

### 7.3 Questioning: a meta-model-style probe (the useful part)

**What you do:** their statement is a classic *generalisation* ("every project overruns") with an embedded *deletion* ("yours will too" — on what basis?). Use precision questions, without making it an interrogation:

- *"Which projects specifically, and what did they have in common?"* (challenge the generalisation with evidence)
- *"When you say overrun — by how much, and against which baseline: the original ask, or the first approved plan?"* (restore the deletion)
- *"What would a delivery plan look like that you'd trust?"* (turn the complaint into a criterion — this is also classic stakeholder elicitation)

**Why it works (honestly):** these questions work because they force *specificity*, which is what good requirements analysis and good negotiation both require — not because they "complete deep structure". The stakeholder's vague threat loses force when it has to be made concrete, and the conversation moves from verdict ("IT fails") to criteria ("what would count as acceptable") — a proven de-escalation and alignment move.

### 7.4 Reframing (the CBT-adjacent part)

**What you do:** after the specificity exchange, reframe the shared situation from threat to joint problem: *"So if we agree the failure mode is unclear requirements and late scope changes, the useful question isn't whether to trust my roadmap — it's what governance makes the roadmap trustworthy. Let's design that gate together: you get a checkpoint at month two, and if the plan is off, you're the first to know."*

**Why it works (honestly):** this is cognitive restructuring applied to a negotiation: the interpretation ("IT will lie to me again") is replaced with a testable alternative ("we will agree on checkpoints and visibility"). It works because changing the interpretation changes the emotional stance — the same mechanism CBT exploits — and because it offers a *concrete structural concession* (the checkpoint), which is real substance, not spin. If the stakeholder has genuine grounds for distrust, the reframe only works if the checkpoint is real — reframing without substance is manipulation.

### 7.5 The outcome: communication skills, not magic

The meeting ends with an agreed checkpoint gate and a date for the detailed plan. What actually happened, honestly:

- **The NLP-derived moves that worked** — pacing (acknowledgment), precision questioning, reframing — are the *evidence-based* 20%: active listening, specificity, perspective-taking. Every one of them is taught better in the mainstream communication and coaching literature ([communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md), [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md)).
- **The NLP moves that would NOT have helped** — reading their eye movements to detect "deception", classifying their representational system, anchoring them into a "resourceful state", or "hypnotically" pacing-and-leading them. These are unsupported, and in a live bank meeting they would have been a distraction at best and a conduct problem at worst.
- **The honest lesson:** the techniques in this example are communication skills — learnable, valuable, and entirely explainable without "neuro-linguistic programming". The NLP vocabulary is a convenient language for *talking about* communication; it is not a science of the mind (Section 8).

## 8. Summary and the Honest Verdict

### 8.1 The verdict

**NLP is an eclectic toolbox of communication techniques — most of them borrowed — wrapped in an unsupported cognitive model.** Fifty years of opportunity produced no evidence for the theory of mind (representational systems, submodalities, "programming", deep-structure linguistics), the signature claims (eye-accessing cues, one-session cures, strategy installation), or the flagship method (modelling). The peer-reviewed record — Sharpley 1984/1987, Witkowski 2010, Sturt et al. 2012, Wiseman et al. 2012 — is consistent and negative on NLP's own models. Academic psychology classifies NLP as pseudoscience, and it is absent from mainstream curricula.

**But the verdict is not "everything in NLP is worthless".** The useful parts — rapport, precise questioning, reframing, goal-directed coaching, imagery — are real skills with real evidence, because they are borrowed from traditions that did the work: the therapeutic-alliance research, cognitive therapy, goal-setting theory, and communication science. The honest rule of thumb:

> **Borrow the useful 20% (the communication skills), discard the unsupported 80% (the theory), and never pay a premium for the jargon.**

### 8.2 The final word

The most useful way to think about NLP in a professional context is as **a language for talking about communication — not a science of the mind.** Its vocabulary ("anchoring", "reframing", "rapport", "state") is genuinely handy shorthand for describing communication and emotional experience, which is why the terminology survives while the theory does not. The moment you treat that vocabulary as *description* ("let's reframe this objection", "we need more rapport in this room") you are speaking usefully; the moment you treat it as *mechanism* ("I anchored her resourceful state", "he's accessing his kinesthetic system") you have left the evidence behind and entered the pseudoscience.

For the manager, the architect, the coach: use the habits, skip the theory, verify the claims, and remember the one-sentence takeaway this guide has been building toward — **NLP is what happens when genuinely useful communication skills are dressed in the costume of neuroscience and sold at a premium. The skills are worth keeping. The costume is not.**

## 9. Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **NLP / Neuro-Linguistic Programming** | The 1970s Bandler & Grinder communication/self-development modality: "the study of the structure of subjective experience". NOT Natural Language Processing (the AI field). |
| **Bandler, Richard** | Co-creator of NLP (b. 1950); UCSC psychology student; primary author of the technique literature (swish, submodalities). |
| **Grinder, John** | Co-creator of NLP (b. 1940); UCSC linguistics professor; brought Chomskyan grammar into the meta-model. |
| **Pucelik, Frank** | UCSC student and early collaborator, "the third man" of NLP's founding; exact contribution documented mainly through practitioner accounts. |
| **Modeling (modelling)** | NLP's core method: studying an expert to extract and teach the "structure" of their excellence; no controlled demonstration of transfer exists. |
| **Meta-model** | Language-pattern system for precision questioning: deletions, distortions, generalisations; useful as a questioning discipline, false as linguistics. |
| **Milton model** | Erickson-derived "artful vagueness": indirect, presupposition-heavy hypnotic language; real rhetorical device, unsupported "unconscious bypass" mechanism. |
| **VAK / representational system** | Visual, Auditory, Kinesthetic sensory channels claimed to structure experience; empirically unsupported as a preference/detection system. |
| **Submodalities** | Sensory qualities of imagery (brightness, size, distance...); imagery affects emotion, but the specific NLP rules are unsupported. |
| **Anchoring** | Conditioning-like association of a stimulus with a state; real conditioning principle, oversold as "technology"; use only as a private self-regulation ritual. |
| **Rapport** | Mutual attention and responsiveness; the most supported NLP concept (therapeutic alliance, similarity research). |
| **Mirroring / pacing** | Matching a counterpart's behaviour/register; a modest, sincerity-dependent rapport aid; obvious mimicry backfires. |
| **Leading** | The claim that after pacing, the other person follows your behavioural changes; overstated mechanism, mundane version (modelling calm) works. |
| **Reframing** | Changing the meaning by changing the frame; in substance CBT cognitive restructuring — supported. |
| **Swish pattern** | Imagery technique (Bandler 1985) to replace unwanted responses; no controlled support. |
| **Fast phobia cure** | Visual-kinesthetic dissociation technique; one small positive RCT (Arroll 2017); exposure therapy is the evidenced standard. |
| **Parts integration** | Metaphorical negotiation between "parts" of the self; no mechanism, no controlled support. |
| **Timeline therapy** | Trademarked 1980s NLP offshoot (Tad James); hypnotic regression; no controlled support; regression memory risks. |
| **TOTE** | Test-Operate-Test-Exit loop; legitimate 1960 cognitive science (Miller, Galanter & Pribram); NLP "strategy" overlay unsupported. |
| **Erickson, Milton H.** | Hypnotherapist (1901–1980), modelled for the Milton model; influential clinician, indirect communication. |
| **Satir, Virginia** | Family therapist (1916–1988), modelled for the meta-model and rapport. |
| **Perls, Fritz** | Gestalt therapist (1893–1970), modelled for the meta-model; his session tapes launched Bandler's work. |
| **Robbins, Anthony** | Self-help author (b. 1960) who popularised "NLP-based" motivation ("Unlimited Power", 1986); anecdotes, not data. |
| **Pseudoscience** | A body of claims presented as scientific but lacking empirical support, falsifiability, or scientific process; the academic classification of NLP's core models. |
| **Sharpley, Christopher** | Author of the 1984/1987 *Journal of Counseling Psychology* reviews finding little empirical support for NLP. |
| **Einspruch & Forman** | Authors of the 1985 counter-critique (*JCP* 32(4): 589–596) arguing NLP was tested badly; the later better tests still failed. |
| **Witkowski, Tomasz** | Author of the 2010 *Polish Psychological Bulletin* review characterising NLP's research base as "pseudoscientific decoration". |
| **Wiseman, Richard** | Lead author of the 2012 PLOS ONE study falsifying NLP eye-accessing lie detection ("The Eyes Don't Have It"). |
| **Evidence-based** | Supported by systematic, replicable empirical research — the standard NLP's core models fail. |
| **Cognitive restructuring** | The CBT practice of identifying and revising automatic interpretations; what NLP "reframing" is in substance. |
| **CBT** | Cognitive-behavioural therapy; among the best-evidenced psychotherapies; home of restructuring and the therapeutic alliance research. |
| **Therapeutic alliance** | The practitioner-client relationship quality that predicts outcomes across therapies; the evidence base behind NLP's "rapport". |
| **Certification** | In NLP: unregulated Practitioner/Master/Trainer credentials from competing schools; no licensing or accreditation standard. |
