# The Staples of Leadership — What Survives Being Wrong About

*A synthesis of the leadership shelf rather than another entry on it. The argument is that a staple of leadership is not a belief about leadership but something that survives being wrong about — a small set of practices that hold when the quarter is bad, the context changes, the team is different, and the fashionable counter-argument has had its hearing. The guide sets out a four-pressure test, applies it to five candidate staples (integrity and trust, direction, judgement, the people decisions, accountability), applies it negatively to four myths (charisma, personality type, the listicle, the title), and then does the harder work: the staples under crisis pressure, at three organisational scopes, and against the actual state of the evidence — which is weaker than the genre admits and is stated here rather than smoothed. It closes with a worked example at Cymbal Bank (illustrative), the anti-patterns that imitate each staple, an audit of the advice industry, a claims audit, and an honest list of what could not be verified.*

## 1. The Overview, the Decoder, the Thesis and the Boundary

### 1.1 The One-Paragraph Version

There are hundreds of leadership books in this repository's neighbourhood and they cannot all be right, because they contradict each other on matters of fact: whether a leader should be directive or facilitative, whether charisma matters, whether leadership is a set of behaviours or a set of traits, whether it can be taught. The industry that produces them has a structural incentive to differentiate rather than to converge — a new framework sells, a confirmation of the old one does not (this is the subject of §13). So the useful question is not "what do the books say" but "what would still be true if the books were wrong." This guide answers that question with a filter and then applies it. The filter is four pressures — a bad quarter, a changed context, a different team, a fashionable counter-argument — and the answer is five staples: integrity and trust, direction, judgement, the people decisions, and accountability. Everything else on the shelf is either a mechanism for executing one of those five, a context-specific expression of one of them, or fashion. The guide's contribution is the filter and the negative space; the mechanisms belong to the guides that own them.

### 1.2 The Decoder — Six Terms the Guide Leans On

The whole argument depends on six words being used precisely. They are defined here once and not re-litigated later.

| Term | What this guide means by it | What it deliberately does not mean |
|---|---|---|
| **The staple** | A practice that survives being wrong about: it still produces its effect when performance pressure, context, personnel and fashion all change. It is a *do-thing*, not a *believe-thing*. | A value, a virtue, a personality trait, a competency heading on a performance form, or a proposition about leadership that one can hold while doing nothing. |
| **The leader** | The person who carries responsibility for direction and for the conditions other people work in, at whatever scope. Leadership is a *function exercised*, not a rank held. | A senior title, a charismatic person, or a moral compliment. The word is not a promotion of the word "manager." |
| **The manager** | The person accountable for converting intention into coordinated output through other people, within a system that already exists. Kotter's distinction — coping with complexity versus coping with change — is treated in §5, including why it is overstated. | A bureaucrat, a lesser leader, or the administrative residue left when "leadership" is taken out. |
| **Followership** | The willingness of others to be influenced: to accept direction, supply candid information, and expend discretionary effort. It is the *effect* the staples are trying to produce, and it is measurable only in behaviour, not in sentiment. | Passivity, obedience, or a demographic category. Followers are the people who decide, hourly, whether the leader's claims are credible. |
| **The context** | The specific conditions that shape which behaviours are available: industry, regulatory environment, culture, scale, maturity, technology, and the current state of the business. | An excuse. Context changes the *expression* of a staple; §9 argues it does not change the mechanism. |
| **The scope** | The unit over which the leader's decisions bind: a team, a function, an enterprise. Scope changes the *instrument* — direct, through other managers, or through systems — and §7 is about what stops working as scope rises. | Seniority, headcount, or budget. A team of six and an enterprise of six thousand can both be led badly in the same way and well by different means. |

Two further distinctions are used throughout without a table. **Position versus influence**: position gives you the right to decide; influence gives you the ability to be believed, and only the second survives a reorganisation. **Behaviour versus disposition**: what a person does repeatedly is observable; what a person "is" is inferred, and the inference is where the leadership literature has done most of its damage (§4, §10).

### 1.3 The Thesis

Stated plainly, and returned to at the end:

> **A leadership staple is not a belief about leadership; it is something that survives being wrong about.**

The test has four pressures, and each candidate staple in §3 is put through all four:

- **(a) Performance pressure.** A bad quarter, a missed target, a crisis, a regulatory finding. Does the practice hold, or is it the first thing traded away for speed?
- **(b) Changed context.** A different industry, culture, scale, operating model, remote or hybrid arrangement, or a new technology in the workflow. Does the practice still have a mechanism, or was it an artefact of the conditions it was observed in?
- **(c) A different team.** Different people, different maturity, different mix of competence and commitment. Does the practice depend on a particular cast, or does it work on strangers?
- **(d) A fashionable counter-argument.** The contrarian book, the conference keynote, the widely shared post that says the opposite. Does the practice have an answer, or only an assertion?

A practice that only works when things are going well was never a staple. It was a luxury good with a management vocabulary.

This is a deliberately deflationary thesis. It does not claim the five staples make a leader good. It claims that if you remove any one of them, the others become theatre within a measurable period — trust without direction becomes a pleasant place to work that achieves nothing; direction without trust becomes a memo; judgement without accountability becomes commentary; accountability without judgement becomes blame. The five are not a hierarchy of virtue. They are a set of load-bearing walls, and the guide's claim is structural rather than moral.

## 2. What Counts as a Staple — The Test

### 2.1 Why a Filter Is the Whole Guide

Anyone can produce a list of leadership qualities. The lists are already abundant, mutually inconsistent, and mostly unfalsifiable: "visionary," "authentic," "empathetic," "decisive," "humble," "bold." A list of that kind cannot be wrong, which is exactly why it is worthless. If "humble" and "bold" are both on the list, the list has no discriminating power — it describes whoever is being praised, after the fact.

A filter changes the genre. The question stops being "what do leaders have" and becomes "what would still work if the leader were wrong about it." That is a question with an answer, because it can be checked against cases: the leader who was wrong about the market, wrong about a hire, wrong about a technology, wrong about their own judgement — and whose practice nonetheless held. The filter is what separates this guide from a listicle, and it is the only original contribution the guide claims.

The filter also implies a discipline the genre avoids: **naming the failure signature**. For each staple in §3 there is a described way it looks when it is absent. That is what makes the claim checkable in your own organisation rather than merely agreeable.

### 2.2 The Four Pressures

Each pressure is a question with a failure condition. A candidate survives only if it passes all four; a candidate that passes three is a tactic, not a staple.

| Pressure | The question | What failure looks like | A worked failure (a real genre, not a real firm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **(a) Performance pressure** | When the quarter is bad, the incident is live, or the regulator is in the building, is this practice still used — or is it the first thing suspended "temporarily"? | The practice is explicitly deferred: "we'll get back to development conversations when we've stabilised." The deferral is never reversed. | The open-door culture that closes during a delivery crunch and does not reopen, because the crunch becomes the operating norm. |
| **(b) Changed context** | Does the practice have a mechanism that survives a change of industry, culture, scale, operating model or technology — or was it an artefact of the original conditions? | The practice depends on physical proximity, a single culture, a small team, or a technology that has since changed the work. | Management-by-walking-around, imported intact into a distributed team across four time zones, becomes a ritual of scheduled calls that nobody finds informative. |
| **(c) A different team** | Does it work on people the leader did not choose, does not like, and cannot motivate by presence — including the high performer who does not need coaching and the low performer who does? | The practice only works with a particular cast: the already-motivated, the co-located, the junior who accepts direction, the senior who happens to agree. | The coaching style that works beautifully with a willing D3 and collapses with a capable peer who regards coaching as condescension. |
| **(d) A fashionable counter-argument** | When the contrarian book of the moment says the opposite, does the practice have a mechanism to defend itself, or only a conviction? | The defence is status or sentiment: "that's just good leadership," "people need to be trusted." No mechanism, no failure condition, no boundary. | "Authenticity" as a defence of unedited self-expression, with no account of what happens to the team when the authentic self is careless. |

The pressures are not independent. (a) and (d) interact viciously: a crisis is when contrarian advice is most attractive, because it promises speed. A staple that cannot survive a crisis *and* a persuasive argument for abandoning it during a crisis is not a staple — it is a fair-weather practice with good public relations.

### 2.3 How a Candidate Fails

The test is applied by attempting to falsify, not to confirm. Take the candidate and ask what observation would show it is not load-bearing.

- **Fails on (a)** if you can name a plausible bad quarter in which the practice would rationally be suspended. "We'd love to do development plans, but we're in a trough" is a failure, not an exception.
- **Fails on (b)** if its mechanism is inseparable from a context: it requires co-location, or a single national culture, or a regulated monopoly, or a labour market where people cannot leave.
- **Fails on (c)** if it requires a particular kind of person to work — the self-motivated, the deferential, the already-skilled. A practice that only works on good teams is a reward for luck.
- **Fails on (d)** if the only available defence is that it feels right, that great leaders are said to have it, or that a study showed a correlation. Correlation is not a mechanism; §8 is about why that distinction matters more than the genre admits.

Two clarifications, because the test is easy to misuse:

**Surviving is not the same as being sufficient.** Trust survives a bad quarter; that does not mean trust alone delivers the quarter. All five staples can be present and the strategy can still be wrong. The claim is necessary, not sufficient, and the guide says so again in §3.6.

**Surviving is not the same as being universal in form.** The staple survives; its expression changes. Direction at a Singapore bank and direction at a two-person startup are the same staple with different instruments. §9 makes this argument for the regional context specifically.

### 2.4 What the Test Cannot Do

Three honest limits.

First, **the test is a filter on claims, not an experiment.** It cannot rank the five staples by importance, and it cannot tell you how much of each you need. It tells you what belongs in the category, not the dosage.

Second, **it is applied retrospectively and therefore subject to hindsight.** A practice that was present when things went well will look load-bearing; one that was present when things went badly will look decorative. The defence is to state the failure signature in advance, which is what §3 and §12 attempt, but the defence is imperfect.

Third, **it cannot settle empirical questions.** Whether trust causes performance, or performance causes trust, is not a question the filter answers; it is a question about evidence, and §8 gives the uncomfortable answer. The filter tells you what to keep while the evidence is being argued over. That is a weaker claim than the genre makes, and it is the strongest one the record supports.

## 3. The Staples Themselves

Five, argued rather than asserted. For each: what it is, the mechanism (why it survives the four pressures), where it is genuinely contested, and which shelf guide owns the execution. The order is not a ranking. It is roughly the order in which their absence becomes visible.

### 3.1 Integrity and Trust

**What it is.** Integrity here is narrower than the moral-philosophy sense and more operational: **what you say, what you decide, and what you reward are the same thing over time.** Trust is the resulting willingness of other people to act on your word without re-checking it — to bring you the bad news early, to accept a decision they disagree with, to expend effort they could withhold.

The operational content is unglamorous: promises kept at the level of granularity people actually notice (the follow-up you said you would send, the decision you said you would make by Friday); bad news delivered before it becomes someone else's discovery; the same standard applied to the high performer and the favourite; credit assigned accurately; and the specific, expensive act of telling someone a truth they do not want to hear. The trust literature's standard decomposition — ability, benevolence, integrity as the antecedents of trustworthiness, with trust itself a separate willingness to be vulnerable — is the usual frame, and it is treated in [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md) §8 and in the organisational-behaviour material. This guide does not re-derive it.

**Why it survives.** Under (a), trust is the thing that makes bad news travel at the speed of the problem rather than the speed of the reporting cycle; a leader without it learns about the incident after the client does. Under (b), the mechanism is informational and does not depend on industry, culture or technology — the question "will this person tell me the truth" is asked in every operating model ever devised, including one where the team is mediated by AI tooling. Under (c), it works on strangers because it is built from observed behaviour rather than affinity; the new joiner calibrates within weeks. Under (d), the fashionable counter-arguments — that trust is soft, that high standards require distance, that "radical transparency" replaces the need for judgement — do not actually displace it; they re-describe parts of it or over-correct into a different failure.

**Where it is contested.** Three real problems, stated rather than smoothed.

- **The measurement problem.** Trust is almost always measured by asking people whether they trust their manager, on the same instrument that asks whether they are satisfied. That is self-report measuring a perception of a relationship, and it is vulnerable to common-method variance — the tendency of one respondent answering one questionnaire to produce correlated answers for reasons that have nothing to do with the construct (§8). The literature can tell you that trust and various good outcomes co-vary. It struggles to tell you the direction, and it is weakest exactly where the genre is loudest: the claim that trust *causes* performance.
- **The naivety problem.** Trust is not unconditionally good. The literature on abusive supervision and on betrayal shows that high trust in a low-integrity leader produces greater exposure, not greater performance. Trust is a staple of the *follower's* condition as much as the leader's practice, and a leader who demands trust without earning it has inverted the mechanism.
- **The conflict-avoidance drift.** "Psychological safety," a genuinely useful construct owned by [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md), is routinely used to justify the absence of the hard conversation. Candour and comfort are not the same thing, and a leader can be trusted and still be avoided because they are unpredictable. The staple is *trustworthiness*, not *niceness*.

### 3.2 Direction — The Ability to Set It

**What it is.** Not a vision statement. Direction is the answer to three questions that a team can act on without asking: **what are we trying to achieve, what are we deliberately not doing, and how will we know if we are wrong?** The third question is what separates direction from slogan. A direction without a falsifier is a mood.

The mechanism is coordination cost. A team with direction can make local decisions without escalating, because the trade-offs are already settled; a team without it escalates everything or, worse, decides locally against unstated criteria and produces drift. This is the same logic Grove applies to the manager's output and the limiting step in [high_output_management_guide.md](high_output_management_guide.md) §3–§4, and the same logic behind the first-ninety-days direction work in [../technology/first_time_manager_guide.md](../technology/first_time_manager_guide.md).

**Why it survives.** Under (a), direction is what prevents a bad quarter from becoming five uncoordinated reactions; the pressure to abandon it is high, and the cost of doing so is a team that optimises for the visible week. Under (b), it survives because the mechanism is decision-theoretic rather than contextual — every operating model needs a criterion for local choice. Under (c), it works on new people precisely because it is external to them: a good direction lets a stranger make a correct decision on their second day. Under (d), the strongest counter-argument is real and is treated below.

**Where it is contested.** This is the staple with the best counter-argument against it.

- **Direction versus emergent strategy.** Mintzberg's critique of the design school and of strategic planning is the serious version of the objection: much of what becomes strategy is pattern that emerges from action, not intention that precedes it, and a leader who insists on top-down direction can foreclose the adaptation that actually produces the outcome. ⚠-knowledge on the exact texts; the argument is standard. The honest resolution is not that direction doesn't matter but that it must be **revisable** — which is why "how will we know if we are wrong" is part of the definition rather than a footnote.
- **Direction versus discovery.** In genuinely novel work — a new product category, a first-of-kind platform migration — the leader often cannot know the answer in advance, and pretending to is a form of dishonesty that the team detects. The staple in that setting is not the answer but the *criterion* and the *review cadence*.
- **The false-precision problem.** A direction that is too specific becomes a target, and targets get gamed; a direction that is too abstract cannot adjudicate a trade-off. There is no formula for the level of abstraction, and anyone who offers one is selling something (§13).

### 3.3 Judgement and Decision Quality

**What it is.** The capacity to make a decision of acceptable quality with incomplete information, under time pressure, in the presence of interests that distort the inputs — and to distinguish a good decision from a good outcome. The staple is not "decisiveness." Decisiveness is a speed property; judgement is a quality property, and the two are routinely confused (§12).

The operational content: framing the actual question rather than the presented one; identifying who has the information and who has the authority (the decision-rights machinery owned by [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md) §6); naming the assumptions that would change the answer; choosing a decision rule appropriate to reversibility; and — the hardest part — running a review that separates process from result.

**Why it survives.** Under (a), it is the staple most needed and most degraded; §6 argues it degrades first. Under (b), the mechanism is cognitive and structural, not contextual: every context presents ambiguous information and interested parties. Under (c), it is exercised on strangers constantly, because decisions are made about people and with people the leader did not choose. Under (d), the counter-arguments — "bias training doesn't work," "trust your gut," "move fast" — are arguments about *how*, not *whether*; the staple is the discipline of deciding deliberately, and none of those arguments supplies an alternative to it.

**Where it is contested.** Badly, and the guide should be direct about it.

- **Outcome bias.** The research tradition on outcome bias (Baron and Hershey's 1988 experiments are the standard citation) shows that people rate the quality of a decision by its result, even when the result was determined by factors outside the decision-maker's control. ⚠-knowledge on the exact study this pass. This means that in practice, "good judgement" is often *hindsight relabelled* — the leader who was lucky is described as having judgement, and the leader who was unlucky is described as having been reckless. Any claim that a leader has demonstrable judgement needs a decision log and a counterfactual, which almost no organisation keeps.
- **Judgement versus luck in the selection literature.** Firms select for judgement using instruments whose predictive validity is modest and whose interview component is among the weaker ones — the selection meta-analytic literature (Schmidt and Hunter's 1998 review is the canonical reference) is the standard citation, and its own numbers are dated and contested. ⚠-knowledge. The implication is uncomfortable: organisations claim to select for judgement while using the tool with the weakest evidence.
- **The speed conflation.** In banking, in incident response, and in most large organisations, the reward system pays for the decision that was made quickly and visibly, not the one that was made well. Judgement is therefore systematically under-rewarded relative to decisiveness, which is a structural reason the staple degrades even when individuals hold it.

### 3.4 The People Decisions

**What it is.** Three decisions, not a "talent strategy": **who to bring in, who to develop, and who to move out.** They are grouped because they share a property — they are irreversible at the relevant timescale, they compound, and they are the decisions leaders most reliably defer.

- **Selection.** The decision to hire, promote, or place someone in a role. The staple is the willingness to hold a bar and to accept the cost of an empty seat, against the pressure to fill it.
- **Development.** The decision to invest in a specific person's capability, which requires a specific judgement about what they cannot yet do rather than a generic "development plan."
- **The hard conversation.** The decision to tell someone, clearly and early, that the current performance is not acceptable — including the version that ends in exit. This is the single most deferred act in management, and the deferral is usually dressed as kindness.

**Why it survives.** Under (a), these are the decisions most likely to be suspended in a downturn — hiring freezes are rational, but development cuts and performance avoidance are not — and the organisations that keep the bar through the trough are the ones that do not have to rebuild afterwards. Under (b), the mechanism is invariant: every operating model, including one heavily mediated by AI, still requires a human judgement about whether a person can do a job and whether they are being told the truth about it (the contemporary version is owned by [../technology/people_management_in_ai_age_guide.md](../technology/people_management_in_ai_age_guide.md)). Under (c), it *is* the mechanism by which the team changes; it cannot fail on (c) by definition. Under (d), the counter-arguments — "hire for attitude," "everyone is coachable," "the team is a family" — are empirical claims that the evidence does not support cleanly, and the guide flags them rather than endorsing them.

**Where it is contested.** Most contested staple of the five.

- **Interview validity is weak.** The structured-interview literature supports structure over unstructured conversation, but the absolute predictive validity of interviews as a class is modest, and the unstructured interview — which is what most organisations actually run — is weaker still. ⚠-knowledge, standard in the selection literature. A leader who believes they can "see" a candidate is relying on an instrument with poor evidence, and the confident ones are not more accurate, only more convincing.
- **The development assumption.** "Everyone is coachable" is an assumption the shelf's guides mostly share and the disposition literature does not support at the tails (§10). Development works; it works less than the industry implies, and it works least on the people about whom the decision matters most.
- **The hard conversation versus due process.** In a regulated bank, the hard conversation is entangled with performance-management procedure, HR policy, and in some jurisdictions employment law. The staple is the *candour*, not a disregard for process; a leader who mistakes bluntness for candour produces a tribunal, not a turnaround.

### 3.5 Accountability — Owning the Outcome

**What it is.** The leader's unconditional ownership of the result, including the parts that were caused by other people, by the system, or by bad luck. Operationally it has three parts: **naming the outcome in advance** (a commitment, not a hope), **reporting it honestly when it misses**, and **absorbing the external pressure rather than transmitting it downward unmodified**.

The third part is the one that distinguishes accountability from its imitation. Anyone can say "the buck stops here" after a success. The staple is visible in the miss: the leader who tells their own manager the unflattering version first, and who does not convert their own anxiety into a demand that the team work the weekend.

**Why it survives.** Under (a), it is definitionally the staple of the bad quarter — and §6 argues it is the one most often *reassigned* under threat. Under (b), it is invariant: every governance model, including a highly automated one, needs a named human answerable for the outcome. Under (c), it is what makes a team willing to take risks with a new leader: they will not be sacrificed for a failure the leader owns. Under (d), the counter-argument — "accountability is a synonym for blame" — is a real abuse, and the answer is definitional: accountability is ownership of the outcome; blame is the assignment of fault to a person, usually after the fact, usually to protect someone else.

**Where it is contested.**

- **Accountability versus blame culture.** The distinction above is easy to state and hard to sustain under pressure, because blame is cheaper: it produces a visible act, a named culprit, and a closure. Accountability produces a review, a change to the system, and an uncomfortable admission that the leader's own decision contributed. Organisations mostly choose blame (§12).
- **The attribution problem.** §8's endogeneity point applies with full force here: leaders are placed in situations, and situations produce outcomes. The leader who "owns" a result they did not cause is making a claim that is morally admirable and causally dubious. The staple is the *willingness to be answerable*, not the claim of omnipotence.
- **Accountability without authority.** The classic organisational pathology: accountability delegated downward while decision rights are retained upward. It is a design failure, not a character failure, and it is treated in [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md) §6. A leader who is accountable without authority is being set up, and the staple requires them to say so rather than absorb it silently.

### 3.6 The Five Together

The five are not independent, and the guide does not claim they are.

| Staple | Absent, the others become | Its characteristic failure signature |
|---|---|---|
| Integrity and trust | A pleasant team that withholds bad news until it is expensive | You learn about problems from someone other than the person who owns them |
| Direction | Effort without coordination; local optimisation against unstated criteria | Every trade-off escalates; two teams make opposite decisions with equal confidence |
| Judgement | Confident motion in the wrong direction, retold as decisiveness | Decisions are reviewed by outcome; the decision log, if one exists, is never read |
| The people decisions | A team that is a residue of avoided conversations | The underperformer is still there, and everyone has adapted around them |
| Accountability | Blame, and a system that learns nothing | The post-mortem names a person, not a mechanism |

Two properties are worth naming because they explain the order of §6. **Trust is the slowest to build and the fastest to spend.** **Accountability is the cheapest to declare and the most expensive to hold.** The other three sit between those poles, and their degradation follows from the interaction: a leader who reassigns accountability in a crisis will, within two or three cycles, find that nobody brings them the information they need to exercise judgement — which is why the staples fail in a sequence rather than at random.

## 4. What Is Not a Staple — The Myths, Named

The negative space is where this guide earns its keep. Four candidates are routinely presented as the core of leadership and fail the test in §2 for different reasons. Each is treated fairly: none is worthless, and each gets right something the staples alone do not capture.

### 4.1 Charisma

**The claim.** Leadership is a personal magnetic quality; the leader who has it can move people by presence. This is the oldest and most persistent story in the genre, and it is the one most resistant to evidence.

**Why it fails the test.** Two reasons, one sociological and one empirical.

The sociological reason is Weber's. Charismatic authority, in his tripartite classification, rests on the attribution of exceptional qualities to a person, and it is inherently unstable because it is attached to a person rather than an office. Weber's answer to the instability is **routinisation**: the charisma must be converted into tradition or into rational-legal rules, or the organisation does not survive the founder. ✅ The routinisation problem is documented in the reference literature on charismatic authority. The implication for the staple question is direct: a property that must be converted into something else in order to persist is, by definition, not the thing that persists. The thing that persists is the converted form — the rules, the decision rights, the successor — which is to say, institutional staples rather than personal ones.

The empirical reason is that the charisma–performance link is weak and badly measured. The construct is often defined by its outcomes ("charismatic leaders are effective"), which the same reference literature flags as circular: if effectiveness is the definition, the claim that charisma produces effectiveness cannot be refuted. ✅ on the circularity point. Where charisma is measured independently, it is usually measured by follower ratings of the leader, on the same instrument that measures satisfaction and perceived effectiveness — which is common-method variance with a bow on it (§8). ⚠-knowledge on the specific meta-analytic estimates; this guide prints none.

**What it gets right.** Presence is real and it is not nothing. A leader who can hold a room, tell the story so that it lands, and make an uncertain situation feel navigable is doing something that a leader who cannot do those things is not. The error is treating presence as the *substitute* for direction, judgement and accountability rather than as a *delivery mechanism* for them. Charisma is an accelerant. An accelerant is not fuel.

### 4.2 Personality Type

**The claim.** Leaders fall into types; know your type (and your team's) and you know how to lead and whom to hire.

**Why it fails the test.** The instrument most associated with the claim, the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator, has a poor psychometric standing that is not a matter of academic taste. ✅ The reference literature records that the MBTI exhibits poor validity and poor reliability, that it imposes dichotomies on dimensions that are not independent, that it is not comprehensive, and that much of the validity research has been produced by an organisation run by the instrument's own foundation and published in its own journal — a conflict of interest. ✅ It is not considered useful in psychological practice or study because it lacks predictive power. ✅ The Barnum effect explains why respondents nonetheless find the results uncannily accurate. ✅

The deeper problem for the staple question is category confusion. A type is a *description of a person*, not a *practice*. Even if the instrument were psychometrically sound, "I am an INTJ" would not tell you what to do on Monday when a delivery slips and a good engineer is burning out. Types describe; staples prescribe. A description cannot survive a bad quarter because it was never doing any work.

**What it gets right.** Two things. First, the vocabulary of difference is genuinely useful for teams: people do differ in how they process information and where they draw energy, and a shared language for that difference — even an imprecise one — can reduce friction and make room for the quiet member of the team. Second, the personality literature that *does* hold up — the Big Five — finds real, modest associations between some traits and leadership emergence and effectiveness. ⚠-knowledge. The associations are modest, they are about emergence as much as effectiveness, and they say nothing about what a leader should do next. A modest correlation is not a staple either.

### 4.3 The Listicle — The Habits Genre

**The claim.** Leadership consists of N habits, principles, or laws, where N is between three and twenty-one. The genre is the commercial backbone of the category: the airport book, the keynote, the workshop deck.

**Why it fails the test.** It fails (a) and (d) simultaneously. Under performance pressure, a list of ten principles provides no priority: when the quarter is bad you cannot do all ten, and the list does not tell you which two to keep. Under a fashionable counter-argument, the list has no defence because it has no mechanism — the items are asserted, not derived, so a competing list of a different length is exactly as well-supported. The listicle is unfalsifiable by construction: it is a set of things that are generally agreeable, and agreeable claims cannot be wrong.

There is a second, subtler failure. The listicle converts a practice into an *identity marker*. The reader who has internalised the seven habits feels like a leader without having changed what they do on a Tuesday. This is the mechanism behind the meta anti-pattern in §12: the staples themselves can be performed as a checklist, and the listicle genre is where that habit is learned.

**What it gets right.** The best of the genre — and some of it is good — functions as a **memory aid**, not a theory. A short list of prompts is a useful thing to review before a difficult conversation. The error is epistemic, not literary: the list is a heuristic that has been marketed as a finding.

### 4.4 "Leadership Is a Title"

**The claim.** Leaders are the people with the title; leadership happens in the executive suite; the rest of us are followers until promoted.

**Why it fails the test.** It fails (b) and (c) outright. Under a changed context — a matrix, a distributed team, a professional organisation of experts who cannot be told what to do — the title confers far less than the org chart implies, because the work requires influence without authority. That mechanism is owned by [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md) and [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md), and this guide does not re-derive it. Under a different team, the title is actively misleading: a newly appointed manager with a title and no credibility has less influence than a senior engineer without one, and everyone in the room knows it.

Kotter's own framing is the cleanest refutation available, and it is verified: leadership "is not the province of a chosen few," and it "isn't mystical and mysterious." ✅ That sentence was written by the person most associated with the manager/leader distinction, which matters for §5: the distinction was never meant to be a hierarchy of persons.

**What it gets right.** The title is not nothing. It confers legitimate authority, control of resources, and the right to decide — and those are real instruments (§7 argues they are the instruments of enterprise scope). The error is mistaking the instrument for the function. Position is a starting balance, not a standing balance; that formulation belongs to [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md), and this guide adopts it rather than re-arguing it.

### 4.5 The Four Myths in One Table

| Myth | The pressure it fails first | Why | What it gets right |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charisma | (b) changed context, and (a) pressure | Routinises into rules or dies with the person; measured circularly | Presence is a real delivery mechanism for direction and reassurance |
| Personality type | (c) a different team | A description of a person cannot prescribe an act; instrument is psychometrically weak | A shared vocabulary for difference reduces team friction |
| The listicle | (a) and (d) | No priority under pressure; no mechanism under challenge | A useful memory aid before a hard conversation |
| "Leadership is a title" | (b) and (c) | Influence without authority is the normal case, not the exception | Position supplies real instruments — resources, decision rights, standing |

The pattern is consistent: each myth is a *true observation promoted to a false explanation*. Presence matters; therefore leadership is presence. Types describe; therefore types explain. Titles confer authority; therefore leadership is title. The staples are what remains when the promotion is reversed.

## 5. The Manager and the Leader

### 5.1 The Distinction, as Kotter Actually Made It

Kotter's argument, in *What Leaders Really Do*, is narrower and better than its popular version. His claim is that leadership and management are **two different systems for dealing with two different problems**: management copes with complexity, and leadership copes with change. Neither is a higher calling; neither substitutes for the other. The verified text is unusually blunt about this: leadership "isn't mystical and mysterious," has "nothing to do with having 'charisma' or other exotic personality traits," is "not the province of a chosen few," and — the sentence the industry ignores — "Nor is leadership necessarily better than management or a replacement for it." ✅

That is a functional distinction, not a moral one. Management's instruments are planning, budgeting, organising, staffing, controlling and problem-solving; they produce reliability and predictability at scale. Leadership's instruments are direction-setting, alignment of people, and motivation; they produce movement under conditions where the plan is wrong. An organisation that has only management becomes rigid and eventually irrelevant. An organisation that has only leadership becomes exciting and eventually chaotic, because nobody is running the machine. Kotter's point is that modern organisations are systematically over-managed and under-led — an argument about a *deficit*, not about a hierarchy of virtue.

### 5.2 Why the Distinction Is Overstated

Three corrections, each of which matters for the staples.

**The same person does both, usually in the same hour.** The distinction is analytical, not occupational. A manager who notices that the plan is built on a false assumption and says so is leading; a leader who runs a disciplined operating review is managing. The verbs are separable; the person is not. Treating the distinction as a caste system produces two failure modes at once: managers who believe direction is someone else's job, and leaders who believe operating detail is beneath them.

**"Leader" has become a status inflation.** Because leadership is the more flattering word, every management role has been relabelled. The result is that "leader" now often means "senior" and "manager" often means "junior", which destroys the analytical value of both. This guide uses the words functionally — leader for the direction-and-conditions function, manager for the conversion-of-intent-into-output function — and accepts that one person occupies both.

**The dichotomy has a serious scholarly critique.** Mintzberg's objection, in essence, is that the separation is empirically thin: managerial work as actually observed is a dense, fragmented mix of interpersonal, informational and decisional activity, and the leadership/management split imports a normative preference into a descriptive claim. ⚠-knowledge on the specific texts. The critique does not destroy Kotter's distinction — the two systems really are different — but it destroys the implication that they are performed by different kinds of people.

### 5.3 The Five Staples in Each Role

The useful question is not "am I a manager or a leader" but "what does each staple look like in each of my two jobs." The table is the practical core of this section.

| Staple | As the **manager** exercises it | As the **leader** exercises it |
|---|---|---|
| **Integrity and trust** | Consistency of process: the review happens when it was scheduled, the criteria are the ones published, the exception is explained. Trust is built by predictability. | Consistency of account: the story about why we are doing this does not change with the audience. Trust is built by coherence between what is said upward and what is said downward. |
| **Direction** | Translating direction into targets, capacity, sequencing and a definition of done that a team can execute against. | Choosing the direction and, harder, choosing what to stop. Naming the criterion by which the team will know the direction is wrong. |
| **Judgement** | Decision quality inside the operating system: which escalation to resolve, which risk to accept, which deadline to trade. | Decision quality at the boundary: which business to be in, which capability to build, which person to bet a role on. |
| **The people decisions** | Staffing the work: assignment, development, the performance conversation, the exit when it is required. | Designing the conditions in which those decisions are made well: the bar, the calibration, the succession bench, the culture that makes candour survivable. |
| **Accountability** | Owning the team's output against the commitment, and not transmitting pressure downward unmodified. | Owning the enterprise's result and the system that produced it — including the parts the leader did not personally cause. |

The table makes the real point of this section: **the staples do not change between the roles; their instrument does.** A manager who cannot hold the hard conversation and a leader who cannot choose what to stop are failing the same staple at different scopes. That is why §7 is about scope rather than about title.

For the transition itself — the identity shift, the first ninety days, the hiring and direction work — the owning guide is [../technology/first_time_manager_guide.md](../technology/first_time_manager_guide.md). This guide does not restate it, and it deliberately does not add another transition model to the shelf.

## 6. The Staples Under Pressure

### 6.1 The Claim: They Fail in an Order

Under a crisis, the staples do not degrade together. They degrade in a sequence, and the sequence is predictable because it follows from what each staple costs to maintain.

The order argued here:

1. **Judgement degrades first.** Time compression, information overload, and the collapse of the review cycle attack it directly.
2. **Trust is spent fastest and rebuilt slowest.** It is not degraded by the crisis itself but by what the leader does in the first days of it.
3. **Accountability is reassigned under threat.** It is the staple most likely to be quietly moved to someone with less power.
4. **Direction survives longest.** It is cheap to restate and expensive to change, so it persists — sometimes past the point where it should.
5. **The people decisions are deferred entirely.** Not abandoned; deferred, which is the same thing with better paperwork.

This is an argument, not a finding. It is offered as a hypothesis with a mechanism for each step, and the reader should test it against their own worst quarter rather than accept it.

### 6.2 Judgement First — The Mechanism

Judgement is the staple with the tightest coupling to conditions. It requires three inputs that a crisis removes simultaneously: **time** to frame the question, **information** that has been checked, and **a review** that separates process from outcome. Under pressure all three are rationed, and the rationing is invisible because the decision still gets made — faster, with more confidence, on worse inputs.

Three specific degradations:

- **Framing collapse.** The presented question is accepted as the question. "Should we delay the migration?" is answered, when the real question was "should we migrate at all this quarter?" The cost of reframing feels unaffordable precisely when it is highest-value.
- **Information narrowing.** The leader's sources shrink to the people who are in the room and the dashboard that is already built. The person who knows the platform is fragile is two levels down and not invited, and the invitation is not sent because there is no time to brief a new attendee.
- **Outcome-based review.** The post-incident review asks "who decided this" rather than "what would have made this decision good at the time." That single substitution converts the review into a blame exercise and guarantees that the next decision is made defensively (§12).

None of this is a character failure. It is what happens to a cognitive process under load, and it is why the staple is a *practice* — a standing decision log, a named reviewer, a pre-committed set of tripwires — rather than a quality of mind. The practices are what survive when the mind is tired.

### 6.3 Trust Second — Spent in Days, Rebuilt in Quarters

Trust is unusual among the five: a crisis does not erode it gradually. It is spent in discrete acts, and the acts are cheap to perform and expensive to undo.

| The act under pressure | What it costs | What it buys |
|---|---|---|
| Withholding bad news until it is confirmed | A few hours of apparent calm | The discovery, later, that you knew — the single most durable trust loss |
| Sending the pressure downward unmodified ("the exec wants this by Friday") | Nothing, in the moment | A team that learns to protect itself from you rather than inform you |
| Making an exception for the high performer | A small fairness cost | Confirmation that the standard is a function of favour |
| Promising a fix you cannot deliver | Relief now | A promise that will be visibly broken within the quarter |
| Cancelling the skip-level, the one-to-one, the review | An hour | The signal that the relationship is conditional on performance |

The asymmetry is the point. Trust is built through many small, consistent acts over a long period and lost through one visible inconsistency at a moment when everyone is watching. That is why the guide calls it the slowest to build and the fastest to spend. It is also why crisis is the highest-leverage moment for it: the leader's behaviour in the first seventy-two hours is the strongest available signal of what the relationship actually is, because the cost of the signal has just gone up.

The trust literature's decomposition — ability, benevolence and integrity as antecedents, trust as a separate willingness to be vulnerable — is treated in [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md) §8 and is not re-derived here. What matters for this section is only the asymmetry, which the decomposition explains: a single integrity violation damages the antecedent that is hardest to rebuild.

### 6.4 Accountability Third — The One That Gets Moved

Accountability is the staple most likely to be **reassigned** rather than abandoned, and the reassignment is usually dressed as structure.

The pattern: something goes wrong; the leader cannot afford to own it upward, so a review is commissioned; the review's terms of reference locate the failure in an execution team; a named individual is "accountable for the remediation"; the leader is accountable for "oversight," which is unfalsifiable. The staple has not been abandoned — the word is still used, prominently — but it has been moved to someone with less power and more exposure.

This is the failure mode that produces the others. A leader who reassigns accountability in a crisis will find, within two or three cycles, that:
- bad news arrives later, because the people who would have brought it are now managing their exposure (§6.3);
- judgement degrades faster, because the information that would have corrected it is being withheld (§6.2);
- and the people decisions get harder, because the team has learned that the cost of a mistake is personal.

Accountability is therefore the *pivot* staple: it is not the first to degrade, but its degradation accelerates the others. The mechanism is incentive, and it is the clearest example of the guide's general claim that the staples are structural rather than moral.

### 6.5 Direction Fourth — Cheap to Restate, Expensive to Change

Direction survives longest for a slightly uncomfortable reason: restating it is nearly free. A leader under pressure can repeat the same three sentences in every meeting at no cost, and the repetition looks like conviction. Direction is expensive to *change* — it invalidates work in flight, admits that the previous judgement was wrong, and requires the leader to absorb the cost of the reversal — so the pressure runs toward restatement rather than revision.

That is the survival mechanism, and it is also the trap. Direction that is only restated becomes a slogan: it is repeated with increasing intensity and decreasing application, and the team learns to recite it while deciding by other criteria. The test of whether direction is alive is not whether it is repeated but whether it has been **used to kill something** in the last quarter. A direction that has never caused a project to be stopped is a decoration.

The corollary: direction can survive too long. §3.2's definition includes "how will we know if we are wrong," and a crisis is exactly when that question should be answered. A leader who restates the direction through evidence that it is failing has not preserved the staple; they have preserved its form while losing its function. That is the failure mode §6.6 names.

### 6.6 Performed Versus Held — The Ritual Failure

Every staple has a ritual twin that looks identical from a distance. The ritual is what gets performed when the staple is absent, and it is more dangerous than an obvious absence because it satisfies the observer.

| Staple | Held (the practice) | Performed (the ritual) |
|---|---|---|
| Integrity and trust | Bad news travels early and upward without penalty | "Transparency" is a value on a slide, and the last three incidents were announced after resolution |
| Direction | The direction has killed a project this quarter | The same three sentences are repeated in every all-hands, and no trade-off has ever been adjudicated by them |
| Judgement | Decisions are logged with assumptions and reviewed against process | Decisions are made fast and confidently, and the review names whoever was closest |
| The people decisions | A named person was told, clearly, that the current performance is not acceptable | A "development plan" has been open for four quarters and everyone knows it is a filing exercise |
| Accountability | The leader tells their own manager the unflattering version first | "The buck stops with me" is said in the meeting, and the remediation owner is someone two levels down |

The ritual is detectable by a single question: **what did this cost the leader?** A held staple has a visible cost — a project stopped, a relationship strained, a bonus foregone, an admission made without qualification. A performed staple costs nothing, which is precisely why it is performed.

This is also the bridge to §8. The reason the ritual is so common is that the evidence base cannot easily tell the difference: both the held and the performed version produce agreeable survey responses from people who have been asked whether their leader is trustworthy. The measurement problem and the ritual problem are the same problem seen from two ends.

## 7. The Staples at Different Scopes

### 7.1 The Same Five, Three Instruments

Scope does not change which staples matter. It changes the instrument through which they are exercised, and — more importantly — it changes what *stops working*.

| Scope | How the staples are exercised | The binding constraint |
|---|---|---|
| **Team** (roughly five to fifteen people) | Directly, daily, in conversation. Trust is built by observed behaviour; direction is discussed; judgement is visible in the room; people decisions are made about people the leader knows well. | The leader's own attention. Everything is personal and everything is sequential. |
| **Function** (several teams, several managers) | Through other managers, and through the design of the operating rhythm. The leader's staples are exercised by *what they require of their managers* and by what they model in front of them. | The quality of the managers in the middle. The leader's intent is filtered through them, and the filter is the constraint. |
| **Enterprise** (multiple functions, a board, external accountability) | Through systems: decision rights, planning and review cycles, incentive design, succession, governance. Personal behaviour still matters, but it is no longer the mechanism. | The design of the system itself, and the leader's ability to change it. |

The move from team to function is the one most often botched, because the instrument changes and the habit does not. A team-scope leader who becomes a function-scope leader typically tries to exercise the staples directly, at a distance, which produces two pathologies at once: over-involvement in individual decisions (the leader becomes the bottleneck) and under-investment in the managers who are supposed to be the instrument.

### 7.2 Function Scope — The Leverage Argument

At function scope the leader's output is not their own work. It is the output of the teams, amplified or damped by the decisions they make about where to intervene. This is Grove's argument, and it is owned by [high_output_management_guide.md](high_output_management_guide.md) §5: the manager's output is the output of their organisation plus the output of the neighbouring organisations they influence, and managerial leverage is the ratio of output to the manager's own activity. This guide does not re-derive the production model or the limiting-step analysis; it draws one consequence.

The consequence is that at function scope **the staples are exercised by proxy, and the proxy is imperfect**. The leader cannot build trust with forty people directly; they build it by how they treat the eight managers, who are watched by their teams for evidence of what is actually acceptable. Trust at this scope is therefore *modelled*, not *transacted*. Direction is not a conversation but a set of commitments that the managers are held to, and which they can translate. Judgement is exercised mostly in the choice of *which* decisions to take and which to leave, and in the design of the review that catches the ones that were left badly. Accountability is exercised by what happens to a manager who brings bad news.

The failure mode at this scope is a leader who believes the staples scale by communication. They do not. A function-wide email about integrity changes nothing; a single visible decision about a popular manager who fudged a number changes everything.

### 7.3 Enterprise Scope — Integrity Is Necessary, Not Sufficient

At enterprise scope, the leader's personal integrity remains necessary — a chief executive whose word is unreliable poisons every system beneath them — but it is radically insufficient, because the mechanisms through which the staples operate are now structural.

- **Direction** becomes strategy and capital allocation: what gets funded, what gets stopped, what the planning cycle rewards. A direction that is not reflected in the allocation is a speech.
- **Judgement** becomes the design of decision rights and the quality of the forums where decisions are made. This is the territory of [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md) §6.
- **The people decisions** become succession, calibration and the bar for senior hiring. The single most consequential people decision an enterprise leader makes is who replaces them, and it is usually made too late.
- **Accountability** becomes governance: what the board is told, when, and in what form. A leader who owns outcomes privately but reports them selectively has not exercised the staple.
- **Integrity and trust** become the system's predictability: whether the rules apply to the powerful, whether dissent survives, whether the bad quarter is reported before it is unavoidable.

The enterprise leader's characteristic error is the mirror of the function leader's. The function leader over-personalises; the enterprise leader over-systematises, and forgets that systems are read by people as evidence of intent. A governance framework that announces accountability while protecting a high performer is not a governance framework; it is a communication.

### 7.4 What Does Not Scale

Three things, stated plainly, because the genre routinely implies otherwise.

**Personal presence does not scale.** A leader can be in one room. Beyond a certain size, presence becomes symbol — the walk-round, the town hall, the video — and symbol is a weak instrument that decays with repetition. This is why charisma fails (b) so badly: it is the least scalable property in the set.

**Direct trust does not scale.** Trust at enterprise scope is *institutional*: trust in the process, in the escalation route, in the review. A leader who tries to be personally trusted by everyone ends up trusted by nobody in particular and dependent on a small circle, which is how founder-led organisations fail their second generation.

**Individual judgement bandwidth does not scale.** One person can hold perhaps a handful of complex judgements at once; an enterprise generates hundreds. The staple therefore has to be distributed — which means the enterprise leader's real judgement question is *who else is authorised to judge, and how would I know if they were wrong*. That is a design question, and it is the one most often answered by hiring people who think like the leader, which reproduces the bottleneck rather than relieving it.

## 8. The Evidence Question

This is the guide's sharpest section, and its conclusion is uncomfortable: **the empirical case for the staples is weaker than the genre claims, and the staples are defended here on different grounds.** Both halves of that sentence matter. A guide that only said the first half would be a debunking; a guide that only said the second would be the genre.

### 8.1 What the Research Base Actually Looks Like

**(a) The instrumentation problem.** Much of the leadership canon rests on cross-sectional, self-report survey designs. A typical study asks subordinates to rate their leader on a set of behaviours and to rate their own satisfaction, commitment or perception of effectiveness, at a single point in time. Three consequences follow, and none of them is controversial among methodologists:

- **Common-method variance.** When all variables come from one respondent on one instrument, the correlations are inflated by the respondent's general disposition and by consistency pressures, independent of the constructs. ⚠-knowledge on the technical treatment; this is standard psychometric criticism, not a fringe view.
- **Cross-sectional design.** A single measurement cannot distinguish "trust produces performance" from "a performing team produces trust." The causal arrow is usually asserted, occasionally modelled, and rarely identified.
- **Leader rating as the measure of leadership.** If leadership is measured by how subordinates rate the leader, and the outcome is also rated by the same subordinates, the finding is close to tautological.

This does not make the literature worthless. It makes it a literature about *perceptions of leadership*, which is a legitimate object of study and a different object from *leadership*. Almost every claim in the genre that begins "research shows that leaders who..." is a claim about correlated perceptions.

**(b) The romance-of-leadership critique.** Meindl, Ehrlich and Dukerich's 1985 paper in *Administrative Science Quarterly* — "The Romance of Leadership," ✅ bibliographic record confirmed — argues that observers have a systematic tendency to over-attribute organisational outcomes to leaders, because leadership is a culturally available explanation that makes complex, systemic events narratable. The consequence is a bias that runs through the whole field, including its research designs: if observers over-attribute outcomes to leaders, then asking observers to rate leaders and correlating that with outcomes will recover the bias as a finding. ⚠-knowledge on the precise experimental details; the thesis is the paper's title and is standard in the field.

For this guide the critique is decisive at the level of *attribution*. It does not show that leaders do not matter. It shows that the evidence cannot easily tell us how much they matter, and that the genre's confident attributions are partly an artefact of how humans explain organisations.

**(c) The construct-stretch critique.** van Knippenberg and Sitkin's 2013 *Academy of Management Annals* paper carries the title "A Critical Assessment of Charismatic–Transformational Leadership Research: Back to the Drawing Board?" ✅ on title, journal and year. The argument, in outline, is that the charismatic–transformational construct family is under-specified and over-broad: it bundles leader behaviours, follower attributions and outcomes into a single package, so that the theory cannot be tested against its rivals and the measurement cannot separate the components. ⚠ on the specifics of the argument, which were not read in full this pass. This matters for the staples because transformational leadership is the empirical backbone of most "leadership matters" claims in the practitioner literature; if the construct is stretched, the backbone is softer than it looks.

**(d) The development literature.** Day and colleagues' 2014 *Leadership Quarterly* review, "Advances in leader and leadership development: A review of 25 years of research and theory," ✅ bibliographic record confirmed, is the standard review of what the development literature has and has not established over a quarter century. Lacerenza and colleagues' 2017 *Journal of Applied Psychology* meta-analysis, "Leadership training design, delivery, and implementation: A meta-analysis," ✅ bibliographic record confirmed, is the standard quantitative synthesis of training effectiveness.

The honest reading of that pair, and the reason this guide does not cite their effect sizes: **the criteria are dominated by reactions and self-report.** Trainee satisfaction, self-assessed learning, and subordinate-rated behaviour change are easier to collect and more flattering than independent measures of capability or organisational results. Where the criteria are self-report, the effect sizes overstate behavioural change, and the overstatement is systematic rather than random. ⚠-knowledge on the criterion mix as characterised here; it is the standard critique of the training-evaluation literature, and it is the reason no number from either paper appears in this guide.

**(e) The endogeneity problem.** Leaders are not randomly assigned to situations. They are selected for, promoted into, and able to choose their contexts — and those contexts have large effects on outcomes. A leader who inherits a growing market will look effective; a leader who inherits a structurally declining one will not, and neither outcome is primarily evidence about the person. This is the fundamental identification problem in the field, and it is not solved by better instruments. It is why the correlation between leader characteristics and firm performance is so hard to interpret, and why the executive-effects literature is a debate rather than a finding. ⚠-knowledge.

### 8.2 The Five Problems in One Table

| Problem | What it does to the evidence | How it is usually handled in the genre |
|---|---|---|
| Common-method variance | Inflates correlations between leader ratings and outcome ratings | Not mentioned |
| Cross-sectional design | Cannot establish direction of causation | Causal language used anyway ("drives," "produces") |
| Leader rating as the measure | Makes the finding partly tautological | Treated as an objective measure of leadership |
| Construct stretch | Bundles behaviours, attributions and outcomes; untestable against rivals | Cited as a unified body of evidence |
| Endogeneity | Selection and situation confound attribution to the person | Anecdote used to fill the gap |

The table is the reason §14 exists in the form it does. If the evidence cannot carry the weight, the claims audit has to be explicit about which claims are carried by evidence and which by argument.

### 8.3 The Argument the Staples Actually Rest On

The staples are not defended here as experimentally established. They are defended on three weaker but honest grounds. Being explicit about the weakness is the point.

**First, mechanism.** Each staple does something identifiable to the organisation's information flow, incentives, or coordination cost, and the mechanism can be stated without reference to any correlation:

- **Integrity and trust** reduce the cost of monitoring and increase the speed of bad news. An organisation that must verify everything is an organisation that pays a verification tax on every interaction, and the tax is largest exactly where speed matters.
- **Direction** reduces coordination cost by settling trade-offs in advance, so that local decisions do not require escalation.
- **Judgement** is the quality of the decision process at the points where the organisation cannot rely on routine — which is to say, at the points that determine whether the routine survives.
- **The people decisions** determine the distribution of capability in the system, and capability is the input to everything else.
- **Accountability** aligns the incentive to report with the incentive to perform; its absence makes reporting a liability, which degrades the information on which every other staple depends.

A mechanism is not proof. It is a reason to expect the effect to hold across contexts — which is exactly what the four pressures test, and exactly what a correlation cannot tell you.

**Second, long observation across many contexts.** The staples are old. Direction, trust, judgement, selection and accountability appear as leadership requirements in texts separated by millennia, in organisational forms with nothing in common — military, religious, commercial, administrative. That is weak evidence in a strict sense (traditions can be wrong for a long time), but it is *relevant* evidence for a claim about what survives context change, because context change is the thing being tested. A practice that has been found necessary in radically dissimilar settings has at least demonstrated that it is not an artefact of one of them.

**Third, falsifiability.** Each staple has a named failure signature — the table in §3.6 — that can be looked for in an organisation. This is what distinguishes the staples from the myths in §4. "Be charismatic" has no failure signature; a leader either has presence or does not, and the claim cannot be checked. "Trust is spent by withholding bad news" has a signature: you learn about problems from someone other than the person who owns them. A claim with a checkable signature is worth more than a claim with a large correlation and no mechanism, even though the correlation looks more scientific.

### 8.4 The Honest Position

Stated without hedging:

- The staples are **not** experimentally established as causes of organisational performance.
- The empirical literature is **consistent with** them, but is also consistent with the romance-of-leadership critique, and cannot currently adjudicate between the two.
- The strongest available case is **mechanism plus cross-context persistence plus falsifiability**, and that is a weaker case than the genre makes.
- The **practical implication** is not that the staples are doubtful, but that they should be held with the right kind of confidence: as structural requirements whose absence produces identifiable failure, not as interventions with known effect sizes. A leader who adopts them because "the research shows" has adopted them for a reason the research does not supply.

This is the position the guide takes, and it is the reason §13 argues that a buyer of leadership advice should demand named mechanisms and stated failure conditions rather than citations.

## 9. The Singapore and Asia Context, Structurally

### 9.1 The Rule This Section Follows

No national-culture claim appears here without attribution, and no attributed claim is treated as a fact about an individual. If Hofstede is invoked, he is invoked as Hofstede: a set of country-level dimensions derived from a factor analysis of an employee-values survey run inside one company between 1967 and 1973. ✅ That is what the reference record says the method was. It is not a description of any person in the room, and the country-level score is a population average that says nothing reliable about an individual. The dimensions are also contested — on sampling, on age of data, on the assumption that a national average is meaningful for a workforce that is itself highly diverse. ⚠ on the contestation.

The practical rule: **culture is a hypothesis about the default, not a fact about the person.** A leader who expects deference because of a country score will misread the senior engineer who does not offer it, and will mistake their own expectation for the other person's culture.

### 9.2 What Is Structurally Different

These points need no cultural claim, because they are features of institutions and markets.

| Structural feature | What it does to the staples | Which staple it pressures |
|---|---|---|
| **State-linked enterprise and GLC governance** | Where the state is a significant owner, the leader answers to a shareholder with non-commercial objectives alongside commercial ones. Direction must be reconcilable with policy; accountability runs to a board whose members are appointed through a political-administrative process as well as a commercial one. | Direction and accountability. The leader cannot set direction purely on market logic, and cannot own an outcome purely on market terms. |
| **The MNC regional-HQ matrix** | A regional headquarters sitting between global function and country market produces dual reporting, conflicting targets and decision rights that are formally clear and practically contested. | Judgement and accountability. The decision is often not the leader's to make, but the outcome is the leader's to own. |
| **High labour mobility among professionals** | People can leave. The cost of a bad management experience is borne by the leader within a year, not by the employee over a career. | Trust and the people decisions. Retention is a live signal of whether the staples are held. |
| **English as the working language** | A cosmopolitan professional class works in a second or third language, which flattens some communication differences and hides others — nuance, humour, and the precise phrasing of disagreement are the first casualties. | Trust and judgement. Candid information is harder to read and easier to withhold when everyone is working in a non-native register. |
| **Regulatory intensity in banking** | Decisions have a documentary afterlife. A judgement made in a meeting becomes a supervisory finding two years later, and the review is conducted by people who were not there. | Judgement and accountability. The decision log is not a nicety; it is the evidence. |
| **Multi-generational teams** | Four cohorts with different expectations of authority, feedback, tenure and technology work in the same team, and the differences are not aligned with seniority. | All five, expressed in the *form* of the conversation rather than its content. |

Two of these deserve a sentence more.

The matrix point is the one most often experienced as a personal failure and is actually a design failure. A leader who is accountable for a country outcome but lacks decision rights over the regional platform is not failing the accountability staple by escalating; they are exercising it. The staple requires naming the mismatch, not absorbing it silently — the argument is in [authority_skills_guide.md](authority_skills_guide.md) §6.

The regulatory point changes the *evidence* requirements of judgement without changing the staple. In a less regulated setting, a good decision can be defended by its outcome. In banking, a good decision has to be defensible by its *process* at the time, because the outcome will be reviewed by someone with the benefit of hindsight and a different set of incentives. That does not make judgement a different staple; it makes the decision log a load-bearing part of it.

### 9.3 What Does Not Change

The five staples are not culture-specific in their mechanism. The claim is precise and worth stating carefully, because it is the kind of claim that is usually made loosely in both directions.

- **Trust is an information problem everywhere.** The content of what must be said, and to whom, and in what register, varies. The mechanism — that withheld bad news degrades the leader's information — does not.
- **Direction is a coordination problem everywhere.** Whether it is spoken in a town hall or encoded in a written charter, whether it is challenged openly or discussed in private, the function is the same: settle trade-offs so that local decisions do not require escalation.
- **Judgement is a decision-quality problem everywhere.** The acceptable speed, the people who must be consulted, and the form of the record vary. The distinction between a good decision and a good outcome does not.
- **The people decisions are selection and candour everywhere.** The legal procedure around an exit varies enormously, and getting it wrong is expensive. The requirement to tell someone the truth about their performance does not vary.
- **Accountability is ownership everywhere.** Whether it is expressed as a formal accountability or as a relational obligation, the test is the same: who tells the unflattering version first.

What varies is **expression**: the register of the hard conversation, the propriety of open disagreement in a meeting, the weight of seniority in a decision, the role of the written record. These are real differences and a leader who ignores them will fail at the expression while holding the staple. That is a failure of skill, not of staple — and it is precisely the distinction this guide has been drawing since §1.

One caution against over-correction. The observation that "in this context, direct feedback does not land" is often true as a description of a *relationship* and false as a description of a *culture*. A leader who concludes that candour is culturally impossible has usually generalised from one bad conversation, and has found a culturally respectable reason to avoid the hard conversation (§3.4). The staple does not permit that; it requires finding the form in which the truth can be said.

## 10. The Development Question — Can Leadership Be Taught?

### 10.1 Dividing the Question

The question "can leadership be taught" is unanswerable as posed, because it bundles three things with different answers. The useful division:

| Layer | What it is | Trainability | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Skill** | A procedure that can be demonstrated, practised and assessed: running a meeting that ends in a decision, giving feedback that is specific and behavioural, structuring a decision so the assumptions are visible, writing a one-page direction. | High. Skills respond to instruction, practice and feedback, and transfer improves when the training context resembles the work context. | Reasonably good, with the criterion caveat in §8.1(d). |
| **Judgement** | The capacity to apply skill to a novel situation where the parameters are not given: which of two bad options is less bad, when to stop an investigation, which risk is worth taking. | Moderate and slow. Develops through varied experience plus feedback, and only when the experience is *varied* — ten years of the same year is not ten years of experience. | Weak. The mechanism is plausible; the outcome evidence is thin and confounded by selection. |
| **Disposition** | The stable tendencies that shape whether someone seeks the hard conversation or avoids it, tolerates ambiguity or needs closure, owns a failure or explains it. | Low after adulthood. | Moderate on stability, weak on the causal link to leadership effectiveness. |

The genre's error is to sell layer-one training and imply layer-two and layer-three outcomes. A programme can teach a manager to structure a decision; it cannot reliably make them willing to make an unpopular one.

### 10.2 Skill — The Honest Case for Training

Skill development is the part of the field with the best support, and the case is straightforward: skills are procedures, procedures improve with practice, and practice improves faster with feedback and with conditions that resemble the job. The training meta-analysis literature (Lacerenza and colleagues, 2017, ✅ bibliographic record) is the standard synthesis, and the design features it examines — needs analysis, practice, feedback, spacing, relevance to the work — are sensible and consistent with the broader training literature.

The caveat, again: **what is measured is often reaction and self-report.** A programme that participants enjoyed and believe helped them is not the same as a programme that changed their behaviour, and the two are routinely conflated in vendor material. The honest claim is that well-designed training reliably improves *knowledge and skill performance in the training setting*, and that transfer to the job is real but smaller and more conditional than the marketing implies. ⚠-knowledge on the transfer literature specifically.

### 10.3 Judgement — The Deliberate-Practice Caveat

The appealing story is that judgement develops through deliberate practice: repeated effortful performance with immediate feedback and correction, in a domain with stable rules and clear signals. That story is well supported in domains with exactly those properties — chess, music, surgery, sport.

Management has almost none of those properties. The rules are unstable, the feedback is delayed by months, the outcome is confounded by factors outside the decision-maker's control (§8.1(e)), and the "coach" is usually the person whose interests are affected by the decision. That is why the deliberate-practice literature's transfer to management is weak, and why claims that a leader can become reliably better at judgement through a structured programme should be treated as claims, not findings. ⚠-knowledge. The Ericsson deliberate-practice framework is real and well-established in its original domains; its extension to managerial judgement is an extrapolation, and the extrapolation is where the difficulty lives.

What does seem to work, on the mechanism if not on strong outcome evidence:

- **Varied experience** — different functions, different markets, different stages of growth, different kinds of crisis — because judgement is pattern recognition and the patterns have to be seen.
- **Feedback that separates process from outcome**, which almost no organisation provides, because performance reviews are written about results.
- **Exposure to a decision review run by someone with no stake in the outcome**, which is the closest management analogue to a coach who is not also a competitor.
- **A decision log**, which converts experience into something that can be examined rather than merely remembered.

These are development *conditions*, not a curriculum. They cannot be sold as a course, which is why they are undersupplied.

### 10.4 Disposition — Selection Beats Development

The disposition layer is where the industry is least honest. The Big Five literature finds real, modest associations between some traits and leadership emergence and effectiveness — extraversion and conscientiousness most consistently, openness and agreeableness less so, neuroticism negative. ⚠-knowledge. Two things follow, and both cut against the development narrative.

First, **the associations are modest**. They are not destiny in either direction: a low-extraversion leader can be highly effective, and a high-extraversion one can be a disaster. Trait scores are a weak prior, not a verdict.

Second, **traits are relatively stable in adulthood**, which means that if a disposition matters for a role, the highest-leverage intervention is **selection and role design**, not a development plan. The derailment literature makes the same point from the failure end: leaders derail for patterned reasons — rigidity, poor interpersonal handling, failure to learn from feedback, over-reliance on a strength — and those patterns are visible before the derailment and are poor candidates for late remediation. ⚠-knowledge.

The guide's position: **select for disposition, develop for skill, and give judgement as many varied reps with as much honest feedback as the system can supply.** The industry's position is the inverse — it develops for all three and selects on interview impression. That is a diagnosis of the market (§13), not a finding about people.

### 10.5 What the Shelf's Guides Assume

Most of the shelf assumes development. [situational_leadership_slii_guide.md](situational_leadership_slii_guide.md) assumes a leader can learn to diagnose development level and flex style; [3d_managerial_effectiveness_guide.md](3d_managerial_effectiveness_guide.md) assumes a leader can learn to add the effectiveness dimension to their style; [high_output_management_guide.md](high_output_management_guide.md) assumes a manager can learn to allocate their own time to high-leverage activity; [../technology/first_time_manager_guide.md](../technology/first_time_manager_guide.md) assumes the transition is learnable in a first ninety days.

Each of those assumptions is reasonable at the skill layer and unearned at the disposition layer. A manager can learn the SLII matrix in an afternoon; whether they will *use* it with a capable subordinate who resents being diagnosed is a disposition question the model does not address. That is not a criticism of the models — they are models of style, not of character — but it is a limitation the shelf should state more often than it does.

## 11. The Cymbal Bank Worked Example (ILLUSTRATIVE)

> **This section is fiction.** Cymbal Bank is a constructed institution. The people, the platform, the dates, the incident numbers and the regulatory timeline are invented for teaching. Nothing here is a case study, an outcome report, or a claim about any real institution. The only thing imported from reality is the structure of the staples and their failure signatures.

### 11.1 The Situation

Cymbal Bank is migrating its domestic payments processing from a twenty-year-old mainframe platform to a new event-driven platform. The programme has been running for fourteen months. It is now eight weeks from a regulatory deadline for a reporting change that the old platform cannot support, which means the migration is no longer a strategic choice; it is the only path to compliance.

The team is forty people across three squads, plus two vendor teams. The leader is **Meera**, a director who took over the programme five months ago, after the previous director left.

Two things are true at the start of the section, and they are the two things the example is built to show:

- **One staple is held under pressure: direction.**
- **One staple is absent: accountability.**

### 11.2 The Staple That Survives — Direction

Meera inherits a programme with a plan, a RAID log, and no answer to the question "what are we deliberately not doing." The plan has forty workstreams and all of them are amber.

In her third week she does the thing that looks least like leadership and is the most load-bearing: she writes a one-page direction with three sentences and a stop list.

- **What we are achieving:** the reporting change live on the new platform before the deadline, for the domestic retail payments book only.
- **What we are not doing:** corporate payments migration, the new reconciliation engine, the data lake, and the decommissioning of the old platform. All four are deferred with a named owner and a review date after the deadline.
- **How we will know we are wrong:** if the new platform cannot process the reconciliation batch within the supervisory window by week four of the pilot, we revert to a bridging solution and re-plan — a decision that will be taken on the evidence, not on the plan.

The direction costs her something visible. Two of the deferred workstreams belonged to a managing director who had sponsored them, and she tells him before the steering committee rather than in it. The reconciliation engine is the piece her own predecessor had called "the whole point." She stops it anyway, and says so in the steering pack in the same sentence that names the deadline.

Three consequences follow, and they are the mechanism, not a moral:

1. **Trade-offs stop escalating.** A squad lead who would have asked "should we fix the reconciliation gap" now has an answer that does not require Meera. The number of decisions reaching her per week drops, and the ones that arrive are the ones only she can take.
2. **The stop list survives the bad week.** In week five the pilot misses a throughput target. The pressure to "just add the reconciliation engine back in, it's half-built" is considerable. Meera restates the direction and shows the tripwire: throughput, not scope, is the failing measure, and the tripwire has not fired.
3. **New people can act.** A contractor who joins in week six makes a correct scoping decision on her second day, because the criterion is written down and she can read it.

This is what §6.5 means by direction being alive: it killed four projects, and it was used to refuse a fifth.

### 11.3 The Staple That Is Absent — Accountability

The failure is not dramatic, which is why it is instructive.

In week six, a batch failure in the pilot causes a four-hour delay in a downstream reporting feed. No customer is affected. The incident review is scheduled for the following Tuesday and is **missed** — not cancelled, missed. The squads assume it will be rebooked. It is not.

When it is finally held, three weeks later, the review is chaired by the programme's delivery lead, and its output is a corrective action list with eight items, seven of which are owned by the two vendor teams. The eighth is owned by a platform engineer who was on call. The review's language is careful: "the batch scheduling configuration was not validated during the pilot cutover." Nobody asks who decided that validation could be skipped. Meera is recorded as having "provided oversight."

The absent staple is visible in four symptoms, and they map exactly onto §3.6's failure signature:

- **The post-mortem names a person and a configuration, not a mechanism or a decision.** The decision — to cut the validation window to protect a milestone — was taken in a programme board that Meera chaired, and it does not appear in the record.
- **Bad news slows down.** After the review, the squads begin pre-filtering what goes into the weekly report. The vendor leads, who are commercially exposed, start copying their account managers on issues before raising them internally. Meera's information quality drops within a month, and she does not notice, because the reports still arrive on time.
- **The pressure is transmitted downward unmodified.** A steering committee asks for assurance that the deadline will be met. Meera converts this into a demand for daily status from all three squads, which consumes roughly an hour a day per squad lead in the final six weeks — precisely when their time is the limiting resource.
- **A people decision is deferred.** One of the two vendor teams is consistently late. The contract has a remedy; Meera does not invoke it, because invoking it would require admitting that she had known for five weeks. The team stays, the lateness continues, and two of her own engineers absorb the work. Nobody is told why.

### 11.4 The Consequence

The programme goes live two days before the deadline. By any external measure it is a success, and it is reported as one.

The cost is in what the organisation now knows about itself:

- The incident review did not produce a mechanism, so the same class of failure — a validation window traded for a date — recurs on the next programme, in a different team, with different people.
- Two engineers who absorbed the vendor team's work have learned that the way to be rewarded is to absorb, silently, and they are both interviewing by the following quarter. Their departure is attributed to "market movement."
- The vendor relationship is now managed through escalation rather than through the contract, which raises the cost of the next engagement.
- And Meera has learned something she will act on: that the way to survive a deadline is to own the outcome in public and distribute the exposure in private. She is a capable leader who has just been trained by the system to hold four staples and perform the fifth.

The illustrative point is not that Meera is a bad leader. It is that **holding four staples and performing the fifth is enough to produce a visible success and an invisible cost**, and the cost lands on the next programme, in the next year, on someone else's watch. This is why accountability is the pivot staple in §6.4 and why its failure is the hardest to see: the outcome that would reveal it is deferred beyond the review cycle that would have caught it.

### 11.5 Back to the Thesis

Meera was wrong about several things. She was wrong about the validation window, wrong about the vendor team's capacity, and wrong about how much daily status her squad leads could absorb without losing the week. The direction survived all three, because it had a stop list and a tripwire, and because it was written down where a stranger could use it.

The accountability did not survive, because it was never a practice — it was a phrase used in a steering committee.

That is the thesis in one case. A staple is not a belief about leadership. Meera believes in accountability; she would say so sincerely, and she has said so in the programme's governance slide. What she has is a belief. What she does not have is the practice that survives being wrong about the vendor, wrong about the date, and wrong about her own earlier decision. **A leadership staple is not a belief about leadership; it is something that survives being wrong about** — and the test is not what the leader says when the programme is green, but what the record shows when it is amber and the review has been missed.

## 12. The Anti-Patterns

Each staple has a characteristic way of being faked. The anti-patterns below are not the absence of the staple; they are its *imitation*, which is more durable because it satisfies the observer. The section closes with the meta anti-pattern that contains all of them.

### 12.1 Integrity Theatre

**What it looks like.** An ethics and conduct module completed annually. A values framework with five words and a launch event. A "speak up" channel with a policy, a poster, and no case in which anyone senior was ever affected by a report. A town hall in which the leader says "my door is always open" while their calendar shows no unbooked hour in six weeks.

**Why it fails the test.** It fails (a) first and completely. Theatre is designed for the good quarter: it is cheap, visible and unobjectionable when nothing is at stake. Under pressure the theatre is the first thing suspended, and the suspension reveals that it was never load-bearing.

**The tell.** Theatre costs the leader nothing. Ask what the last person who raised a genuine concern to this leader lost — in standing, in access, in the next allocation. If the answer is "nothing, because nobody raises concerns," the channel is decoration.

**What it gets right.** A values framework is not worthless. It gives people a vocabulary for objecting, and a leader who is genuinely held to it can use it as cover for a decision they would otherwise have to make alone. The framework is an instrument; the error is treating the instrument as the practice.

### 12.2 Direction as Slogan

**What it looks like.** A mission statement that could belong to any organisation in the sector. A strategy deck with a pyramid, three horizons and no sentence that could cause a project to be cancelled. Quarterly all-hands in which the same four phrases are repeated with increasing emphasis and no example of a trade-off that was actually adjudicated.

**Why it fails the test.** It fails (d) before it fails (a): a slogan cannot answer a counter-argument because it does not contain a claim. When someone asks "should we do X," the slogan provides no criterion, so the decision is made by hierarchy or by whoever is most persistent. Under pressure the slogan is not abandoned — it is repeated more loudly, which is the signature in §6.5.

**The tell.** Has this direction ever stopped something? Name the project that was killed, the customer that was declined, the feature that was deferred, and the date. If the list is empty, the direction has never been used.

**What it gets right.** Repetition is necessary. A direction that is stated once and never repeated is not a direction; it is a document. The error is repetition *instead of* application, not repetition as well as it.

### 12.3 Judgement as Decisiveness

**What it looks like.** A leader who is admired for "making the call." Decisions taken in the meeting, announced in the meeting, and reviewed never. A bias toward action that is genuinely valuable in a crisis and genuinely destructive in a complex one, applied indiscriminately to both. Speed reported as a virtue without a corresponding measure of rework.

**Why it fails the test.** It fails (a) — the pressure condition it appears to be designed for — because speed without quality produces decisions that have to be remade, and the remaking is more expensive than the deliberation would have been. It also fails (c): decisiveness works on a team that will implement and flag problems; on a team that has learned that flagging is unwelcome, it produces silent non-compliance.

**The tell.** Look at the decision log. If there is no record of the assumptions that would have changed the decision, "decisiveness" is a description of temperament, not of judgement. The second tell: how are decisions reviewed? If the review is a discussion of the outcome, the organisation is measuring luck.

**What it gets right.** Decisiveness has real value: a decision made at the right time is often better than a better decision made late, and a leader who cannot decide is a bottleneck that the organisation routes around. The error is treating speed as the whole of quality rather than one of its parameters.

### 12.4 The Deferred People Decision

**What it looks like.** The development plan that has been open for four quarters and contains no observable behaviour. The underperformer who is "going through a difficult period" for the second year. The high performer who is left alone because they are fine, and who leaves because nobody invested. The hiring bar that is lowered "just this once" because the seat has been open for five months.

**Why it fails the test.** It fails (a) and (c) together, and it fails them *silently*. Nothing visibly breaks when the decision is deferred; the cost is absorbed by the team, which routes around the underperformer, and by the leader, who spends increasing time managing the consequences of not having had the conversation. The deferral is usually dressed as kindness, and it is the opposite: it denies the person the information they need to change.

**The tell.** Count the conversations. If the performance concern has been discussed with everyone except the person concerned, the decision has been deferred. The second tell: has any development plan in this team closed in the last year — either because the person improved or because they left? A plan that never closes is a filing cabinet.

**What it gets right.** Timing and process matter. In a regulated organisation, a poorly handled exit is expensive for everyone, and a leader who rushes a dismissal to demonstrate decisiveness is committing a different error. The staple is the *candour*, delivered early, with the process run properly — not the speed of the exit.

### 12.5 Accountability as Blame

**What it looks like.** A post-incident review that identifies a person within the first thirty minutes. A "lessons learned" document whose lessons are all about individual diligence. A leader who says "I'm accountable" and then conducts a search for who caused it. A remediation plan that adds a checklist rather than changing a decision right.

**Why it fails the test.** It fails (a), and it does so in a way that is self-reinforcing. Blame produces closure, and closure feels like accountability, so the organisation learns that the ritual works. What it actually produces is information suppression: the next person who might have flagged the problem calculates the personal cost and stays quiet, which degrades the input to the next decision (§6.2), which produces the next failure.

**The tell.** Read the review's recommendations. If every recommendation is addressed to an individual's behaviour ("ensure that...", "remind the team to..."), the review has located the failure in people rather than in the system or the decision. The second tell: is the leader's own decision in the causal chain? If it is absent from the document, the review was scoped to protect someone.

**What it gets right.** Accountability does require a named person. A review that concludes "the system failed" and names nobody is the opposite error, and it is just as evasive: it makes the failure nobody's, which means the fix is nobody's too. The staple requires both — a named owner *and* an honest causal account that includes the decisions of the powerful.

### 12.6 The Meta Anti-Pattern — The Checklist

The anti-pattern that contains the others is treating the five staples as a checklist to be completed and reported.

It has a recognisable form. A leadership framework is adopted; the five staples become five columns; managers are assessed against the columns; a maturity model is built; a dashboard is produced; the dashboard is reviewed quarterly. Within eighteen months the organisation has a measurement system for the staples and no evidence that any of them is held.

The mechanism is the one §6.6 describes. A checklist converts a practice into a reporting obligation, and a reporting obligation selects for the cheapest evidence that satisfies the reporter. "Accountability" becomes a field in a template. "Trust" becomes a survey score. "Direction" becomes a document with the right sections. Each of these can be produced without the underlying practice, and the production is rewarded because it is visible.

The defence is the question from §6.6, applied at the level of the system rather than the individual: **what did holding this staple cost someone last quarter?** If the answer is nothing — no project stopped, no bonus reduced, no promotion refused, no difficult exit run — then the system is producing artefacts, not practices. The staples are not a maturity model. They are a set of things that are expensive to hold, which is why they are informative when they are held.

## 13. The State of the Advice

### 13.1 What the Industry Sells

Four markets, each with a different failure mode.

**The training market.** Programmes, workshops, certifications, digital libraries. The product is an experience; the outcome claimed is capability. The gap between them is where the industry lives: a two-day workshop reliably produces a good experience and unreliably produces a behaviour change, and the evaluation form measures the former. The meta-analytic literature (§8.1(d)) can tell you that training is associated with improvements on the criteria that are usually measured. It cannot tell you that your programme will change your organisation, and no vendor will say so.

**The assessment industry.** Instruments, profiles, 360 surveys, "insights" reports. The product is a mirror; the outcome claimed is self-knowledge and team effectiveness. The commercial structure is the problem: the instrument is often sold by the organisation that validated it, published in its own journal, and administered by accredited practitioners whose accreditation depends on the instrument's continued legitimacy. The MBTI is the clearest documented case (§4.2), and it is not the only one. ⚠ on the generalisation; the documented case is the MBTI.

**The keynote circuit.** The product is a person and an hour. The economics reward novelty, narrative and stage presence — which is to say, they reward exactly the properties §4.1 identifies as an accelerant rather than a fuel. A keynote cannot be wrong, because it makes no testable claim; it can only be more or less moving.

**The airport book.** The product is a framework with a memorable name. The economics reward differentiation: a book that confirms the previous book does not sell, so the genre is structurally biased toward the novel framework and against the replication. This is the deepest problem, because it operates on the supply of *ideas* rather than on the quality of any particular one. A field in which novelty is the commercial requirement will produce novelty, whether or not the underlying phenomenon has changed.

### 13.2 The Structural Incentives

Three incentives explain most of what the industry produces, and none of them requires anyone to be dishonest.

1. **Differentiation beats confirmation.** A consultant who says "the five things you already believe are correct, and here is the evidence" has no product. A consultant who says "everything you know is wrong, here are seven new things" has a product. The market selects for the second, and the second is much more likely to be wrong.
2. **Unfalsifiability is commercially safer.** A prescription that cannot fail cannot be blamed. "Be authentic" cannot be wrong; "hold the hard conversation within two weeks of the first missed commitment" can be. The industry's language drifts toward the unfalsifiable because it is the safer inventory.
3. **The buyer is not the user, and the metric is not the outcome.** Training is bought by a sponsor, experienced by participants, and evaluated by satisfaction. The person who pays is rarely the person whose behaviour must change, and the measure that decides renewal is rarely the measure that matters. This is a procurement problem, and it is why bad products persist.

### 13.3 What a Buyer Should Demand

The staples guide implies a purchasing standard, and it is a short one. Before buying leadership advice — a programme, an instrument, a framework, a keynote — ask four questions.

| Demand | Why it matters | What a good answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| **A named mechanism** | Without one, the claim is a correlation or an assertion, and it cannot be reasoned about. | "This practice reduces the number of decisions that escalate, because it settles the trade-off in advance." |
| **A stated failure condition** | A claim that cannot fail cannot be trusted. | "If the direction has not caused a project to be stopped in two quarters, it is not working." |
| **Dated evidence, with the instrument named** | The genre's numbers are mostly untraceable (§14). A date and an instrument make a claim checkable. | "This 2017 meta-analysis measured trainee reactions and self-reported learning; it did not measure organisational outcomes." |
| **The boundary of the claim** | Most advice is true in a context and sold as universal. | "This works at team scope, with a leader who has decision rights. It does not apply in a matrix where the leader does not." |

A vendor who can answer all four is selling something that can be argued with. A vendor who cannot is selling an experience, which may still be worth buying — but should be bought as an experience, at the price of an experience.

### 13.4 This Guide's Own Position

This guide is a product of the industry's absence, not its membership. It sells nothing, cites no vendor, and prints no figure it cannot trace. Its claims are structured so that they can be wrong: the four-pressure test can be applied by a reader and can produce a different answer; the ordering in §6 is an argument that a reader can reject with a counter-example; the five staples are a set that a reader can argue should be four or six.

That is the only defence available to a document in this genre. It cannot escape the genre's conditions — it is a synthesis, written by a practitioner, in a repository that also contains model guides with their own commercial lineages — but it can refuse the genre's core move, which is to convert an agreeable assertion into an established finding. Whether it succeeds is for the reader to judge, and §14 and §15 exist so that the judgement can be made on the evidence rather than on the prose.

### 14.1 The Missing Statistic — A Deliberate Omission

> **No engagement figure, failure rate, return-on-investment number, or "times more likely" claim appears anywhere in this guide.** This is not an oversight and it is not squeamishness. The numbers that dominate this genre — the share of change programmes that fail, the share of employees who are disengaged, the performance premium of high-trust teams, the return on leadership development — are, in the great majority of cases, untraceable to a primary instrument: they are cited from other citations, the original survey is proprietary, the sample is undisclosed, or the figure has drifted from its source while keeping its authority. Printing one would fail the guide's own credibility test (§13.3), which asks for dated evidence with a named instrument. A guide that demands that standard of others and exempts itself has no standard at all.

Two consequences follow, and both are stated in place rather than buried here:

- **§8 has no effect sizes.** The argument is made from mechanism, persistence and falsifiability instead, and §8.4 says plainly that this is the weaker case.
- **§13 describes the industry's structure without sizing it.** The training market, the assessment industry and the keynote circuit are characterised by their incentives, not by their revenue. The incentives are observable; the revenue figures are mostly vendor-supplied and would have needed exactly the sourcing this guide refuses to skip.

## 15. What Could Not Be Verified

Each entry is a specific gap, not a general disclaimer. The guide's argument does not depend on any of them being closed, but a reader who wants to check the guide's work should start here.

1. **The exact page references in Drucker and Grove.** The books are real and their arguments are standard, but this pass did not verify page numbers in any edition of *The Effective Executive*, *Management: Tasks, Responsibilities, Practices*, or *High Output Management*. No page citation appears in the guide for that reason. Where the shelf's [high_output_management_guide.md](high_output_management_guide.md) gives chapter-level references, this guide defers to it rather than inventing a page.

2. **Whether any specific "most change programmes fail" figure has a primary instrument behind it.** The claim circulates in several forms with different denominators, and the guide could not trace a single one to an original survey with a disclosed sample. This is the paradigm case for §14.1: the figure is asserted, re-cited, and effectively unfalsifiable. No figure is printed.

3. **The MBTI's test-retest and dichotomisation problems, in detail.** The direction is verified — the reference literature records poor reliability and non-independent dichotomies ✅ — but the specific test-retest statistics, the proportion of respondents who change type on retest, and the exact scoring convention that produces the dichotomisation were not verified against a primary psychometric source. The guide therefore states the problem qualitatively and prints no number.

4. **The cross-cultural leadership literature's sampling.** The GLOBE programme and Hofstede's dimensions both rest on samples with known limitations — one company in Hofstede's case, a particular set of countries and respondent levels in GLOBE's. The guide did not verify the sampling details, and it makes no claim that depends on them. This is why §9 is built on institutional structure rather than on cultural scores.

5. **The causal status of the charisma–performance link.** The circularity of outcome-based definitions is verified ✅. What is not verified is the direction and size of the association when charisma is measured independently, and the guide deliberately prints no estimate. The claim made — that the link is weak and badly measured — is a judgement about the literature, marked ⚠ in §4.1, not a reported finding.

6. **The specific study behind "trust outperforms" claims.** The trust literature's decomposition is well established, but the popular claim that high-trust organisations outperform low-trust ones descends from a chain of consulting surveys and secondary citations that this pass could not trace to a primary, disclosed instrument. The guide makes a mechanism argument about trust (monitoring cost, speed of bad news) and does not make the performance comparison.

7. **Leadership-training return-on-investment claims.** No audited, controlled evidence of financial return was found. The training meta-analysis literature supports effects on learning and self-reported behaviour; the ROI claims in vendor material are not derived from that literature. §10.2 therefore claims skill improvement and explicitly limits the transfer claim.

8. **Whether Collins's "Level 5" was derived from a sample selected on the dependent variable.** This is a real methodological criticism and it should be stated carefully. *Good to Great* selected companies that had already achieved sustained outperformance and then looked for what they had in common — a design that cannot distinguish causes of greatness from correlates of it, and one that is vulnerable to the survivorship problems the criticism literature documents ✅ (Levitt's point that several selected companies later failed; Rosenzweig's halo and correlation-causation points). What this pass did **not** verify is Collins's own account of the selection procedure and his response to the criticism. The honest statement is: the design is vulnerable to selection on the dependent variable, the vulnerability is documented in the critical literature, and the author's defence was not read.

9. **Kotter's original 1990 pagination.** The article text quoted in §5.1 is verified from the *Harvard Business Review* page carrying the December 2001 issue ✅, which presents the article as a reprint. The original May–June 1990 issue, its page range, and whether the 2001 text is identical were not verified. The guide cites the article by title and notes both dates rather than asserting a 1990 page range.

10. **The claim that transactional and transformational leadership are complementary rather than opposed.** This is standard in the literature and appears in the reference material, but the guide did not verify it against Burns or Bass directly, and so makes no claim about the original formulation beyond naming the construct family in §8.1(c).

11. **The Cymbal Bank case.** Not a verification gap but a provenance statement: Meera, the programme, the platform, the vendor teams, the missed review, the deadline and the outcomes are **invented for teaching**. Only the staple structure and the failure signatures they instantiate are sourced. Nothing in §11 should be read as a claim about any institution, and no real bank, vendor or regulator is depicted.

None of these gaps undermines the argument, because the argument does not rest on any of them. It rests on the mechanism claims in §8.3, which are stated as mechanisms rather than as findings, and on the shelf's own guides for the model mechanics. That is a weaker foundation than the genre claims and a more honest one than the genre provides.

## 16. The Glossary, the Cross-References and the Closing

### 16.1 The Glossary

- **Accountability** — unconditional ownership of an outcome, including the parts caused by others, the system or luck; distinguished from blame, which assigns fault to a person after the fact (§3.5, §12.5).
- **Anti-pattern** — the imitation of a staple that satisfies the observer while costing the leader nothing; more durable than absence because it produces evidence (§12).
- **Blame** — the assignment of fault to a person, usually to produce closure or protect someone else; the characteristic corruption of accountability (§3.5, §12.5).
- **Charisma** — a personal quality of magnetic appeal; in Weber's sociology, the basis of one type of legitimate authority, which must be routinised to survive its holder (§4.1).
- **Charismatic authority** — legitimacy grounded in attributed exceptional personal qualities rather than office or custom; unstable by construction (§4.1).
- **Common-method variance** — inflation of correlations that occurs when all variables are measured by one respondent on one instrument (§8.1(a)).
- **Construct stretch** — the broadening of a theoretical construct until it bundles behaviours, attributions and outcomes, and can no longer be tested against rivals (§8.1(c)).
- **Context** — the conditions that shape which behaviours are available: industry, regulation, culture, scale, maturity, technology, business state. Changes the expression of a staple, not its mechanism (§1.2, §9).
- **Decision log** — a record of a decision, its assumptions and the conditions that would have changed it; the operational form of judgement and the evidence a regulatory review will ask for (§3.3, §9.2).
- **Deliberate practice** — effortful practice with immediate feedback in a domain with stable rules; well supported in chess, music and surgery, weakly extended to managerial judgement (§10.3).
- **Derailment** — the patterned failure of a leader who was previously effective, typically through rigidity, poor interpersonal handling, or over-reliance on a strength (§10.4).
- **Direction** — the answer to three questions a team can act on: what we are achieving, what we are deliberately not doing, and how we will know we are wrong (§3.2).
- **Endogeneity** — the confounding that arises because leaders are selected for, and choose, the situations whose outcomes are then attributed to them (§8.1(e)).
- **Followership** — the willingness of others to be influenced: to accept direction, supply candid information and expend discretionary effort (§1.2).
- **Integrity** — alignment over time between what is said, what is decided and what is rewarded; narrower and more operational than the moral-philosophy sense (§3.1).
- **Judgement** — decision quality under incomplete information and time pressure, distinguished from decisiveness (a speed property) and from the outcome (which may be luck) (§3.3, §12.3).
- **Leader** — the person who carries responsibility for direction and for the conditions others work in, at whatever scope; a function, not a rank (§1.2, §5).
- **Manager** — the person accountable for converting intention into coordinated output through other people within an existing system (§1.2, §5).
- **Mechanism** — the identifiable process by which a staple produces its effect on information flow, incentives or coordination cost; the ground on which this guide defends the staples instead of citing correlations (§8.3).
- **Outcome bias** — the tendency to judge a decision by its result rather than by the information and process available at the time (§3.3).
- **Performed staple** — the ritual version of a staple: the form is produced, the cost is not paid, and the observer is satisfied (§6.6).
- **Romance of leadership** — the tendency of observers to over-attribute organisational outcomes to leaders, because leadership is a culturally available explanation for complex events (§8.1(b)).
- **Scope** — the unit over which a leader's decisions bind: team, function, enterprise. Changes the instrument, not the staple (§1.2, §7).
- **Staple** — a practice that survives being wrong about: it still produces its effect when performance pressure, context, personnel and fashion change (§1.2, §2).
- **The test** — the four pressures applied to a candidate staple: performance pressure, changed context, a different team, a fashionable counter-argument (§2.2).
- **Trust** — willingness to be vulnerable to another based on positive expectations; downstream of perceived ability, benevolence and integrity (§3.1, §6.3).

### 16.3 The Closing Summary

The shelf is enormous and mostly partial. That is not a criticism of the individual guides — each of them does a job this one cannot — but it creates a problem the shelf did not solve: a reader who has read six of them has six models and no way to decide which one to keep when the quarter is bad.

This guide's answer is a filter rather than a model. The filter is four pressures, and its purpose is subtractive: it removes the practice that only works when things are going well, the property that only works in one context, the claim that only works on one kind of team, and the prescription that has no answer to the fashionable objection. What survives is small, old, and unglamorous: tell the truth and be consistent (integrity and trust); decide what you are doing and what you are not (direction); make decisions you can defend on the information you had (judgement); choose people carefully, develop them specifically, and tell them the truth early (the people decisions); and own the outcome, including the parts you did not cause (accountability).

The evidence for these is weaker than the genre admits, and §8 says so at length. The defence is not a correlation. It is that each staple has a mechanism you can trace, a history long enough to have survived many contexts, and a failure signature you can look for in your own organisation this week. That is a modest foundation. It is also the only foundation that survives being examined, which is the whole point.

The five staples do not make a leader good. They make a leader *load-bearing* — and the difference matters, because the leader who is merely impressive is replaceable by the next impressive person, while the leader who is load-bearing leaves behind a system that works when they are not in the room. That is the test the guide has been applying since §2, and it applies to the guide itself: what survives here is not the taxonomy, the ordering, or the worked example. It is the question.

**a leadership staple is not a belief about leadership; it is something that survives being wrong about.**
