## 1. The Overview

### 1.1 The Name Disambiguated: Three Other TMXes

**This guide is about one entity and only one: TMX Transform**, the supply-chain consultancy trading at **tmxtransform.com**, which describes itself in its own words (first-party About page, extracted October 2026) as *"an end-to-end supply chain consultancy, providing expert advice and practical, innovative solutions to businesses globally."* It is a private services firm, not a listed company, and the abbreviation "TMX" in the wild denotes at least four different things. A reader must be told which one is meant before a single fact lands.

### 1.3 The Thesis

**A supply-chain consultancy is paid for decisions — and judged on the ones the client actually implements.** The advisory product is not a document; it is a *better decision*, made by the client, on evidence the client could not have assembled alone, at a moment when the money has not yet been committed. The document is the evidence; the decision is the product; and the measure that matters is whether the design survived contact with procurement, construction, and the operational reality of a warehouse at peak.

Three consequences follow, and they organise this whole guide:

- **The value is front-loaded into the decision.** Move a DC site, and you commit a decade of rent, labour and freight. A consultancy earns its fee at the moment that decision is made well — or costs the client far more than the fee at the moment it is made badly.
- **The consultancy's own success is not the deliverable's success.** A recommendation can be excellent and still go unbuilt — because the board said no, because the volumes never arrived, or because nobody on the client side could maintain the model. Section 7 exists precisely to stop a buyer confusing a good report with a good outcome.
- **A firm that also does the implementation has a conflicted incentive and a real advantage at the same time.** "Real-world experience" and "long-term partner" (the firm's own differentiator phrases, first-party About page, October 2026) cut both ways: the same firm that advises may also build, and the buyer's job is to see the seam (Section 6.3).

### 1.6 Why a Bank Should Care

A bank touches a firm like this from two directions, and only one of them is evidenced.

**As a buyer (structural, unevidenced).** A bank runs its own estate — branches, offices, data centres, cash centres, vaults — and every one of those is a footprint, property and build question of exactly the kind the Industrial Real Estate and Construction Project Management arms answer. That is a **structural adjacency, not an observed practice**: the firm publishes no bank client, and its own industry lists (first-party, October 2026) include "ICT & Data Centre" and "Government & Infrastructure" but **not** banking or financial services. Section 10.1 states this as unasserted, because the honest finding is the absence.

**As a financier (structural adjacency).** A client's network redesign is a **financeable capital event**: new DCs, automation, fit-out, working capital across a longer or shorter node set. A bank financing that capex wants the *same* costed design the consultancy produces — the two products sit on opposite sides of one decision. That is a structural adjacency only; **there is no evidence that TMX operates in supply-chain finance or trade finance**, and this guide does not claim it does. Section 10.2 crosses to the four banking guides rather than re-deriving them.

**Why the buyer's view is the bank's own view.** A bank is itself a large, multiparty client that buys professional services and must run the same evaluation a corporate buyer would: does the diagnostic convert to a costed, implemented decision (Section 7)? A bank that can read a supply-chain consultancy's proposal critically is also a better financier of the capex those proposals trigger — it can see whether the numbers a borrower is banking on were ever costed (Section 13).

**TMX Transform is a private, end-to-end supply-chain consultancy founded in Australia** (first-party 'Our journey' timeline, October 2026), with a self-description that leads on advice and solutions delivered "to businesses globally" (first-party About page, October 2026). It is not a software vendor, not a 3PL, and not a brokerage in the transaction sense; it sells expert advice and implementation support across supply-chain strategy, physical distribution, industrial property and construction project management, and it runs a separate data-centre practice, TMX Edge.

Two structural facts shape everything in this guide. ### 2.5 Legal Form: Not Public

**Record the absence as the finding.** Across the sources consulted for this guide, **no company number, no registration, and no legal-entity form (Pty Ltd / Ltd / Inc) for TMX Transform is public**. ## 6. How an Engagement Actually Works

### 6.1 The Phases

**The firm publishes the arc; it does not publish a commercial model.** Combining the five service stages (Section 4.2) and the Construction PM four-step process, an engagement of this kind runs as follows — with the phases framed here from the **buyer's** side, not the seller's:

| Phase | What happens | What the buyer is committing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0. Scoping / diagnostic** | A short, structured assessment ("Rapid end-to-end diagnostic") to find where the improvement is. | A small fee and, critically, **access to the client's data and people**. |
| **1. Analysis & Strategy** | Current-state analysis; needs identification; the blueprint. | Time from operations, finance and IT; agreement on the problem statement. |
| **2. Design & Planning** | Future operating model; the **business case**. | A decision gate: fund the change or stop. This is the moment the fee earns its keep. |
| **3. Procurement** | Procuring property, equipment and systems. | Capital commitment begins to crystallise; the consultancy is now transacting. |
| **4. Implementation** | Support through integration and transition "to operational Go Live". | The largest spend; the moment the design meets reality. |
| **5. Ongoing Management** | "Ongoing assistance to ensure your new operations are working at their best." | An ongoing relationship the buyer can choose to end or continue. |

### 6.2 The Deliverables

**A buyer should ask, deliverable by deliverable, "is this an artefact I own, or a slide I am shown?"** The firm's own vocabulary implies a set of concrete artefacts — a **diagnostic**, a **blueprint**, a **business case**, a **network model**, a **DC design**, a **lease or transaction**, a **make-good plan**, a **feasibility study**, a **procurement recommendation** — plus the softer, less verifiable outputs (advice, assurance, "confidence to implement change", which is a phrase the firm uses on its own pages). The business case is the pivotal artefact: it is where the recommendation becomes a decision, and where the repository's `business_case_development_guide.md` applies (cross-referenced, not re-derived).

### 6.3 Where Advice Ends and Delivery Begins

**This is the seam the buyer must see.** The firm markets itself as end-to-end and as a "long-term partner" that "can also help you implement it" (first-party, October 2026) — so for this firm, advice and delivery can be the *same* engagement. Two honest observations follow:

- **The seam is commercial, not philosophical.** At the **Procurement** stage the firm stops advising and starts acting on the client's behalf; at **Implementation** it supports a build. The client should identify, in the contract, exactly where "we recommend" becomes "we execute", who is accountable at each point, and how the firm's interests are handled when it both recommends and executes.
- **The incentive cuts both ways.** A firm that stays to implement has better feedback from reality than one that leaves after the report — but it also has an interest in the change being built. The buyer's protection is not suspicion; it is a **costed business case with an agreed measurement** (Section 7).

### 6.4 What No Consultancy in This Category Can Promise

**Honest boundary-setting.** However good the firm is, a consultancy of this kind **cannot promise**:

- **That the volumes its model assumed will actually arrive.** A network is designed for a forecast; if the forecast is wrong, the design is wrong, and no consultancy can guarantee a market.
- **That the recommendation will be implemented.** This is the firm's own acknowledged gap in the market — and the reason it markets implementation. A consultancy can advise, cost, and support; it cannot force a board to fund, or a business to change.
- **That the delivered building will perform as modelled.** Design and simulation reduce uncertainty; they do not eliminate it. Actual throughput depends on labour, systems and demand.
- **That the advice is independent of its own implementation interest.** Where the firm also builds, the independence question is structural, not a matter of character (6.3).

**The thesis restated at the engagement level:** a supply-chain consultancy is paid for decisions — and judged on the ones the client actually implements. Everything in Section 7 exists to help a buyer make that judgement.

## 7. The Buyer's Questions

**This is the guide's practical contribution: a checklist a buyer can actually put to a supply-chain consultancy in a pitch, a proposal evaluation, or a contract negotiation.** It is organised as questions, each with *why it matters*, *what a good answer looks like*, and *the red flag*. The checklist is written for the buyer of advice — not as a brochure for the seller.

### 7.1 The Checklist, Developed Properly

**A. On who survives past the pitch.**

| Question | Why it matters | Good answer | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Who exactly stays for implementation?** | The people who sold the work are often not the people who run it; "we don't just give advice and run" (firm's own words) is a claim until named. | **Named individuals** with roles, committed days, and a written commitment that they remain through the phases the client cares about. | A team presented without names, or a partner who appears at the pitch and never returns. |
| **Who is accountable at each phase?** | The firm's own model moves from advising to procuring to supporting (Section 4.2); accountability must move with it. | A phase-by-phase responsibility matrix (who decides, who recommends, who executes). | "We own it end to end" with no matrix. |
| **What does the post-go-live phase cost, and is it optional?** | "Ongoing Management" is a published stage; a buyer should not discover it as a locked-in retainer. | A priced, optional ongoing-support phase with an exit. | Ongoing support bundled so the client cannot tell where the fee ends. |

**B. On the model and the numbers.**

| Question | Why it matters | Good answer | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **How is the model validated?** | A network or DC model is only as good as its validation; the firm's own claims (a "Rapid end-to-end diagnostic") do not say how it is checked. | A stated validation method: against the client's actual transaction history, actual costs, and a pilot or a known-operating facility. | "Peer-reviewed", "proprietary", or "trust the model". |
| **What data do you require from us, and what if we do not have it?** | The model's quality is capped by the client's data; the buyer needs to know the data bill before it signs. | A **specific data list** (order lines, SKUs, costs, volumes, service levels) and an explicit plan for gaps and estimates. | "We work with whatever you have", with no list — which usually means the gaps are modelled silently. |
| **How is the business case costed, and how is it discounted?** | This is the decision artefact (Section 4.2); an uncosted recommendation is just an opinion. | A costed case with stated discount rate, payback horizon, capex/opex split, and the assumptions register. | Benefits asserted in percentages with no input costs (cross-ref `business_case_development_guide.md`). |
| **What are the top three assumptions, and how wrong can they be?** | Volumes, labour cost and growth are the usual culprits; a buyer must see the sensitivity. | An explicit assumption register with a sensitivity or scenario analysis. | Assumptions buried in an appendix, or a single-point forecast. |
| **What happens when volumes change — down as well as up?** | A network is designed for a forecast; the buyer needs the downside case. | Modelled scenarios at, say, below-plan and above-plan volumes, with the break-even change that flips the decision. | Only the upside case. |

**C. On ownership and lock-in.**

| Question | Why it matters | Good answer | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Who owns the model, the data and the outputs?** | If the client cannot reuse the model, it is rented, not owned. | Written confirmation that the client owns the model, the data inputs and the deliverables, with source files handed over. | "Provided under licence" / "on request". |
| **Will the model be handed over, and can our people run it?** | The single most common failure is a model nobody on the client side can maintain (Section 13). | A **knowledge-transfer plan**, training, and a handed-over, documented model. | "We'll keep it updated for you" — i.e. permanent dependency. |
| **Can we validate the model ourselves after handover?** | Independent reuse is the test of true transfer. | Yes, with documentation and a working copy. | Opaque spreadsheets or vendor-locked tooling with no export. |

**D. On the firm's independence and interest.**

| Question | Why it matters | Good answer | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Does the firm also build or procure what it recommends?** | For this firm, advice and delivery can be one engagement (Section 6.3); the conflict is structural. | A clear statement of where the firm will and will not bid, and how the recommendation is segregated from the delivery bid. | A firm that recommends its own arm without disclosing the interest. |
| **Will it bid for the automation, the build or the lease it recommends?** | The buyer needs to know before, not after. | Full disclosure, in writing, of likely conflicts. | "We'll talk about that later." |
| **Can the client take the design to a competing bidder?** | Competitive tension is the buyer's best protection on price. | Yes — the design is portable and the firm commits not to lock it. | Design deliberately tied to one vendor's kit. |

**E. On evidence.**

| Question | Why it matters | Good answer | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Show me a comparable engagement, and can I speak to that client?** | A consultancy's client logos and case pages are marketing evidence, not audited results (Section 11). | A named, referenceable client of similar size, sector and problem — with a live reference call. | "Confidential" for every case, or a logo wall with no references. |
| **What did that engagement deliver, measured how, and over what window?** | Outcome claims need a baseline and a horizon. | A before/after with an agreed metric and a stated measurement window. | A percentage saving with no baseline. |
| **What went wrong on the last one, and what did you change?** | Firms that answer this honestly are usually the safer ones. | A concrete failure and a corrective. | "Nothing ever goes wrong." |

### 7.2 How to Weight the Answers

**Not all questions are equal, and the buyer should pre-commit to weights before the pitch.** A usable weighting for a capital-scale network or DC decision:

- **Highest weight — the costed business case and its sensitivity (B).** If the numbers are not costed, discounted and stress-tested, nothing else rescues the engagement.
- **High weight — ownership and knowledge transfer (C).** A brilliant design the client cannot maintain becomes a liability at the next volume change.
- **High weight — independence and the delivery seam (D).** The firm's own end-to-end model makes this a live conflict, not a theoretical one.
- **Medium weight — who stays (A).** Take the good answer in writing, not in a slide.
- **Medium weight — evidence (E).** Marketed references are starting evidence; a live reference call converts them.

### 7.3 The Contract and Handover Questions

**Three things should be contractual, not conversational:**

1. **A deliverable list with acceptance criteria** — what artefact (model, business case, design, feasibility) is delivered, in what format, and what "accepted" means.
2. **An assumptions register as a contract annex** — so that when the forecast fails, it is visible which assumption failed, and the fee is not re-litigated on hindsight.
3. **A handover clause** — model, data, documentation and training, with a stated completion event.

**The buyer's one-line discipline:** *buy the decision, demand the model, and contract the handover.* A consultancy is a supplier of decisions; the client keeps the decision and should therefore keep the means to remake it.

## 8. The Market and Competitive Frame

### 8.1 The Five Categories

| # | Category | What it is | Why it competes for the same engagement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **i** | **Global consultancies' operations / supply-chain practices** | The operations and supply-chain arms of the large generalist advisory firms. | They bring brand, scale, cross-industry benchmarking and board-level access; they are the default shortlist when the problem is strategic and the client is large. |
| **ii** | **Specialist supply-chain and distribution-centre advisories** | Focused firms that do network design, DC design and logistics diagnostics as their core business. | This is the firm's own category (TMX Transform sits here), and the closest like-for-like comparison on method and price. |
| **iii** | **Industrial-property brokerages and developers' advisory arms** | The industrial arm of the property industry — brokerages, developers and their advisory practices. | They own the market intelligence, the site pipeline and the lease/transaction mechanics that the firm's Industrial Real Estate group also sells. |
| **iv** | **Warehouse-automation and WMS software vendors' advisory / feasibility arms** | Automation integrators and warehouse-software vendors that offer feasibility and design services alongside their products. | They will model a warehouse and build a business case — but toward their own equipment or software (the classic buyer conflict, Section 7.1-D). |

### 8.2 The Comparison, Structural and Unranked

**How the five categories differ, structurally — not how they rank.** A buyer's real choice is between four *bases of authority*:

| Basis of authority | Typically the category | The trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Benchmark depth across many clients** | Global consultancies (i) | Excellent at the strategic frame and the board conversation; often thinner on the physical build and the local property market. |
| **Focus and method depth** | Specialist advisories (ii) | Deep on network and DC method; smaller brand and less cross-industry data unless (as TMX does) they build a Centre of Excellence to pool it. |
| **Market and transaction access** | Property brokerages / developers (iii) | Best site pipeline and lease knowledge; their interest is in a transaction occurring. |

**This section must earn its place and invent no link.** There are exactly two honest connections between a bank like Cymbal Bank and a supply-chain consultancy of this kind — the institution as **buyer** of the advice, and the institution as **financier** of a client's project. It is a worked decision by Cymbal Bank, considering **a consultancy of this kind**, and it is calibrated so that every step maps to a mechanism this guide has already established.

### 12.1 The Scenario

**Cymbal Bank is rationalising its Asia-Pacific operations and technology footprint, and must decide whether to buy external advisory advice — and how to structure it.** The concrete decision on the table: consolidate three ageing regional operations sites into one new **Asia-Pacific operations and data-centre hub** (or two, if the power and site economics say so), within four years, while keeping service continuity. Cymbal Bank estimates the programme at a capital scale large enough that the fee for good advice is trivial next to the cost of a wrong site — which is precisely why it is buying advice at all.

The bank shortlists **two firms of the specialist kind** this guide describes (Category ii in Section 8.1), plus the regional property market's own advisers (Category iii). It will not, at this stage, use a global consultancy's operations practice (Category i): the problem is physical, local and build-oriented, not board-strategic. ### 12.2 Working Section 6: The Engagement

**And this is the whole point.** The fee bought a *better decision* at the moment capital was still uncommitted; the value shows up not in the report but in the number the board approved and the asset the bank got. **Which is why a supply-chain consultancy is paid for decisions and judged on the ones the client actually implements.**

## 13. The Anti-Patterns and Open Questions

### 13.1 The Anti-Patterns

**These are the ways a supply-chain-advisory engagement goes wrong. Each is a real failure mode for any firm in this category, and each is visible from the buyer's side before the money is spent.**

**1. Buying a diagnostic and expecting delivered change.** A diagnostic (Section 6.1, Phase 0) is a map of where the improvement is; it is not the improvement. A buyer who funds "a diagnostic" and assumes a transformation arrives has bought the front page of a book and expected the whole plot. **Guard:** decide in advance whether you are buying a decision (a diagnostic and a business case) or a delivered change (an implementation programme), and price them separately (Section 6.3).

**2. A model nobody on the client side can maintain.** The most common quiet failure. A network or DC model is delivered in a consultancy's tooling, by a consultant who leaves, in a format the client cannot open — and within a year no one can answer "what did we assume, and is it still true?". **Guard:** ownership, source files, documentation and a **named internal owner** as contractual conditions (Section 7.1-C, 7.3). A model you cannot rerun is a model you do not own.

**3. A network designed for volumes that never arrive.** Every network is designed to a forecast (Section 6.4). If the growth case was optimistic — or the downside was never modelled — the client builds a facility priced for a business that did not materialise. **Guard:** the downside scenario and the break-even change are a required deliverable, not an optional extra (Section 7.1-B).

**4. Advisory work whose recommendations were never costed.** A recommendation without a business case is an opinion with a logo on it. If the benefits are asserted in percentages and the costs are not in the same document, no board and no financier can act on it — and no one can later say whether the advice was right. **Guard:** no design without a costed, discounted case and an assumptions register (Section 7.1-B, 7.3; cross-ref `business_case_development_guide.md`).

**5. The logo wall as evidence.** "500+ valued clients" and 54 case pages are marketing (Section 11.2). Treating them as proof of fit for *your* problem is the error. **Guard:** a live reference call for a comparable engagement (Section 7.1-E).

**6. The indefinite implementation promise.** "We stick around to help you implement" is a differentiator on a website and a set of unstaffed hours in reality. **Guard:** name the people and commit the days (Section 7.1-A).

**7. The conflict left unstated.** A firm that recommends and bids is not necessarily conflicted — but a buyer that never asks is unprotected. **Guard:** written disclosure of likely bids and a recommendation segregated from the delivery bid (Section 7.1-D).

**8. The ongoing-management retainer that becomes lock-in.** The fine print of "Ongoing Management" (Section 4.2) can quietly convert a project into a perpetual dependency. **Guard:** priced, optional, exit-able (Section 7.1-A).

### 13.2 The Open Questions

**Honest questions this guide could not close from the sources consulted** (carried into Section 15):

- **What is the firm's legal entity, ownership and financial scale?** Not public (§2.5). A buyer cannot assess balance-sheet stability, key-person risk, or who ultimately owns the firm and the related TMX Edge brand.
- **How is any outcome claim measured?** The "15% average cost-to-serve saving" and the growth CAGRs come with no published methodology, baseline or window (Sections 9.1, 11.2).
- **What is "TMX Xchange", concretely?** A single published phrase — "a single operating framework" (Section 5.1). Its substance is not public.
- **Is the "200+ specialists / 16 years" tagline measured or aspirational?** The tagline and the size band are company claims (Section 2.4); the underlying measurement is not published.
- **Which founding year is correct, 2009 or 2011?** The firm's timeline and its own CEO's quoted words disagree (Section 2.2).
- **Where is the headquarters, Sydney or Melbourne?** The firm's contact page and its LinkedIn profile disagree (Section 3.3).
- **Does the firm ever serve financial institutions?** No bank client and no financial-services industry is published (Section 10.1); a bank evaluating a firm of this kind enters a space the firm does not publicly claim.
- **Is the firm's implementation interest disclosed to clients as a matter of course?** The firm markets end-to-end delivery; whether the advice/delivery seam is disclosed commercially (Section 6.3) is not public.

### 16.3 The Closing Summary

**TMX Transform is a private, Australian-founded, end-to-end supply-chain consultancy** that sells advice, design and implementation support across supply-chain strategy, industrial real estate and construction project management, and — since September 2026 — runs a data-centre arm, **TMX Edge**, under a consolidated **Global Centre of Excellence**. The verified record is clear on structure and thin on achievement, which is exactly what a private advisory firm looks like from outside: the name, self-description, origin timeline, offices, leadership, service taxonomy, five service stages, innovation products, Centre of Excellence and TMX Edge are all published by the firm and can be taken as fact about *what the firm is*; its size band, growth rates, outcome claims, client logos and awards are its own marketing and can be taken only as the firm's claims; and its **legal form, ownership, revenue, founding year and headquarters city are either not public or self-contradictory**, recorded here as absences rather than smoothed over.

The guide's value is the buyer's view. Section 6 shows the engagement arc and where advice ends and delivery begins — the seam that matters most for a firm that markets end-to-end delivery and "we don't just give advice and run". Section 7 turns that into a checklist: ask who stays, how the model is validated, what data is needed, whether the business case is costed and stress-tested, who owns the model, and what the firm does when volumes change. Section 8 frames the market by category and ranks no one. Section 10 keeps the bank honest, stating the buyer and financier connections as structural adjacencies and refusing to invent an evidenced financial-services practice. Section 11 states the method for weighing a private firm's marketing against its verifiable facts. And Section 12 works it all through one fictional Cymbal Bank decision.

**The one idea that ties it together is the thesis this guide opened with, and the reason to read a consultancy profile at all:**

**a supply-chain consultancy is paid for decisions and judged on the ones the client actually implements.**
