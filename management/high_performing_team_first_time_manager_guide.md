## 1. The Intersection: The One-Page Thesis

### 1.1 What this guide is

The repo already has two deep disciplines, each with its own guide: the **transition discipline** — what you do in your first 90 days in a new role ([the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md), built on Michael Watkins' *The First 90 Days*: break-even point, STARS, learning agenda, early wins, alliances) — and the **team-building discipline** — what a high-performance team is and how to engineer one ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md), built on Katzenbach & Smith, Google Project Aristotle, Amy Edmondson, and Richard Hackman). This guide is the **intersection**: the operating playbook for the moment the two disciplines collide in one person — the engineer or architect who just got promoted to manager and now owns both a transition *and* a team.

The one-page thesis: **a first-time manager builds a high-performing team in their first quarters by doing five things, roughly in this order:**

1. **Convert themselves.** The peer→manager mindset shift: from doing the work to enabling the team (§2). This is the prerequisite; nothing below works if the new manager is still playing.
2. **Convert the group into a team.** Charter, goals, and working agreements (§4), plus the rituals that make the agreements real (§5).
3. **Install the substrate.** Psychological safety (§6) and trust (§7) are the load-bearing walls; the 1:1 practice (§8) and feedback loops (§9) are the plumbing.
4. **Convert individual effort into collective output.** Delegation and empowerment (§10), goals and performance management (§11), and the team-performance levers — accountability, decisions, communication, conflict, culture (§12).
5. **Survive the first year.** Name the pitfalls (§13) before they name you.

### 1.2 What this guide is not

It is not another summary of the HPT canon (that is [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md)), not another book companion (that is [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) and [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md)), and not another framework catalogue (that is [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md)). Where a sibling already derives a model, this guide **cross-references it in one line and moves on** — the value here is the *sequencing, the prioritization, and the first-quarter decisions* that a first-time manager faces, in a bank/enterprise-technology setting (the Cymbal Bank context used across this repo).

### 1.3 Reading paths

- **Promoted last week?** Read §2 and §3 now (mindset + first 90 days), then §6 (safety) and §8 (1:1s) before your first week of 1:1s is over. The rest can wait until days 30–60.
- **Inheriting a struggling team?** Start with §13 (pitfalls — including the "savior complex"), then the diagnosis stack in [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §9.2, then §4–§6.
- **Studying the discipline?** Read front to back; §14 (the worked example) shows everything assembled in one artifact, and §15–§16 are the honest verification ledger.

## 2. The Foundation: The Mindset Shift

### 2.1 From doing to enabling

The single most important mental move of the first-time manager is also the most resisted: **your output is no longer your output.** Andrew Grove's *High Output Management* (Random House, 1983; Vintage reissue 1995 — verified via Open Library) states the thesis that became Silicon Valley's management bedrock: a manager's output is the output of the team (and of the neighboring organizations the manager influences), not the work the manager personally produces (✅ thesis verified via multiple summaries of the book; the exact printed wording ⚠ — see §16). Every behavior in this guide follows from that one line: if the team does not deliver, you did not deliver; if the team delivers, you delivered — whether or not you wrote any code.

The practical consequence is a **calendar and identity audit** the new manager should run in week one:

| Old identity (senior IC) | New identity (first-time manager) | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| My code, my design, my fix | Their code, their design, their fix — my coaching | You stop being the author; you become the enabler |
| My ticket board | The team's roadmap and the org's outcomes | Your scoreboard is team-level now (§11) |
| My deep-work blocks | Their deep-work blocks, which you protect | Your calendar is mostly 1:1s, meetings, and thinking time (§5, §8) |
| I am measured on my commits | I am measured on the team's delivery and health | The review criteria change before your first review (§11) |
| Being right | Being useful | The best answer loses to the answer the team owns (§4) |

### 2.2 The peer→manager transition

The repo's deepest treatment of this journey is [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) — Camille Fournier's *The Manager's Path* (O'Reilly, 2017 — ✅ verified) — and this guide does not re-derive it. Three of its findings are load-bearing enough to restate in one line each, with the cross-ref:

- **The rungs exist for a reason.** Mentoring → tech lead → manager → manager of managers; each rung adds a hat and removes a hat. A first-time manager is usually skipping straight into "managing people" without having done the tech-lead rung's letting-go practice ([the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §5–§6).
- **The alpha geek warning.** The pattern that most often ends a promising manager is the brilliant IC who cannot stop redoing others' work — including, sometimes, in the mirror ([the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) §13.1).
- **The relationship inversion.** Your former peers are now your reports; the friendship is not gone, but the accountability line is real. This is the "friend-boss trap" (§13.4).

### 2.3 The two jobs

Everything a first-time manager does collapses into two jobs, and the first quarter is about proving you can hold both:

1. **Get results through others** — goals, delegation, accountability, decisions (§10–§12).
2. **Grow the people** — 1:1s, feedback, coaching, stretch assignments, career conversations (§8–§9).

A team that delivers but whose people are not growing is a short-term team; a team whose people grow but that never delivers is a club. The high-performance team (Katzenbach & Smith's top of the performance curve) is precisely where the two converge: deep commitment to *each other's growth* is what produces outsized delivery ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §1.2). The first-time manager's job is to hold both jobs in tension from day one — the organization will happily let you do only the first.

### 2.4 The mindset checklist (first week)

- [ ] I can state the team's mission and how it connects to the bank's objectives — or I have a plan to learn it (§3).
- [ ] I have stopped assigning myself the hardest technical work. (Hardest *organizational* problem: yes. Hardest ticket: no.)
- [ ] I have a 1:1 slot with every report this week, and they own the agenda (§8).
- [ ] I know which of my old tasks I will delegate first, and to whom (§10).
- [ ] I have told my old team — and my new boss — what "done" looks like for my first 90 days (§3).

## 3. The First 90 Days: The Transition Discipline Applied

### 3.1 The cross-ref, in one paragraph

[the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) is the full discipline: the break-even point (the moment the organization needs you as much as you need the job), the STARS model (start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success), the learning agenda, waves of early wins, and the alliance map. This section does not re-derive any of it. What it adds is the **first-time-manager operationalization**: the same 30/60/90 structure, but with team-building actions attached to every phase, because for a first-time manager the transition *is* the team.

### 3.2 The first-time manager's 30/60/90

| Phase | Transition focus (cross-ref) | Team-building actions | Success measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Days 1–30: Learn and diagnose** | Learning agenda; STARS diagnosis; five conversations with your boss ([the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) §6–§9) | Intake 1:1 with every report (the questions in §8.5); observe every ritual before changing it; map the stakeholders; draft the team assessment (keep/develop/replace triage — ⚠ framing per sibling, see §16); audit the last two quarters' outcomes | Written learning agenda; a one-page team diagnosis; every report met 1:1; zero changes made to rituals |
| **Days 31–60: Stabilize and win** | Early wins in waves; negotiate resources ([the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) §10) | Run the charter workshop (§4.4); install or fix the rituals (§5); hold the first blameless retro (§5.4); start weekly 1:1s with a shared agenda doc (§8); delegate your first three tasks (§10.4) | A ratified charter the team references; rituals running on cadence; one early win shipped that the team owns |
| **Days 61–90: Align and accelerate** | Alignment; alliances; accelerate everyone ([the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) §11–§15) | Lock team goals to the bank's objectives (§11.2); run the first performance-conversation round (§11.4); start the evidence log for review season; hold the first team health check (anonymized, includes a safety question — [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §8.3) | Team goals published and visible; every report has had a goals-and-development conversation; health check baseline recorded |

### 3.3 Before day one

The moves that cost nothing and pay the most:

- **Read.** The org chart, the team's last two quarters of delivery artifacts, the open incidents, the previous manager's notes (if any), the team's charter or mission statement (if any — its absence is diagnostic).
- **Draft your learning agenda** before you start, per [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) §2.3 — questions, not answers.
- **Have the five conversations with your boss** early: situational diagnosis, expectations, style, resources, personal development (⚠ labels per the sibling's own audit).
- **Do not have opinions yet.** The single biggest transition trap for a promoted insider is arriving with answers about a team you think you already know — you knew it as a peer, which is precisely the wrong vantage point.

### 3.4 The first week, hour by hour

1. **Monday:** meet your boss (expectations, the five conversations); block your calendar for the week's 1:1s.
2. **Tuesday–Thursday:** intake 1:1s with every report (45 minutes each — see §8.5 for the questions); observe the standup, the planning meeting, the retro; read the board.
3. **Friday:** the first team meeting as *manager* — agenda: what you're doing in the first 30 days, what you will *not* change yet, and one question to the team: "what is the one thing I should know about this team that nobody has told me?" Then shut up and listen.

### 3.5 The first month's moves

- **Finish the intake round** and write the one-page team diagnosis: where the team is on the K&S performance curve (working group / pseudo-team / potential team / real team — [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §1.2), who the anchors and the risks are, where the safety gaps are.
- **Agree the 30-day plan with your boss** in writing — the transition discipline's "negotiate success" applied to your own ramp.
- **Do not hire, fire, or restructure** in month one unless there is a fire. (Turnaround STARS situations are the exception — [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md) §8.)
- **Start the charter conversation** informally in 1:1s ("what should we agree on as a team?") so the workshop in §4.4 is a ratification, not a surprise.

## 4. The Team Charter: Purpose, Goals, and Working Agreements

### 4.1 What a charter is (and the cross-ref)

A charter is the team's own written answer to three questions — **why do we exist, what are we trying to achieve, and how do we work together** — which is the Katzenbach & Smith specification operationalized: small number, complementary skills, common purpose, performance goals, mutual accountability ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §2; the charter mechanics live in [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) §2.4). The research point that makes charters non-optional: the *purpose must be shaped by the team, not received* — a mandate handed down produces compliance, not commitment ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §2.2). For a first-time manager, the charter is also the cheapest way to establish that you lead by facilitation, not decree.

### 4.2 The three charter elements

| Element | The question it answers | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **Mission (purpose)** | Why does this team exist, and for whom? | One or two sentences in the team's own words, with a named customer/outcome |
| **Goals (performance goals)** | What will we achieve this quarter/half, and how will we know? | 2–4 specific, measurable, challenging goals derived from the mission (§11.2) |
| **Working agreements (common approach)** | How do we behave with each other? | 5–8 explicit norms: decision rules, conflict rules, definition of done, communication norms |

### 4.3 The charter workshop — run it, don't write it

The event craft is covered by [facilitation_skills_guide.md](facilitation_skills_guide.md) and [workshop_methodology_guide.md](workshop_methodology_guide.md) — facilitation technique, room design, energy management, and the facilitator's toolkit are all there. The charter-specific structure, in the repo's worked-example tradition:

- **Session 1 — Purpose (90 minutes, facilitated by the new manager).** The team rewrites the inherited mission in its own words. Prompts: "who is our customer?", "what would this bank lose if we disappeared tomorrow?", "what do we want to be known for in a year?" The manager writes nothing on the flip chart; the team does. Park every "how" question — the how comes in session 2.
- **Session 2 — Roles and norms (90 minutes, one week later).** The ownership map (who owns what domain — cross-ref RACI in §12.2), the definition of done, and the working agreements. Every agreement is phrased as a *behavior* ("disagreement happens in the room, not in the corridor"), not a value ("we value honesty").
- **Ratification (30 minutes).** The team reviews the draft as a whole, argues, and adopts it. The ratification ritual matters: a charter nobody explicitly adopted is a poster.

### 4.4 Working agreements that actually work

From the repo's Cymbal worked examples and the canon (synthesized here; each is a behavior a first-time manager can hold the team to):

- **Disagreement happens in the room.** No ambushing in side channels; anyone can call a decision review.
- **No blame in retros.** Incidents are examined as systems, not culprits (the Edmondson move, §6).
- **Definition of done is public** and applies to everyone, including the manager.
- **Bad news travels fast.** The person who surfaces a problem early is thanked, not shot — this is the single highest-leverage agreement for a new manager.
- **Own your handoffs.** Every handoff has a named owner and a done-criteria.

### 4.5 Charter pitfalls for the first-time manager

- **Writing it yourself.** A lead-authored charter is a directive in disguise; the team will treat it as such ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §9.6).
- **Charter as poster.** No goals, no ratification, no reference in planning — the charter dies within a month. Reference it in every planning meeting (§5).
- **Charter before diagnosis.** Running a charter workshop in week one, before you have listened, is theater. The intake round (§3.4) comes first.
- **Ignoring the org's real constraints.** At a bank, the change board, compliance, and the matrix are real; the charter should state how the team works *within* them, or it will be cynicism fuel ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §9.4).

## 5. Team Norms and Rituals: The Operating Cadence

### 5.1 The cross-ref, in one paragraph

The full treatment of meetings, retros, and team dynamics lives in [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) §8 (one-on-ones and meetings) and §2 (team dynamics frameworks), with the facilitation craft in [facilitation_skills_guide.md](facilitation_skills_guide.md) and [workshop_methodology_guide.md](workshop_methodology_guide.md), and the team-health measurement in [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §8.3. This section is the first-time manager's decision list: which rituals to run, at what cadence, and what each one is *for*.

### 5.2 The meeting diet

The rule of thumb for a new manager: **every recurring meeting must have a job, and the job must not be "status."** The canonical bank-squad diet:

| Ritual | Cadence | Owner | The job | The anti-pattern to kill |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Standup** | Daily, 15 min | Team rotates | Coordination: blockers, handoffs, today's plan — *not* status to the manager | The manager's morning interrogation |
| **Planning** | Biweekly/fortnightly | Team + product | Commit to the next sprint's goals; reference the charter's goals | Planning as ticket-shuffling with no goal conversation |
| **Review / demo** | Biweekly | Team | Show outcomes to stakeholders; celebrate; adjust | The demo as a manager's slide-deck report |
| **Retro** | Fortnightly | Team (facilitated) | Improve the way we work; own actions; measure the loop (§5.4) | Skipped when busy — the classic death spiral |
| **1:1** | Weekly, 45–60 min | Report | The report's agenda: issues, coaching, career (§8) | Status update / cancellation |
| **Team health check** | Quarterly | Manager, anonymized | Measure safety, clarity, meaning, impact (§11.5) | The non-anonymous "how are we doing?" that returns polite greens |

### 5.3 Norms are caught, not taught

Norms form from what the team *observes being rewarded and punished*, not from what is posted. The first-time manager sets norms by micro-behavior from week one: what you tolerate in a meeting (interruptions, blame, silence), what you do when a deadline slips (inquire or punish), who you thank publicly (the hero or the helper). This is the "the team calibrates to what the leader punishes, not what the leader preaches" rule ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §9.5, [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) §2.5).

### 5.4 The retro that works

The retro craft is in [workshop_methodology_guide.md](workshop_methodology_guide.md); the first-time-manager version has three non-negotiables:

1. **Blameless by design.** The facilitator's first rule: no person-talk, only process-talk ("what in our system allowed this?"). Edmondson's research made this the standard move (§6).
2. **Owned actions.** Every retro ends with 1–3 actions, each with a named owner and a check-in date; the next retro opens by reviewing them. An action-less retro is a venting session.
3. **The manager participates but does not dominate.** Your job in the retro is to be a normal member with a bigger mouth to keep shut — and to *not react defensively* when the team names something you did. Your reaction is the retro's real curriculum.

### 5.5 Cadence design for a first-time manager

- **Right-size to the team, not the template.** A 7-person integration squad needs the full diet; a 3-person platform pair needs half of it. The first month is for observing, the second for adjusting.
- **Protect maker time.** Block team deep-work windows; meetings go in the afternoon where possible; never let the meeting diet grow to fill the week ([time_energy_management_guide.md](time_energy_management_guide.md) is the repo's deeper treatment).
- **Your calendar is the model.** If you cancel 1:1s and retros, the team learns those are optional. The first-time manager's calendar is the team's most-watched artifact (§2.4).

## 6. Psychological Safety: The Load-Bearing Wall

### 6.1 The definition and the origin

Amy Edmondson's 1999 paper is the primary source for team psychological safety, and it is verified this run (✅ — *Administrative Science Quarterly* 44(2), June 1999, pp. 350–383; the definition and the field study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company confirmed via the paper's own abstract at SAGE, the MIT-hosted copy, and the sibling guide's verification appendix in [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md)). The paper's definition is the one this guide uses throughout:

> "A shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking."

Two origin points are worth separating, because they are routinely conflated:

- **Kahn (1990)** coined *psychological safety* at the individual level — the sense that one can "employ one's self without fear of negative consequences to self-image, status, or career" — in "Psychological Conditions of Personal Engagement and Disengagement at Work," *Academy of Management Journal* 33(4): 692–724 (✅ verified via the JSTOR and APA records this run).
- **Edmondson (1999)** operationalized the construct at the *team* level and showed it predicts learning behavior — asking questions, seeking help, talking about errors, experimentation — which in turn predicts performance. Safety enables speaking up, and speaking up is how teams learn ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §6.2).

### 6.2 Why it is the load-bearing wall

Two independent evidence streams put psychological safety at the center of the HPT discipline:

- **Edmondson's field study (1999).** Psychological safety predicted learning behavior, learning behavior predicted performance, and team efficacy was not significant once safety was controlled (✅ abstract-verified).
- **Google Project Aristotle (2012–2015).** Google studied 180+ teams and found psychological safety the #1 of the five dynamics (safety, dependability, structure & clarity, meaning, impact); who was on the team mattered less than how the team worked (✅ verified via Google re:Work and multiple secondary records; popularized via the NYT Magazine piece, Feb 2016).

For a first-time manager the mechanism matters more than the finding: **safety is what converts a group into a learning system.** Without it, errors are hidden until they are expensive, questions go unasked until they are overdue, and the loudest voice sets the technical direction. In a bank, where error-reporting is both compliance-relevant and career-relevant, the absence of safety has a specific cost: incidents get worked around instead of reported, and the same root causes recur in the next release.

### 6.3 Safety is not niceness

The most common first-time-manager misreading is that psychological safety means "everyone is comfortable and we never disagree." Edmondson's own framing — in *The Fearless Organization* (Wiley, 2018; ✅ publisher and framing verified via the sibling's appendix) — is a two-axis matrix: **safety × standards.** High safety with low standards is a comfort zone; low safety with high standards is an anxiety zone; low safety with low standards is apathy. The high-performance quadrant is **high safety + high standards** — people speak up *and* are held to demanding expectations. The first-time manager's job is to raise both axes at once: make it safe to say "I don't know" while making clear that "I don't know" is not a resting place.

### 6.4 Edmondson's three levers, operationalized

*The Fearless Organization*'s practical levers (✅ framing per the sibling's appendix; presented here as the first-time-manager operationalization):

1. **Frame the work.** Name the uncertainty out loud: "this migration has never been done at this scale in this bank; we are going to find problems, and finding them early is the job." When difficulty is framed as expected, surfacing problems becomes competence rather than confession.
2. **Invite participation.** Ask the question, then *wait*. Explicitly solicit the dissenting view — "who sees it differently?" In the first months, track who has not spoken in meetings and deliberately create a channel for their voice.
3. **Respond productively.** The response to a mistake, a question, or a challenge is the real curriculum. Acknowledgment ("good catch — thank you"), curiosity ("what did we learn?"), and follow-through ("and here is the action we are taking") teach more than any charter line. The classic killer: punish the messenger once — everyone sees it, and the safety level resets to zero.

### 6.5 What kills safety in the first 90 days

The first-time manager is at peak risk of killing safety precisely while trying to establish authority:

- **The first retro reaction.** If the team names a problem that implicates you and you defend, explain, or deflect, the retro becomes a theater of compliance. Rule: in your first six retros, respond to criticism with "tell me more," then act visibly on one named item.
- **Punishing bad news.** The person who surfaces a slipped deadline or a regulatory risk is thanked, not interrogated. One punishment — a raised voice, a "whose fault?", a skeptical silence — teaches the whole team to route around you.
- **Laughing at a question.** A nervous laugh at an earnest question reads as ridicule. Treat every question as legitimate; if the answer is "I don't know," say exactly that and bring the answer back.
- **Asking for feedback and reacting badly.** Soliciting input and then rebutting or ignoring it is worse than never asking (§9.4).
- **Favoritism, real or perceived.** The old peer group, the new pet, the star IC who gets all the airtime — the team reads differential access as differential safety (§13.8).

### 6.6 The safety audit (quarter one)

- [ ] I can name the last time a report disagreed with me in front of the team — and what I did with it.
- [ ] The retro has produced an action that changed how we work, and I can point to it.
- [ ] An error was surfaced by the team this month, and the response was learning, not blame.
- [ ] I know who has not spoken in the last three team meetings — and I have a plan for their voice.
- [ ] My 1:1s contain more listening than talking (§8).

## 7. Trust-Building: The Behavioral Levers

### 7.1 What trust is (and the cross-ref)

The research definition this guide uses is Mayer, Davis & Schoorman's (✅ — "An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust," *Academy of Management Review* 20(3), July 1995; DOI 10.5465/amr.1995.9508080335, verified via the scholarly record this run): trust is **the willingness to be vulnerable to another party based on positive expectations of their intentions or behavior.** Note what that makes trust: not a feeling but a *calculation* the other party makes about you, continuously. The repo's deeper treatments live in [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §8.1 and [360_management_guide.md](360_management_guide.md); this section is the first-time-manager lever list.

### 7.2 The three components

The Mayer–Davis–Schoorman model decomposes trustworthiness into three factors, which map directly onto what a new manager is being evaluated on:

| Factor | The question the team is asking | How a first-time manager demonstrates it |
|---|---|---|
| **Ability** | Can you actually do this job? | Domain and org mastery: knowing the systems, the constraints, the stakeholders; asking sharp questions in week one (§3.4) |
| **Benevolence** | Do you have our interests at heart? | Protecting the team from above, crediting them outward, never throwing anyone under the bus, caring about careers (§8, §11.4) |
| **Integrity** | Do you do what you say? | Keeping every small commitment: follow-through on follow-ups, consistent standards, confidentiality of 1:1s |

### 7.3 The asymmetry problem

A promoted insider does not inherit trust — they inherit *relationships*, which are not the same thing. Former peers watch for favoritism and for whether the old friendship survives the new accountability line (§13.4); inherited reports watch for whether the new boss will merely repeat the old one. The result: **the first-time manager starts at a trust deficit and earns trust through predictability.** The fastest trust-building sequence is boring and reliable:

1. Say what you will do (small, concrete commitments).
2. Do it, on time, every time.
3. When you cannot, say so *before* the deadline, not after.
4. Repeat for sixty days. (§5.3: the team calibrates to behavior, not posters.)

### 7.4 The two-way street

Trust in the manager→report direction is equally load-bearing: the manager who does not trust the team gets the team that does not deserve trust — micromanagement creates the dependency it fears (§10.6). Delegation is the trust signal that matters most: **every delegated task is a deposit in the trust account; every re-do is a withdrawal.** A first-time manager who wants a trusted team must be willing to look exposed when a delegated task fails — and to own that failure outward (§10).

### 7.5 Trust repair

Trust breaks fastest through inconsistency (words vs. actions), confidentiality leaks, and public blame. Repair follows a known sequence: **acknowledge the specific breach without qualifiers → apologize without excuses → change the observable behavior → let time do the rest.** What does not work: explaining why the team misread you. Budget the repair to take roughly an order of magnitude longer than the breach took.

### 7.6 The trust checklist (first 60 days)

- [ ] A public commitment I made in week one is done.
- [ ] No 1:1 content has leaked — not to the team, not to my boss (except where law or risk requires otherwise) — and the team knows it.
- [ ] I have said "I don't know" out loud in front of the team at least once — and brought the answer back.
- [ ] I have credited a report's idea to them in a forum where their name is attached, not mine.
- [ ] I have delegated something I am better at than the person I gave it to (§10.4).

## 8. The 1:1 Practice: The Manager's Most Powerful Tool

### 8.1 The cross-ref

The full craft — structure, cadence, note-taking, the manager's side of the agenda — lives in [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) §8 (one-on-ones and meetings), with the career-conversation layer in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) and the coaching layer in [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md). This section is the first-time-manager operationalization: the structure, the intake questions promised in §3.2 and §3.4, and the pitfalls.

### 8.2 Why the 1:1 is the most powerful tool

The 1:1 is the only recurring artifact where the two jobs (§2.3) — get results through others, grow the people — happen at the same time. It is also the cheapest early-warning system the manager will ever own: most team problems (safety gaps, conflict, disengagement, flight risk) show up in 1:1s months before they show up anywhere else. Grove's *High Output Management* made the recurring, report-owned one-on-one a canonical management practice (✅ practice documented in the book per multiple summaries; exact prescription wording ⚠ — see §16).

### 8.3 The structure

- **Cadence:** weekly, 45–60 minutes, sacred. Skipping two in a row is how the team learns the 1:1 is optional.
- **Ownership:** the report owns the agenda. The manager's default opening is "what's on your mind?" — followed by silence.
- **The four buckets** (the report's agenda cycles through them; the manager's job is to make sure all four actually occur across a month):
  1. **Issues and blocks** — what is in the way, today.
  2. **Projects and priorities** — what they are working on, what changed, what they need.
  3. **Coaching and development** — skills, stretch, feedback from recent work.
  4. **Career** — trajectory, aspirations, what the ladder looks like here (§11.4).
- **The talking ratio:** the manager's share should shrink toward roughly 20–30% over the first quarter (⚠ heuristic, not a research finding — see §16). A 1:1 where the manager talked is a status meeting with extra steps.
- **Notes and follow-through:** one running doc per report; every 1:1 ends with "what did we agree?", and the next one opens with it. Nothing destroys the 1:1 faster than agreed actions that never happen.

### 8.4 The shared agenda doc

One running document per report, shared, with three sections: *their agenda* (they add items any time — the doc is the async channel between meetings), *the manager's notes* (prep and follow-ups), and *agreements* (dated commitments from both sides). The doc does three jobs at once: it makes the 1:1 a continuous conversation rather than a weekly cold start, it becomes the evidence log for review season (§11.4), and it signals that the manager takes the relationship seriously enough to write things down.

### 8.5 The intake 1:1 questions (days 1–30)

The questions referenced in §3.2 and §3.4 — the intake round: 45 minutes per report, the manager talks as little as possible (⚠ synthesized from practice and the sibling guides; not from a single cited source — see §16):

1. **The job:** "Walk me through what you actually do — the work, the systems, the handoffs." (You want their map, not the org chart.)
2. **The team:** "How does this team really work? Where does it work well, and where does it grind?"
3. **The manager:** "What did the previous manager do that worked? What would you not want repeated?"
4. **The bank:** "What does the bank or division not understand about this team's work?"
5. **The person:** "What do you want to be doing in a year? What would a great year look like for you?"
6. **The ask:** "What is the one thing I should know that nobody has told me?" — then silence. (The Friday team-meeting version from §3.4 is the group echo; this is where the real answers live.)
7. **The offer:** "What do you need from me to do your best work?"

Two intake disciplines: **share your notes** (a report should never wonder what you wrote down), and **never use intake answers against anyone** — the intake round's confidentiality is the seed of §7's trust.

### 8.6 The 1:1 pitfalls

- **The status report.** If your 1:1s are project updates you could get from the board, you are doing them wrong — move updates out, keep coaching and career in.
- **The cancellation reflex.** "I'm too busy this week" — the message received is "your growth is my last priority." Cancel anything else first (§5.5).
- **The manager's monologue.** The 1:1 is not your debrief of the week's meetings.
- **No follow-through.** The fastest way to prove the 1:1 is theater.
- **Skipping career.** It is the bucket that gets dropped first and the one that keeps people — three months of agenda-only 1:1s and the report starts interviewing.
- **The proxy 1:1.** Meeting the "important" reports more often than the quiet ones is how the inner circle forms (§13.8).

## 9. Feedback Loops: Giving and Receiving

### 9.1 Why feedback is the growth engine

Feedback is the mechanism that makes §2.3's second job — grow the people — operational. It is also the highest-variance skill in the first-time-manager toolkit: done well, it compounds; done badly or not at all, it produces the surprises that end careers (theirs, and eventually yours). The repo's full treatments are [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) (feedback models) and [conflict_management_guide.md](conflict_management_guide.md) (difficult conversations); this section is the first-time-manager version: the model, the cadence, the receiving half, and the difficult cases.

### 9.2 The model: SBI plus radical candor

- **SBI (Situation–Behavior–Impact).** Describe the *situation* (when and where), the observable *behavior* (what was said or done — no interpretation), and the *impact* (what the behavior caused). SBI is widely documented as the Center for Creative Leadership's feedback model (⚠ the exact original CCL publication could not be re-pinned this run — the CCL site's article pages returned 404 during verification; see §16). Its discipline is the part that matters: it forces behavior, not character, into the sentence — "in Tuesday's demo, you interrupted Mei three times, and the effect was that her point never landed" rather than "you are dominating."
- **Radical candor (✅ — Kim Scott, *Radical Candor: Be a Kick-Ass Boss Without Losing Your Humanity*, St. Martin's Press, March 2017, verified via the publisher record this run).** Scott's two axes — *care personally* and *challenge directly* — give the first-time manager the quadrant map: praise and criticism are both required, and the failure quadrants (ruinous empathy, obnoxious aggression, manipulative insincerity) are each recognizable first-year behaviors. The operational rule: feedback that is not both caring and direct is not feedback; it is something else.

### 9.3 Praise and recognition as feedback

Recognition is feedback too, and it is the half most first-time managers starve. The patterns that work: **public** (the forum the person values — team meeting, demo, retro), **specific** (the behavior, not the vibe: "the way you walked the change board through the rollback plan cut two weeks of review friction"), **timely** (within days, not at review season), and **tied to the team's goals** (§11.2) so the team learns what "good" means. Recognition inflation — praising everything equally — is how recognition stops meaning anything; the standard stays high (§6.3) and the praise marks the moments the standard was met under difficulty.

### 9.4 The receiving half

The first-time manager's own feedback loop is the model the whole team copies (§5.3, §6.5):

- **Ask explicitly and repeatedly:** "what should I stop, start, or continue?" in 1:1s, at the end of retros, and after any visible manager decision.
- **Do not rebut.** The sentence that ends the flow is "tell me more" — not "but here's why."
- **Respond visibly.** One piece of received feedback acted on, named in the next team meeting, is worth a hundred feedback requests. The team learns that telling the manager the truth works.

### 9.5 The cadence

- **In-the-moment beats batched.** A behavior named the same week it happened is coaching; the same behavior named at review season is an accusation.
- **The 1:1 is the default channel** for developmental feedback; the retro is the channel for team-level feedback; the team meeting is for recognition.
- **No feedback sandwiches.** The praise-criticism-praise wrapper trains the team to hear any praise as the warning shot of criticism. Give each item straight (§9.2).
- **The review-season principle:** if the annual review contains a single surprise, the feedback system failed, not the employee — the evidence log (§11.4) exists precisely to make surprises impossible.

### 9.6 Difficult feedback

Underperformance, missed commitments, and behavior problems are the cases that separate managers who last from managers who flinch. The first-time-manager rules:

- **Address it early, at the first sign** — the first missed commitment is the cheapest moment; the pattern is expensive.
- **Describe behavior and impact, not character** (§9.2); name the standard the team agreed (§4.4) when there is one.
- **Make it a conversation, not a verdict:** "here is what I observed, here is the impact, help me understand what happened, here is what we need to see change."
- **Escalate deliberately, never vengefully:** coaching → written expectations → formal performance management, per [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md)'s performance section and the bank's HR process; the PIP is a tool for clarity, not punishment.
- **"Clear is kind"** — the maxim that honest, direct feedback is the respectful act, while vague niceness robs the person of the chance to improve (⚠ popularly attributed to Brené Brown's *Dare to Lead*, 2018; exact origin not re-verified this run — see §16).

## 10. Delegation and Empowerment: Letting Go to Scale

### 10.1 The cross-ref

Delegation is treated deeply in [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md) (the letting-go practice of the tech-lead rung), [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md) (the delegation frameworks), and [situational_leadership_slii_guide.md](situational_leadership_slii_guide.md) (matching style to readiness). This section is the first-time-manager operationalization: what to delegate first, how to run the delegation conversation, and the two failure modes.

### 10.2 Why delegation is the hardest skill

Delegation is the mechanism that makes §2.1's Grove thesis real: if the manager's output is the team's output, then the manager's only route to output is through delegation — and the first-time manager's identity is still invested in doing. Every re-do of a delegated task is a double loss: the work did not grow the report, and the manager just taught the team that "delegation" means "the boss will take it back." The alpha-geek pattern (§2.2, §13.2) is delegation's opposite number, and the first quarter is where it either gets broken or becomes the manager's permanent style.

### 10.3 The delegation ladder

Match the degree of authority to the person's readiness for this specific task (cross-ref [situational_leadership_slii_guide.md](situational_leadership_slii_guide.md)):

| Rung | What the person gets | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Do it, show me** | Execute a fully specified task; report back | New to the domain; first exposure |
| **2. Do it, tell me what you did** | Execute with some latitude; debrief | Building confidence; standard work |
| **3. Do it, decide how** | Own the method; manager owns the outcome call | Competent; ready to stretch |
| **4. Decide and do, tell me if it fails** | Full autonomy with a pre-agreed tripwire | Strong performer, aligned judgment |
| **5. Own it entirely** | Outcome ownership, escalation only at the boundaries | The team's anchors; the manager's eventual replacements |

The first-time-manager rule: **delegate outcomes, not methods.** Specify what good looks like, the constraints (budget, compliance, change board), and the checkpoint cadence — then let the person find the path. Delegating the method as well as the outcome is just outsourcing with extra steps.

### 10.4 What to delegate first (the first three tasks)

The plan referenced in §3.2 — the first three delegations, chosen deliberately in days 31–60:

1. **A task you are personally best at.** Pick the thing that hurts most to hand over — the code review pass, the incident write-up, the stakeholder briefing. Delegating your own strength is the loudest possible signal that you have converted (§2.1); it is also the one that exercises your coaching muscle hardest, because you genuinely know what good looks like.
2. **A task you are sick of.** The recurring chore (release notes, board hygiene, demo prep) is someone's growth opportunity; hand it over with the purpose attached — "this is how we learn what the release actually contains."
3. **A visible task with a named owner.** Something the bank's stakeholders can see (a status review, a stakeholder demo slot, a section of the roadmap write-up). This is how the team's faces — not just yours — become the interface to the organization, and it is the practical antidote to the manager-as-single-point-of-contact failure mode (§12.4).

For each: run the delegation conversation (§10.5), set a checkpoint, and *do not take it back* even if the first attempt is rougher than your own would have been.

### 10.5 The delegation conversation

A complete delegation hand-off answers six questions out loud:

1. **What** — the outcome, and what "done" looks like (public definition of done, §4.4).
2. **Why** — the purpose and the stakeholder; the person cannot make judgment calls without it.
3. **Constraints** — budget, compliance, change-board windows, the org's real limits (§4.5).
4. **Authority** — what they can decide alone and what needs a check-in (the rung from §10.3).
5. **Checkpoints** — when you will touch base and what triggers an early conversation (the tripwire: "if the change-board date slips, tell me the same day").
6. **Support** — what you will provide (access, air cover, coaching) and the honest answer to "what happens if it fails?" — the answer is "you own the attempt, I own the outcome, outward" (§7.4).

### 10.6 Micromanagement vs. abandonment

The two failure modes are symmetric and both teach the same lesson — that the manager does not trust the team:

- **Micromanagement** (checking in daily, re-doing work, demanding pre-approval on trivial calls) produces the dependency it fears: people stop deciding, wait for the manager, and the manager's bottleneck becomes the team's bottleneck. The tell: your 1:1s are full of status the report already sent you.
- **Abandonment** (delegating without the conversation, no checkpoints, feedback only at failure) produces drift and surprise: people make judgment calls without the constraints, and the manager discovers it at review season (§9.5).

The corrective is the same: **a delegation is a conversation with checkpoints, not a hand-off followed by silence or surveillance.** Cadence the checkpoints to the rung — tight at rung 1, loose at rung 5 — and loosen them as the person's track record grows. The dignity of risk is the point: a team that never fails small will fail big, in public, at the worst moment.

## 11. Goals, Performance Management, and Motivation

### 11.1 The cross-ref

Alignment and goal-setting mechanics live in [balanced_scorecard_guide.md](balanced_scorecard_guide.md); the full performance-management lifecycle (hiring to reviews) lives in [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md); the delivery metrics (DORA, SPACE) live in [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §7. This section is the first-time-manager version: team goals, the performance-conversation round, the health check, and the motivation research that explains why the standard approaches fail.

### 11.2 Team goals: lock to the bank's objectives

The goal referenced in §3.2 — the day-61 move — is to convert the charter's mission (§4) into **2–4 specific, measurable, challenging team goals** that visibly connect to the division's and the bank's objectives, and to publish them where everyone can see them. The OKR habit is the repo-standard vehicle: an *objective* (the direction, qualitative) and 2–3 *key results* (the evidence, quantitative). The modern popularization is John Doerr's *Measure What Matters* (✅ book and subtitle verified this run via the scholarly review record; ⚠ publisher/edition details not independently re-verified — see §16), which credits Andy Grove's Intel "iMBOs" as the origin — consistent with the Grove thesis in §2.1. Two first-time-manager rules:

- **The team's goals are the team's, not yours.** The manager proposes, the team argues, the manager ratifies — per §4.1's "purpose shaped by the team, not received." A goal the team does not own is a target, and targets get gamed.
- **Goals are the scoreboard (§1.4, §2.1).** If the team's goals are not the thing the manager reviews against, the real scoreboard is something else — usually tickets — and the goals become decoration.

### 11.3 Goal quality: SMART as a check, not a strategy

The SMART criteria — Specific, Measurable, Assignable, Realistic, Time-bound — originate with George T. Doran's "There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives," *Management Review*, November 1981 (✅ verified via the bibliographic record this run). Use SMART as a *quality check* on goals the team already finds meaningful — never as the mechanism that generates them. A goal that is perfectly measurable but meaningless to the team produces compliance and metric-gaming; meaning comes first (this is also the empirical finding of the motivation research in §11.6 and Aristotle's "meaning" dynamic, §6.2).

### 11.4 The performance-conversation round

The round referenced in §3.2 — the first one runs in days 61–90, and then quarterly:

- **Every report gets one dedicated goals-and-development conversation per quarter**, separate from the weekly 1:1: where they are against their goals, what they want next, what they need from you.
- **The evidence log makes reviews boring.** The shared agenda doc (§8.4), the delegation outcomes (§10), and the recognition moments (§9.3) accumulate all year; the review meeting then *confirms* what everyone already knows (§9.5's no-surprises principle).
- **The calibration conversation** — comparing assessments across managers — is where fairness actually gets manufactured; ask your boss how the division calibrates before your first review season.
- **The underperformance conversation** is part of this round, not an ambush: coaching → written expectations → formal process, per §9.6 and [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md).

### 11.5 The team health check

The check referenced in §3.2 and §5.2 — quarterly, **anonymized**, and it must include a safety question:

- Measure the team against the Aristotle dynamics (§6.2): safety ("I can bring up problems without fear of blame"), dependability ("people here do what they say"), structure & clarity ("roles and priorities are clear"), meaning ("my work matters"), impact ("my work contributes to the bank's outcomes"). Cross-ref the instrument discussion in [high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §8.3.
- **The manager never sees individual answers** — an external tool or a trusted facilitator collects them; the team sees the aggregate; the retro owns the action list. Anonymity is the entire point: the non-anonymous "how are we doing?" returns polite greens (§5.2).
- **Trend, don't snapshot.** The first baseline (day 90) is a baseline; the signal is the trajectory across quarters, and the commitment is that every dip produces an owned action.

### 11.6 Motivation: the research the first-time manager needs

Three verified strands explain most first-year motivation failures:

- **Herzberg's two-factor theory (✅ — developed by Frederick Herzberg, with Mausner and Snyderman, *The Motivation to Work*, 1959).** *Hygiene factors* (pay, conditions, policy, job security) cause dissatisfaction when absent but do not cause motivation when present; *motivators* (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, growth) cause motivation. The first-time-manager reading: fixing hygiene (fair pay, decent tools, sane hours) is *necessary* — a team with broken hygiene is demotivated no matter what — but hygiene fixes alone will never *motivate*; motivation comes from the work and its recognition. Managers who mistake hygiene for motivation keep buying pizza for a team that wants meaningful work.
- **Self-determination theory (✅ — Ryan & Deci, "Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being," *American Psychologist* 55(1), 2000, pp. 68–78, verified via DOI 10.1037/0003-066x.55.1.68 this run).** Intrinsic motivation runs on three innate needs: **autonomy** (choice over how), **competence** (mastery and progress), and **relatedness** (connection to people and purpose). This is the research version of §10's whole argument: autonomy is not a perk, it is a need; a manager who removes it (micromanagement) is directly dismantling motivation.
- **Job characteristics theory (✅ — Hackman & Oldham, "Motivation through the design of work: test of a theory," *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance*, 1976, verified via DOI 10.1016/0030-5073(76)90016-7 this run).** The work itself motivates through five core dimensions — skill variety, task identity (doing a whole piece), task significance (the work matters), autonomy, and feedback from the work. The first-time-manager application is the **task-design pass**: before blaming a person for low engagement, check whether the work was stripped of variety, identity, significance, autonomy, and feedback — most disengagement is a design problem.

The synthesis: **motivation is not something a manager injects; it is something the work and the context either support or suppress.** The manager's levers are the ones the research names: meaningful goals (§11.2), autonomy (§10), competence through feedback and stretch (§9, §10.4), recognition (§9.3), and hygiene that is good enough to get out of the way.

### 11.7 Recognition: the team's reward architecture

- **Recognition is a behavior, not a budget item.** The channels are the meeting diet (§5.2): demo shout-outs, retro appreciations, the 1:1 "this is what I noticed you do well," and the review's evidence log.
- **Recognize the helper, not just the hero.** The team calibrates to what you celebrate (§5.3): if only the visible hero gets praised, the invisible load-bearing work (code review, docs, incident support, onboarding) quietly dies.
- **Career currency is the deepest recognition.** A promotion path, a stretch assignment, a seat at a stakeholder table — these outrank any award. The first-time manager who wants a motivated team runs the career conversation (§8.3's fourth bucket) as if it matters, because it does.

## 12. The Team-Performance Levers: Accountability, Decisions, Communication, Conflict, Culture

### 12.1 The five levers

The levers referenced in §1.1 and §2.3 — the mechanisms that convert individual effort into collective output. For each: what it is, the failure mode, and the first-time manager's move.

| Lever | What it is | The failure mode | The first-time manager's move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accountability** | People hold themselves and each other to the team's commitments | The manager is the only accountability (everyone reports to you, no one answers to each other) | Mutual accountability: public commitments, peer review of commitments, the standard named in the charter (§12.2) |
| **Decisions** | A clear answer to who decides, how, and when | Decision by committee, by silence, or by the loudest voice; decisions that rot | A decision rule per decision type; a decision log; disagree-and-commit (§12.3) |
| **Communication** | Information flows up, down, and sideways without distortion | The manager as single point of contact; corridor news; surprise in either direction | The communication charter; no-surprises rule; overcommunication in transition (§12.4) |
| **Conflict** | Task conflict that sharpens the work without becoming personal | Suppressed conflict (Lencioni's fear of conflict) or personal conflict that outlives the issue | The room-not-the-corridor rule; task/relationship separation; the difficult-conversation craft (§12.5) |
| **Culture** | The residue of what the team repeatedly rewards and punishes | Culture by accident — the inherited habits nobody chose | Deliberate norm-setting through behavior, celebration, and response to bad news (§12.6) |

### 12.2 Accountability: mutual, not managerial

The fifth component of the Katzenbach & Smith definition is **mutual accountability** — peers holding peers (and themselves) to the team's commitments, not merely each person answering to the manager ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §2). The first-time manager builds it structurally:

- **The ownership map (RACI).** For every outcome the team owns, name who is *responsible* (does the work), *accountable* (answers for it), *consulted*, and *informed* — the RACI mechanics live in [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md), and the ownership map is the §4.3 session-2 deliverable (cross-ref as promised there). A team where "everyone owns it" owns nothing.
- **Public commitments.** Goals and task owners are visible on the board; commitments are made in the room, to each other, not privately to the manager — that is what makes accountability *mutual* rather than *vertical*.
- **The accountability conversation is about the standard, not the person.** "We agreed the definition of done includes the rollback test; this release shipped without it" — the team's own agreement (§4.4) is the referee, which keeps the manager from being the only enforcer.
- **The DRI habit.** One named directly-responsible individual per outcome (cross-ref [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md)'s delegation chapter) kills the diffusion of responsibility in handoffs.

### 12.3 Decisions: the decision log and the three modes

Most first-year decision pain is not wrong decisions; it is **unclear decision processes** — people do not know who decides, so they stall, or they decide, and the team finds out sideways. The fixes:

- **Name the mode per decision, out loud.** *Command* (you decide — for speed, crisis, or where you own the accountability), *consult* (you decide after listening — the default for most team decisions), *consensus* (the team decides — for charter-level and norm-level questions, §4). The failure is mixing them: consulting for hours and then commanding, or commanding a decision the team believed was consensus.
- **The decision log.** One visible document: the decision, the date, who decided, who was consulted, and the review date. The log is the team's institutional memory and the antidote to re-litigating settled questions in every retro.
- **Disagree and commit.** Once the decision is made, the room unifies — arguing in the room, delivering as one outside it (cross-ref [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md)). The first-time manager's special duty: when *your* decision is the minority one, say "I heard the case against; here is why I am going this way; here is the trigger that flips it" — that sentence preserves both speed and safety (§6).
- **Decisions with no owner rot.** Every decision ends with a named owner and a date; otherwise it is a wish.

### 12.4 Communication: the no-surprises rule

The transition discipline's "overcommunicate during change" (cross-ref [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md)) is doubly true for a first-time manager, who is the new interpreter between the bank and the team:

- **The communication charter** (a §4 working agreement made concrete): what goes to the team meeting, what goes to the 1:1, what goes to email/chat, and the response-time norm. Corridor news is the enemy — decisions and context travel in the channels the charter names.
- **The manager is the relay, not the wall.** Bad news from above (a reorg rumor, a budget cut, a change-board rejection) is filtered but not hidden — the team cannot plan around news it does not have. The rule: *the team should never learn about its own future from someone outside the team.*
- **Upward communication is a discipline, not a talent.** A one-page weekly or fortnightly to your boss — outcomes, risks, asks — makes you predictable to manage and protects the team from the boss's surprise visits. Cross-ref [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) and [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md).
- **The single-point-of-contact failure.** If every stakeholder question routes through you, you are the bottleneck (§10.4's antidote: team members own stakeholder-facing slots).

### 12.5 Conflict: task conflict is the engine, relationship conflict is the fire

Lencioni's pyramid puts **fear of conflict** directly above absence of trust ([high_performance_team_guide.md](high_performance_team_guide.md) §1.2 — Lencioni, *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*, Jossey-Bass, 2002, ✅ sibling-verified): teams that cannot argue cannot commit, and teams that cannot commit cannot hold each other accountable. The first-time-manager rules:

- **Distinguish the two kinds.** Task conflict — disagreement about the work — is the engine of better decisions and belongs *in the room* (§4.4's agreement). Relationship conflict — personal attacks, status fights, grudges — is the fire that must be put out fast, because it is contagious and outlives every issue.
- **The room-not-the-corridor rule is non-negotiable.** The manager's job when conflict appears in a corridor: take it back to the room, facilitated. The facilitation craft is in [conflict_management_guide.md](conflict_management_guide.md) and [facilitation_skills_guide.md](facilitation_skills_guide.md).
- **Your own defensiveness is the wildcard.** When the conflict implicates you or your decisions, your reaction is what the team learns from (§6.5). "Tell me more" beats "let me clarify."
- **Escalate by design, not by accident.** Two senior engineers who cannot work together after the room-process has run its course is a manager decision, not a waiting game — restructure the ownership map (§12.2), separate the interfaces, and if it is a values-level clash, name it (§9.6's process, applied with HR where the bank requires).

### 12.6 Culture: the residue of what you reward

The aphorism "culture eats strategy for breakfast" is the most quoted line in this territory and the least verifiable — the attribution is disputed among Peter Drucker, Mark Fields, and others, and Quote Investigator documents the uncertainty (⚠ — see §16). It survives because it is true in practice, and the first-time manager does not need the quote; they need the mechanism:

- **Culture is the residue of repeated reward and punishment (§5.3).** What you tolerate, what you celebrate, how you react to bad news, who gets the promotion — these compound into "how we do things here" faster than any values poster.
- **You inherit a culture; you do not have to keep it.** The first quarter's diagnosis (§3.5) includes the cultural read: what behaviors are currently rewarded *de facto* (heroics? blame? quiet compliance? overtime?) — the charter's working agreements (§4.4) are the team's own rewrite of those defaults.
- **Subculture within the bank is real and legitimate.** The bank's risk culture (compliance, change control, audit trails) is the container; the team's culture (how we collaborate, how we learn, how we speak up) lives inside it. The charter states how the team works *within* the constraints (§4.5) — a team culture that fights the container is a culture of cynicism.
- **Culture shows up in the stories.** The team's lore — the incident everyone retells, the manager reaction everyone remembers — is the culture's actual constitution. A first-time manager who wants to change culture changes which stories get told: celebrate the helper, the honest bad-news bearer, the person who admitted the mistake (§6, §11.7).

## 13. The First-Time-Manager Pitfalls

### 13.1 The map

The meta-pitfall: **trying to avoid every pitfall.** You will fall into some of these; the discipline is catching yourself early and being honest in the retro when the team names it (§5.4). The list below is the repo's compiled first-year failure catalogue — each entry names the pattern, why it happens, and the exit.

### 13.2 The do-it-yourself trap (alpha geek)

The brilliant IC who cannot stop redoing the team's work (§2.2). It feels like quality control and reads as distrust; every takeover teaches the team to stop trying. **Exit:** delegate your own strengths first (§10.4), move your code access out of the critical path, and let a rough first attempt ship (§10.6's dignity of risk).

### 13.3 The savior complex

Arriving with the diagnosis already written — the promoted insider's special hazard, because you *were* in the team and "know" what is wrong (§3.3's "do not have opinions yet"). The savior manager "fixes" the team at its members, runs change for change's sake, and treats the intake round as theater. **Exit:** the diagnosis discipline of §3 — intake first, charter second, change third; if the team does not recognize the problem you are solving, you are solving your own narrative.

### 13.4 The friend-boss trap

The relationship inversion (§2.2): your former peers are now your reports, and the friendship is real but the accountability line is now real too. Three failure shapes: **over-indexing** (protecting friends from accountability, which breeds resentment in the rest of the team and contempt in the friends), **over-correcting** (treating former friends harder than everyone else to prove neutrality), and **the club** (the old peer group as the de facto inner circle, §13.8). **Exit:** name the inversion out loud early — "we are still friends, and now I also have a job to do" — keep 1:1s and decisions on the same standards as everyone else's, and let the charter's norms apply to everyone including your friends (§4.4).

### 13.5 The micromanagement–abdication pendulum

The two failure modes of §10.6, often alternating: a manager who starts by hovering, gets burned out by the load, and swings into disappearing — then back. **Exit:** the delegation ladder (§10.3) gives both ends a home: explicit checkpoints kill the urge to hover, and the rung conversation kills the urge to vanish.

### 13.6 The conflict-avoider

The people-pleaser manager (cross-ref [the_managers_path_guide.md](the_managers_path_guide.md)'s Ch.7 dysfunction) who suppresses disagreement, postpones the difficult conversation, and lets the loudest compromise win. The cost is Lencioni's whole pyramid (§12.5): no argument, no commitment, no accountability. **Exit:** the room-not-the-corridor rule (§12.5) and the difficult-conversation script (§9.6) — conflict handled early and in the room is cheaper than the resentment that accrues in the corridor.

### 13.7 The feedback coward

Hoarding feedback until review season, then delivering it as surprise verdicts (§9.5). Usually driven by fear of damaging the relationship — which is precisely what the surprise damages. **Exit:** in-the-moment SBI (§9.2), the weekly 1:1 channel, and the rule that a review contains no surprises by construction (§11.4).

### 13.8 Favoritism and the inner circle

Real or perceived, it is the fastest trust-killer (§7.3). Shapes: the old peer group (§13.4), the star IC who gets all the airtime and all the interesting work, the "proxy 1:1" pattern (§8.6), and hiring in your own image (the comfortable clone). The team watches access — to you, to work, to credit — and differential access reads as differential safety (§6.5). **Exit:** rotate the visible work (§10.4), make the ownership map public (§12.2), and run recognition on the behavior standard, not the relationship (§9.3, §11.7).

### 13.9 Arriving with answers

The transition trap of §3.3, in its second form: not the savior's wholesale diagnosis, but the reflex to answer every question with the IC's muscle memory — "let me just look at it." It stalls the team's learning and quietly reinstates you as the doer. **Exit:** the coaching reflex — "what do you think?" — and the discipline of letting the team answer first (§8.3's talking ratio).

### 13.10 The wrong scoreboard

Managing the old scoreboard — tickets closed, commits, your own output (§2.1's identity table) — while the team's real scoreboard (outcomes, health, §11) goes unmanaged. The team learns what you measure; if you measure tickets, you get tickets. **Exit:** publish the team goals (§11.2), review against them, and let the quarterly health check (§11.5) sit beside the delivery metrics.

### 13.11 The poster charter

A charter that was written (often by the manager, §4.5), ratified once, and then never referenced. The poster charter is worse than no charter: it is a standing demonstration that the team's agreements are decorative. **Exit:** the charter is referenced in every planning meeting (§5.2), its working agreements are the referee in the accountability conversation (§12.2), and it gets revised — by the team — when reality outruns it.

### 13.12 The transition traps

The 90-day discipline's own failure catalogue (cross-ref [the_first_90_days_guide.md](the_first_90_days_guide.md)): staying in learning mode too long (diagnosis as procrastination), winning the wrong early wins (quick wins that please the boss but mean nothing to the team), and negotiating success only once instead of continuously. The first-time-manager twist: each trap is also a team-building failure — learning without acting is leadership without direction, and wins the team does not own are not wins at all (§3.2).

## 14. The Worked Example: A Cymbal Bank First-Time Engineering Manager

*Synthetic illustration in the repo's worked-example tradition — a composite built from the patterns in this guide and its siblings, not a case study of a real engagement (flagged in §15 and §16).*

### 14.1 The setup

Priya is promoted to engineering manager of the **Payments Integration squad** at Cymbal Bank — six engineers, four of them former peers — after the previous manager moved to a platform role. The squad owns the integration layer between the bank's core payments engine and its channels: a realignment-ish STARS situation (the bank's objectives changed; the squad's charter was never rewritten for them) with a sustaining core of good people. Her boss gives her one sentence of mandate: "make the squad reliable again, and grow two of them into seniors." Her first 90 days follow §3's plan almost literally.

### 14.2 Days 1–30: learn and diagnose (§3.2, §3.4, §8.5)

- **Week 1:** the five conversations with her boss (expectations, style, resources, the real definition of "reliable"); the intake 1:1 round — 45 minutes each, seven questions from §8.5, mostly silence on her side. The recurring answer to "what should I know?": *the change board is strangling us — every integration change takes three weeks of review, and the previous manager fought the board instead of learning its rhythm.*
- **Weeks 2–4:** she observes every ritual before touching it (standup, planning, retro), reads two quarters of delivery artifacts and the incident log, and writes the one-page diagnosis: the squad is a *working group* drifting toward pseudo-team — individually competent, collectively unaligned; no charter; planning is ticket-shuffling; the retro is a venting session with no actions; two anchors (Mei, the integration architect; Ravi, the operations lead) are quietly carrying the load, and two juniors are being silently written off by the seniors.
- **The discipline that mattered:** she does *not* change the standup, does not "fix" the retro, does not hire or fire. She tells the team, in her first Friday meeting: "for 30 days I am going to learn this team, not change it. Here is what I will not do yet." Then she listens (§3.4, §13.3's antidote).

### 14.3 Days 31–60: stabilize and win (§3.2, §4.4, §5.4, §10.4)

- **The charter workshop** (§4.3): two sessions plus ratification. The team rewrites its mission in its own words — "the squad that makes payments integration boring for the rest of the bank" — and adopts five working agreements, including "disagreement happens in the room" and "bad news travels fast; the messenger is thanked." Priya writes nothing on the flip chart.
- **The rituals get jobs** (§5.2): planning now opens with the charter's goals; the retro gets the three non-negotiables (§5.4). At her first retro, the team names something she did — she had interrupted Mei in planning. She says "tell me more," and the next planning meeting visibly checks the behavior. The safety signal lands (§6.4's respond-productively).
- **The early win, team-owned** (§3.2): the squad's biggest recurring pain is change-board friction. Instead of fighting the board herself, Priya delegates the change-board relationship to Ravi with the delegation conversation (§10.5): the outcome (cut average review time), the constraints (compliance is non-negotiable), the authority (rung 4 — decide and do, tell her if the board escalates), and air cover. Ravi — who had been doing the work uncredited — rebuilds the board's trust with a pre-review checklist the squad shares, and average review time drops by half within six weeks. The win is *his*, named in the demo (§9.3, §11.7). This is also delegation #1 of §10.4: the thing Priya was personally best at (stakeholder wrangling), handed over.
- **Delegations #2 and #3:** release notes and demo prep go to the juniors, with purpose attached (§10.4). The first demo is rougher than hers would have been. She does not take it back.

### 14.4 Days 61–90: align and accelerate (§3.2, §11.2, §11.4, §11.5)

- **Goals locked to the bank's objectives** (§11.2): two squad goals — "cut integration change lead time to under two weeks" (key results: change-board review time, rework rate) and "every squad member owns a visible outcome" (key results: ownership map coverage, junior-led demos). Published on the squad's board.
- **The performance-conversation round** (§11.4): every report gets the goals-and-development conversation. The surprise-free principle pays off: Mei wants the architect path — Priya starts the ladder conversation (§8.3's career bucket) and secures a stretch assignment for her on the bank's instant-payments program. One junior's disengagement turns out to be a task-design problem (§11.6's Hackman & Oldham pass): he had been handed ticket fragments with no task identity — the fix is a whole feature, owned end to end.
- **The health check baseline** (§11.5): anonymized, external tool, includes the safety question. The aggregate tells the truth the room never did: safety is mid, meaning is high, structure & clarity is low. The retro owns the action list.

### 14.5 Months 4–12: the levers in action (§12)

- **Accountability** (§12.2): the ownership map goes public — RACI for every outcome the squad owns. When a regulatory filing deadline slips because a handoff had no owner, the conversation is about the map, not the person: "we agreed the rollback test was part of done; this release shipped without it. What in our process allowed that?" (the §5.4 question). The map gets the fix; the person keeps their dignity.
- **Decisions** (§12.3): a decision log starts. The first logged decision is Priya's: the squad will *not* rewrite the integration layer on the new framework this half, despite the seniors' enthusiasm — she consults (two sessions), then commands, with the trigger named: "if the change-board lead time stays under two weeks for two more quarters, we revisit." The team grumbles in the room and delivers as one outside it — disagree and commit, with the safety to argue the next time (§6).
- **Conflict** (§12.5): Mei and Ravi collide over the instant-payments architecture — task conflict turning personal in the corridor. Priya takes it back to the room, facilitated: each states the technical case, the team interrogates both, and the decision (a hybrid adapter layer) is made by the room on technical merit. The grudge does not survive; the adapter does.
- **Communication** (§12.4): the communication charter holds — when a reorg rumor reaches the division, the squad hears it from Priya first, with what is known and what is not. The weekly one-pager to her boss makes her predictable to manage, and her boss stops surprise-visiting the squad.
- **Culture** (§12.6): the stories change. The incident everyone retells by month 10 is the one where a junior surfaced a production risk early, the squad thanked him in the retro, and the release was held — *and management backed it*. That story is the culture's new constitution, and Priya tells it herself in the demo.

### 14.6 What she would redo, and the map

Her own retrospective at month 12 is honest (§5.4): she should have run the health check at day 60, not day 90 — structure & clarity was the squad's real problem and she diagnosed it late; and her first delegation to Ravi came with too few checkpoints (rung 4 before the trust account was full, §7.3), which cost her one sleepless week. The squad's trajectory — DORA-style lead time down, health-check safety up two quarters running, one promotion (Mei) and one senior ready to stretch (Ravi) — is the §1.1 thesis in operation: she converted herself, converted the group into a team, installed the substrate, and ran the levers. It is, in the end, what the guide promised in §1.4: her break-even was defined by their delivery.

## 17. The Glossary

- **Psychological safety** — "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking" (Edmondson 1999); the #1 Project Aristotle dynamic (§6).
- **Safety × standards matrix** — Edmondson's two axes: comfort zone (high safety, low standards), anxiety zone (low safety, high standards), apathy (low both), performance (high both) (§6.3).
- **Learning behavior** — asking questions, seeking help, talking about errors, experimentation; the mediator between safety and performance in Edmondson's model (§6.1).
- **Project Aristotle / five dynamics** — Google's 180+ team study: safety, dependability, structure & clarity, meaning, impact (§6.2).
- **Trust** — the willingness to be vulnerable based on positive expectations of another's intentions or behavior (Mayer, Davis & Schoorman 1995); decomposes into ability, benevolence, integrity (§7).
- **1:1 (one-on-one)** — the recurring, report-owned meeting; the manager's most powerful tool (§8).
- **Intake round** — the first-30-days listening tour of 1:1s with every report, using the §8.5 questions.
- **SBI** — Situation–Behavior–Impact, the CCL feedback structure that keeps feedback behavioral rather than personal (§9.2).
- **Radical candor** — Kim Scott's care-personally × challenge-directly feedback framework (2017); the failure quadrants: ruinous empathy, obnoxious aggression, manipulative insincerity (§9.2).
- **Delegation ladder** — five rungs of authority transfer, from "do it, show me" to "own it entirely" (§10.3).
- **OKR** — objective + key results; the goal vehicle popularized by Doerr (2018), credited to Grove's iMBOs (§11.2).
- **SMART** — Doran's (1981) goal-quality check: specific, measurable, assignable, realistic, time-bound (§11.3).
- **Hygiene factors / motivators** — Herzberg's two-factor theory: hygiene prevents dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, the work itself) create it (§11.6).
- **Autonomy, competence, relatedness** — the three innate needs of self-determination theory (Ryan & Deci 2000) (§11.6).
- **Job characteristics** — Hackman & Oldham's five motivating dimensions of work: skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback (§11.6).
- **Team health check** — the quarterly, anonymized team survey, including a safety question, whose trend is the team's vital sign (§11.5).
- **RACI** — responsible / accountable / consulted / informed; the ownership-map notation (§12.2).
- **DRI** — directly responsible individual; the named owner of an outcome (§12.2).
- **Disagree and commit** — argue in the room, deliver as one once the decision is made (§12.3).
- **Task conflict vs. relationship conflict** — disagreement about the work (the engine) vs. personal animus (the fire) (§12.5).
- **Room-not-the-corridor** — the working agreement that disagreement happens in the room, never in side channels (§4.4, §12.5).
- **Alpha geek** — the brilliant IC who cannot stop redoing others' work; the classic first-manager failure (§13.2).
- **Friend-boss trap** — the former-peer-now-report inversion and its failure shapes (§13.4).
- **Savior complex** — arriving with the diagnosis pre-written (§13.3).
- **Pseudo-team** — a group that calls itself a team but is individually accountable (Katzenbach & Smith) (§1.4, §3.5).
- **Change board** — the bank's review gate for production changes; a real constraint the charter must work within (§4.5, §12.2, §14).
- **Cymbal Bank** — the repo's recurring fictional bank persona (Singapore context), used for all worked examples including §14.

## 18. The Closing

### 18.1 The argument, in five moves

The first-time manager's first year has one job and one job only: **turn a group of individuals into a team that delivers more than the sum of its parts — and survive long enough to see it happen.** The playbook, in the order this guide sequenced it:

1. **Convert yourself first** (§2) — the peer→manager mindset shift is the prerequisite; nothing else works if you are still playing.
2. **Convert the group into a team** (§4–§5) — charter, goals, rituals; purpose shaped by the team, not received.
3. **Install the substrate** (§6–§9) — psychological safety and trust as the load-bearing walls, 1:1s and feedback as the plumbing.
4. **Convert individual effort into collective output** (§10–§12) — delegation, goals, performance, and the five levers: accountability, decisions, communication, conflict, culture.
5. **Name the pitfalls before they name you** (§13) — and when you fall into one anyway, use the retro, not the cover-up.

The evidence base is the honest one: the big findings are real and verified (Edmondson, Aristotle, the motivation research — see §15), the practices are mostly the accumulated craft of managers who went before (cross-referenced from this repo's sibling guides), and the things we could not verify are listed, not laundered (§16). The worked example (§14) shows the whole thing assembled — not as proof, but as a picture.

### 18.3 The final word

Every leader's origin story contains the same sentence, spoken at a promotion meeting or a farewell or a bar: *this was my first team.* The managers who say it with pride and the managers who say it with a wince did not differ in talent — they differed in whether, in the first 90 days, they converted themselves before converting the group, installed safety and trust before demanding performance, and ran the levers instead of the tickets. This guide exists so that when your team is described that way — as *your first team* — the sentence is true in the way that matters: not the first team you managed, but the first team that made you a manager. Build that one. The bank will get its outcomes, the people will get their growth, and you will get the only prize this job reliably pays: a team that delivers, and that would follow you anywhere. That is the whole playbook, and it ends where every first-time manager's story begins — with the first team.
