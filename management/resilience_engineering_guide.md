## 1. The Overview, the Disambiguation and the Decoder

### 1.1 The Identity Gate — Two Meanings, One of Which This Guide Is About

There are **two unrelated things called "resilience engineering"**, and almost every confused conversation about the subject is a conversation in which the two have been silently merged.

**Meaning (1) — the safety-science discipline.** A research community that formed in the mid-2000s around a specific set of people (Erik Hollnagel, David D. Woods, Nancy Leveson, Sidney Dekker, Richard I. Cook, Jens Rasmussen and others), with its own founding edited volume, its own biennial symposium series, its own association, its own method (FRAM), its own assessment instrument, its own internal disputes, and its own journal venues. Its unit of analysis is the **socio-technical system**. Its central question is not *why does this fail* but *why does this nearly always succeed, and what supplies that capacity*. **This is the subject of this guide.**

**Meaning (2) — the colloquial systems-engineering usage.** "Resilience engineering" as shorthand for making systems survive failures: fault injection and chaos drills, redundancy and failover, circuit breakers and bulkheads, disaster-recovery exercises, multi-region architecture, "we run gamedays". Its unit of analysis is the **technical system**. It is a real and valuable body of practice — this repository covers it well — but it is a **different field**, with a different lineage, different methods, a different literature and different failure modes.

The relationship between them is directional and it is **not symmetric**: the safety-science discipline **predates and informs** the colloquial usage. The colloquial usage does **not** inform, define or contain the safety-science discipline, and it is not "the engineering version" of it. Treating meaning (2) as the practical form of meaning (1) loses the entire content of meaning (1) — because the thing meaning (1) studies, the adaptive capacity of people under varying conditions, is precisely the thing meaning (2)'s methods do not measure.

There is a datable, checkable proof that the two usages were already running in parallel and already meant different things. In **November 2012**, *Communications of the ACM* published a piece titled **"Resilience engineering: learning to embrace failure"** (*Commun. ACM* **55**(11), pp. 40–47, DOI `10.1145/2366316.2366331`, ISSN 0001-0782 ✅). Its own abstract identifies it as **"a discussion with Jesse Robbins, Kripa Krishnan, John Allspaw, and Tom Limoncelli"** ✅ — that is, a conversation among four prominent practitioners of web operations and failure experimentation, not a safety-science paper. In the same decade the safety-science community was holding its biennial Resilience Engineering Symposia (Sophia-Antipolis 2006, Antibes 2008, Sophia-Antipolis 2011, Soesterberg 2013, Lisbon 2015 ✅) and publishing *Resilience Engineering in Practice: A Guidebook* (2011 ✅). **Same phrase, different rooms, same years.**

**What this repository contains today.** State it plainly, because the whole point of a gate is to be honest about which side of it you are standing on. The repository as of this pass contains **zero** content on meaning (1) and a substantial amount of content on meaning (2): chaos engineering owns fault injection, experimentation and game days; zero-downtime system design owns availability mathematics, SLOs/error budgets and the resilience patterns; the operational-resilience framework guide owns the regulatory regime and its testing and assurance obligations. **This guide is the repository's first treatment of meaning (1).** It does not replace any of those; it names what they are not.

And it does **not** blur this tradition into SRE either. Site reliability engineering is a third tradition — an engineering-and-operations discipline with its own canon, its own error-budget arithmetic and its own post-incident review conventions, born inside a large platform operator in the 2000s and since generalised. Resilience engineering and SRE **intersect** in exactly one place worth naming: both are interested in how operators of complex production systems actually cope, and both reject the "operator as the weakest link" framing. They are **not** the same tradition and they do not share a canon, a community, a journal or a method. §9 states the intersection and the separation precisely.

### 1.2 The One-Line Definition, and Why Safety Science Needed a New Lens

**Resilience, in this discipline, is the capacity to succeed under varying conditions — not merely the absence of failure.** That is not a paraphrase I have invented; it is the formulation the field's own white paper uses. In *From Safety-I to Safety-II: A White Paper* (Hollnagel, Wears & Braithwaite, 2015, read as full text ✅), Safety-II "relates to **the system's ability to succeed under varying conditions**" ✅, and the paper's executive summary sets the two definitions against each other explicitly: Safety-I defines safety as "a state where as few things as possible go wrong", while under Safety-II "the purpose of safety management" becomes ensuring that "as many things as possible go right" ✅.

Why did safety science need a new lens at all? The case, in the field's own terms, has three parts, and it is worth separating what is a **claim** from what is an **observation**.

- **The observation:** in complex socio-technical systems, the number of things that go right enormously exceeds the number that go wrong, and the *work* that produces the two is the same work. As the white paper puts it, "things do not go right because people behave as they are supposed to, but because people can and do adjust what they do to match the conditions of work" ✅. The same sentence appears in substance on Hollnagel's own page about the "no view" of human error, where he writes that "all human activity… is variable in the sense that it is adjusted to the conditions. The variability is therefore a strength, indeed a necessity, rather than a liability" ✅.
- **The claim:** therefore a safety discipline that studies only the adverse cases is studying a **biased sample**, and its conclusions about causes will be systematically wrong. The white paper's phrasing: "Safety-I… does not stop to consider why human performance practically always goes right" ✅. Wikipedia's article on the subject states the same point more sharply — if work practices are examined only after an accident, the analysis suffers **selection bias** ✅ (secondary source; treat as a fair restatement, not as a primary).
- **The inference (and this is where the discipline's ambition and its vulnerability both live):** if you want to improve the ratio, you should be able to say what supplies the capacity behind the successes, and manage *that*. Everything in §3 — the four abilities — is an attempt to answer "what supplies it".

The honest framing, which §4 and §11 develop: the **observation** is uncontroversial and is shared by every serious safety tradition (high-reliability organisation research, normal-accidents theory and safety-science ergonomics all accept it). The **inference** is where the field's conceptual richness, its operational difficulty and its contested empirical status all sit.

### 1.3 The Filing Rationale, and the Four Boundaries

**Why this guide lives in `management/` and not in `technology/`.** Because the discipline's unit of analysis is the **socio-technical system and the adaptive capacity of the people inside it** — not a platform component. Its object of study is work: how it is designed, resourced, constrained, normalised, adjusted and learned from. Its methods are interviews, model-building, observation and structured judgement, not measurement of a running artefact. Its levers are staffing, resourcing, procedure design, incentive design, investigation practice, learning systems and managerial attention. An architect in a bank applies it by changing **how work is reviewed and resourced**, not by changing a manifest. That places it in `management/` alongside the organisational-capability material — and it is why a reader looking for a *resilience pattern* is in the wrong guide.

**The four boundaries, stated explicitly, because the four traditions are genuinely different bodies of work.**

| The tradition | Its unit of analysis | Its core question | Where its concepts transfer here — and where they do not |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Resilience engineering (safety science)** — Hollnagel, Woods, Leveson, Dekker, Cook, Rasmussen | The socio-technical system and the people adapting inside it | Why does work almost always go right, and what supplies that capacity? | This guide. Its concepts transfer to *any* domain where people adapt under uncertainty — including software operations (§9). Its methods do **not** produce a test result, a pass/fail or a control attestation, and cannot be substituted for one. |
| **Site reliability engineering (SRE)** | A production service and its service-level objectives | How much unreliability can we afford, and how do we spend it deliberately? | Transfers: the blameless post-incident review and the rejection of "operator error" as a root cause. Does **not** transfer: this discipline is not an error budget, has no steady-state metric, and cannot be satisfied by an SLO. They intersect at §9.1 and nowhere else that matters. |
| **Business continuity management (BCM)** | A service, its recovery objectives and its plans | Can we restore this service to its objectives after a disruption? | Transfers: the focus on *outcomes* rather than assets. Does **not** transfer: BCM is a plan-and-rehearse discipline whose unit is the plan; this discipline treats *the plan* as work-as-imagined (§5) and asks what the operators actually did. §12 draws the line; the operational-resilience guide owns the BCM machinery. |
| **Regulatory operational resilience (DORA, MAS, PRA/FCA, BCBS)** | An important business service and its impact tolerance | Can the firm stay within its tolerance through a severe-but-plausible scenario? | Transfers: the attention to severe-but-plausible rather than expected disruption. ### 1.4 The Decoder — Glossary in Advance

| Term | Working definition | Origin as established in this guide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Resilience** (in this discipline) | The capacity to succeed under varying conditions — the system's ability to sustain required operations under both expected and unexpected conditions by adjusting its functioning before, during or after events | Hollnagel's own formulation, on his SPM/RAG page ✅; the same construction appears in the 2015 white paper ✅. | **Safety-I** | Safety defined as a state where **as few things as possible go wrong**; the purpose of management is to reduce adverse outcomes; the human is predominantly a liability to be constrained | Hollnagel, Wears & Braithwaite, *From Safety-I to Safety-II: A White Paper*, 2015 ✅; earlier formal statement in the EUROCONTROL white paper, Hollnagel, Leonhardt, Licu & Shorrock, 2013 ✅ |

| **The four abilities** (Hollnagel's current term: **the four potentials**) | The ability to **respond**, to **monitor**, to **anticipate**, and to **learn** — the capacities whose presence explains resilient performance | Hollnagel, "Epilogue: RAG – The Resilience Analysis Grid", in *Resilience Engineering in Practice: A Guidebook*, 2011, pp. 275–296 ✅ (citation established); the canonical statement of the four as a set, with the publisher's own blurb listing them ✅; Hollnagel's own current wording ✅ |

| **The assessment grid's unit** | A scored statement of what the organisation can do, not a count of what went wrong | RAG/SPM, 2011/2022 ✅ |
| **The new view of human error** | Error as a **consequence** of the conditions in which people work — a symptom of deeper trouble — rather than a cause located in a fallible individual | The "old view / new view" framing is **Dekker's**; the underlying argument is older and wider. ⚠ Hollnagel himself pushes past it to a **"no view"**, which he dates to a 1983 position paper on his own site ✅ — see §8.3. |
| **Sharp end / blunt end** | The sharp end is where practitioners directly interact with the hazardous process; the blunt end is where regulators, administrators, economic decision-makers and technology suppliers control the resources, constraints and competing demands the sharp end must satisfy | **Woods and colleagues** — the *Behind Human Error* line (CSERIAC report 1994; *Behind Human Error*, reissued with DOI `10.1201/9781315568935` ✅). **Not** Hollnagel's coinage; he uses it ✅. |
| **Normalisation of deviance** | The gradual, social-organisational process by which a previously unacceptable deviation becomes normal and unremarkable | **Diane Vaughan**, *The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA* (University of Chicago Press, 1996) ✅. **Not** Hollnagel, **not** Reason, **not** resilience engineering. §8.3. |
| **Drift into failure** | Failure as the slow, incremental migration of an organisation's practices away from safety, each step locally reasonable, with no single broken component to find | **Dekker**, *Drift into Failure* (Ashgate, 2011) ✅ (Crossref monograph record and chapter titles ✅). Distinct from Rasmussen's migration-to-boundary model (§8.4). |
| **Migration to the boundary of acceptable performance** | The observed tendency of work to migrate toward the edge of what is acceptable as efficiency pressure accumulates, with accidents occurring when the boundary is crossed | **Rasmussen**, "Risk management in a dynamic society: a modelling problem", *Safety Science* 27, 183–213, 1997, DOI `10.1016/S0925-7535(97)00052-0` ✅. **Not** Hollnagel's, and **not** the same idea as drift into failure. |
| **Resilience versus robustness** | Robustness is the capacity to resist a disturbance without changing; resilience is the capacity to adjust functioning in order to sustain required operations. A system can be robust and brittle, or apparently fragile and resilient. | Distilled from Hollnagel's definition ✅ and from the 2006 volume's framing that failure "is the result of the adaptations necessary to cope with the complexity of the real world" ✅ (publisher's description of *Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts*). |

## 2. The Origins and the Canon

### 2.1 The Meeting at Söderköping, and the Community That Formed

The discipline has a specific origin event, and unlike most "founding moments" this one is datable.

**The first Resilience Engineering Symposium was held in Söderköping, Sweden, in October 2004 (25–29 October 2004)** — a small meeting that, per the proceedings of the fourth symposium, brought the field into existence ✅. The date and place are corroborated by two independent library catalogue records quoting the *Proceedings of the Fourth Resilience Engineering Symposium*'s own front matter ✅, and the same date appears in the secondary literature ✅. A commonly repeated figure holds that the first meeting brought together **fourteen** safety-science researchers with an interest in complex systems ⚠ — I found this only in the secondary (encyclopaedic) source this pass, not in a primary proceedings record, so I state it as attributed rather than verified.

Note that the Resilience Engineering Association's **own symposia archive begins with the second symposium** ✅ — its papers page lists the 2nd (Sophia-Antipolis, 8–10 November 2006) onward. The first is established from the proceedings' preface, not from the association's own archive. If you only read the association's site, you would conclude the series started in 2006. That is a small example of a pattern worth internalising for this whole field: **the primary community's own record-keeping is incomplete, and secondary sources are sometimes more complete and sometimes simply wrong.**

Hollnagel dates the **idea** to 2004 as well, listing "Resilience engineering (2004)" in the ideas index of his own site ✅. Treat that as a primary source for his own chronology, not as an independent one.

**How the community formed, in outline.** The people who convened it came out of **two adjacent traditions** rather than out of one:

1. **Cognitive systems engineering / human factors** — the study of how people and machines jointly accomplish cognitive work, with the "joint cognitive system" as the unit (Hollnagel & Woods, *Joint Cognitive Systems: Foundations of Cognitive Systems Engineering*, 2005, and *Joint Cognitive Systems: Patterns in Cognitive Systems Engineering*, 2006 ✅ per Hollnagel's own bibliography). This tradition already rejected the "human as a fallible component" model before resilience engineering had a name.
2. **Systems safety and system dynamics / risk management** — Rasmussen's work on risk management in a dynamic society and on the migration of work to the boundary of acceptable performance (1997 ✅), and Leveson's systems-theoretic safety work (STAMP/STPA) ✅.

The **reported deeper root** the association itself names is the **"pioneering Clambake Conferences of the 1980s"** ✅ — an informal, invitation-only gathering of cognitive engineers and safety researchers whose format (small, argumentative, cross-industry, no proceedings-industrial-complex) the Resilience Engineering Symposia visibly inherit.

## 3. The Four Abilities

### 3.1 The Canonical Set, in the Author's Own Words

The four abilities are the operational core of the discipline: if resilience is the capacity to succeed under varying conditions, the four abilities are the discipline's answer to *capacity for what*. They are best quoted from the person who proposed them, because the circulating paraphrases lose the precision. Hollnagel's own current framing ✅ calls them **"the four basic potentials for resilient performance"** and introduces them with an argument worth following: "Since resilient performance is possible for most, if not all, systems, the explanation must refer to something that is independent of any specific domain" ✅.

| # | Ability (Hollnagel's phrase) | His own wording, quoted | The short form he uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **The potential to respond** | "Knowing what to do, or being able to respond to regular and irregular changes, disturbances, and opportunities by activating prepared actions or by adjusting current mode of functioning." ✅ | respond to the actual |
| 2 | **The potential to monitor** | "Knowing what to look for, or being able to monitor that which is or could seriously affect the system's performance in the near term – positively or negatively. The monitoring must cover the system's own performance as well as what happens in the environment." ✅ | monitor the critical |
| 3 | **The potential to learn** | "Knowing what has happened, or being able to learn from experience, in particular to learn the right lessons from the right experience." ✅ | learn from the factual |
| 4 | **The potential to anticipate** | "Knowing what to expect, or being able to anticipate developments further into the future, such as possible disruptions, novel demands or constraints, new opportunities, or changing operating conditions." ✅ | anticipate the potential |

**Origin, established.** The four are presented as a set in **Hollnagel's "Epilogue: RAG – The Resilience Analysis Grid"**, in *Resilience Engineering in Practice: A Guidebook* (2011), pp. 275–296 ✅ — the chapter's existence and pagination are verified through Crossref, and the four abilities are given as that volume's own published framing in the publisher's blurb: "Resilience engineering depends on four abilities: the ability a) to respond to what happens, b) to monitor critical developments, c) to anticipate future threats and opportunities, and d) to learn from past experience — successes as well as failures" ✅. Hollnagel's current page states them as the resilient-performance potentials ✅. The later monograph devoted to them is *Safety-II in Practice: Developing the Resilience Potentials* (Routledge, 2017), whose chapter "The resilience potentials" occupies pp. 26–49 ✅.

**Two currency problems, both worth knowing before you cite this anywhere.**

1. **Abilities, or potentials?** Hollnagel's current language is **potentials**, and the guide follows the source in using "potentials" when quoting him and "abilities" when describing the canonical set as the field's common formulation. The difference is not cosmetic: "ability" suggests something an organisation *has*, "potential" suggests something it *can develop* — which is exactly why the later book is about *developing* them.
2. **The ordering is not stable, and this guide will not pretend otherwise.** Hollnagel's own page lists them **respond, monitor, learn, anticipate** ✅. The publisher's blurb for the 2011 Guidebook lists **respond, monitor, anticipate, learn** ✅. Both are the same four; neither is wrong; but a table that lists them in a different order from the one your reader has seen will look like an error. Say which source you are quoting.

**What is not part of the canonical set, and gets smuggled in anyway.** The four are **respond, monitor, anticipate, learn**. They are *not* "plan, prepare, respond, recover" — that is business-continuity language. They are *not* "prevent, protect, detect, respond, recover" — that is NIST-style cybersecurity framing. They are *not* the four stages of an incident lifecycle. In particular, **the four abilities are capacities, not phases**: an organisation does not "do the anticipate phase" and move on. Hollnagel's own point is that they are functions that run continuously and depend on one another ✅.

### 3.2 What It Means to Have or Lack Each

The value of the four as an assessment frame is that each can be absent in recognisable, describable ways. The following table is a working translation — it is **this guide's construction**, not a Hollnagel artefact, and it should be used as an interrogation checklist rather than a scale. (The instrument that *is* Hollnagel's is in §3.4.)

| Ability | What it looks like when present | What it looks like when missing | The characteristic failure it cannot prevent once missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Respond** | Prepared actions exist and are *inside* the work — rehearsed, reachable, understood; and, critically, the organisation can also act *without* a prepared action by adjusting its current mode of functioning | The only available responses are the ones in the plan; anything else escalates to a person who is not there; response requires authority nobody on shift has | A familiar disturbance is handled; a novel one becomes a decision-latency incident |
| **Monitor** | The organisation monitors what *could* affect performance in the near term, positively as well as negatively, and it monitors *itself*, not just the environment and not just the customer-visible service | Dashboards track outputs and not capacities; nobody monitors the thing that is actually degrading; monitoring is retrospective (reports, not signals) | A drift that was visible in a signal nobody watched |
| **Anticipate** | The organisation reasons about futures — disruptions, novel demands, new constraints, changed operating conditions — and uses that reasoning before the future arrives | Scenarios are built only from historical incidents; the horizon is one planning cycle; "we have never seen that" is treated as a reason not to prepare | The surprise that a small amount of imagining would have surfaced |
| **Learn** | The organisation learns "the right lessons from the right experience", including from **successful** work, and the learning changes what is done | Learning means writing a new procedure after an adverse event; near-misses and successes generate nothing; the same finding recurs in successive reviews under different names | Recurrence — the second, third and fourth instance of the same underlying trouble |

A necessary honesty note: **the "present/missing" column is a diagnostic heuristic, and the discipline does not provide a validated instrument that will tell you which box you are in.** The most interesting thing about the four is not that they can be scored; it is that the four *interrogate each other*. An organisation that says it "responds well" but cannot point to a single anticipation artefact is describing a **reactive** organisation with a good escalation process — which is not the same claim.

### 3.3 How the Abilities Couple

Hollnagel is explicit that the four are not independent, and he uses the discipline's own method to say it: "the potential to respond can benefit from, and perhaps even requires, the potential to monitor… Similarly, the potential to learn is likewise needed to improve the potentials to monitor and to respond. The four potentials can be seen as functions, and understanding how these functions are coupled is obviously essential for managing them" ✅. He then offers a simple FRAM model of the four as generic functions and notes that it "is too simple to be of practical value" but that a more detailed model can easily be developed ✅.

Two consequences worth internalising:

- **A weak ability is a capacity constraint on the others.** An organisation that cannot monitor cannot learn — not because it lacks a learning process, but because it has nothing to learn from. This is why "we do lessons-learned well" is a claim about the *learn* potential that is only credible if you can name the monitoring that fed it.
- **Improving one ability in isolation can degrade another.** Hollnagel's framing of the potentials as coupled functions is the mechanism. In practice the most common version of this is a monitoring investment that increases the volume of signals without increasing the ability to act on them, which consumes *respond* capacity and teaches the organisation that monitoring is noise.

### 3.4 The Resilience Assessment Grid, and What It Actually Is

**What it is.** The **Resilience Assessment Grid (RAG)** is a structured assessment instrument that scores the four abilities across a set of dimensions, producing a profile rather than a single number. It was introduced in Hollnagel's 2011 epilogue ✅ and is **still the instrument the field calls by that name everywhere except at source**.

**The currency correction.** Hollnagel's own site now presents the instrument as **Systemic Potentials Management (SPM)**, under the heading "Systemic Potentials Management (SPM), formerly referred to as the Resilience Assessment Grid (RAG)" ✅, with the page last updated **2022-02-15** ✅. Anyone citing "the RAG" as current terminology is citing the 2011 name. That is a small, checkable, extremely common error in secondary writing, and it is the kind of thing this guide exists to catch.

**What it produces.** A structured profile of where an organisation's resilience potentials are strong or weak, distributed across **levels** (typically the individual, the team/group, the organisation, and the wider system) and across the four potentials. Hollnagel notes that the four can be treated as **FRAM functions** and modelled as such, which makes the RAG and FRAM two halves of one approach: FRAM models the work, the RAG scores the capacity.

**What it is not.** It is not a certification, not a maturity model with levels and a badge, not a benchmark against other organisations, and not a predictive instrument. It produces **a structured description and a set of development priorities**. If someone sells you a "resilience score", they are not selling this.

**Where it has actually been used — Hollnagel's own list** ✅, which is worth reproducing precisely because it is the field's own evidence statement and it is modest: railways (traffic management — Rigaud et al.); offshore oil and gas logistics and emergency response; healthcare (including emergency departments); radiation protection; a prison's performance-management system (Steen, Ingvaldsen & Patriarca, *Safety Science* 142, 105367, 2021 ✅, per Hollnagel's own citation); Brazilian Army aviation; and ATM (air traffic management — a Linköping thesis applying the RAG). Hollnagel's own caveat on this list is candid: "there is no comprehensive list of practical examples" ✅. The Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency included the RAG in its **holistic safety guidelines** ✅ — a rare example of a regulator-adjacent body adopting the instrument.

Read that list for what it is: **a set of applied cases, mostly theses, reports and domain studies, not a body of controlled evaluations.** §10 and §11 develop what that means for how much the field can claim.

## 4. Safety-I versus Safety-II

### 4.1 The Reframing, From the Primary White Paper

The single most consequential argument in this field is a **change in the definition of safety**, and the cleanest statement of it is not in the 2014 book but in two white papers. The earlier one is the **EUROCONTROL white paper of 2013** (Hollnagel, Leonhardt, Licu & Shorrock, Brussels) ✅ — established as a citation through the acknowledgements of the later paper. The one I read in full is the **2015 white paper** (Hollnagel, Wears & Braithwaite, The Resilient Health Care Net) ✅.

| | **Safety-I** | **Safety-II** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition of safety** | "A state where as few things as possible go wrong" ✅ | The system's "ability to succeed under varying conditions" ✅; management aims to ensure "as many things as possible go right" ✅ |
| **Assumption about why things go wrong** | "Identifiable failures or malfunctions of specific components: technology, procedures, the human workers and the organisations in which they are embedded" ✅ | Everyday performance variability is the source of adaptation; adverse outcomes arise when the variability cannot be monitored or controlled, or when functions resonate |
| **Role of the human** | "Predominantly as a liability or hazard, principally because they are the most variable of these components" ✅ | "A resource necessary for system flexibility and resilience" ✅ |
| **Purpose of investigation** | "To identify the causes and contributory factors of adverse outcomes" ✅ | "To understand how things usually go right, since that is the basis for explaining how things occasionally go wrong" ✅ |
| **Purpose of risk assessment** | "To determine their likelihood" — the likelihood of adverse outcomes ✅ | "To understand the conditions where performance variability can become difficult or impossible to monitor and control" ✅ |
| **Management principle** | "Respond when something happens or is categorised as an unacceptable risk, usually by trying to eliminate causes or improve barriers, or both" ✅ | "Facilitate everyday work, to anticipate developments and events, and to maintain the adaptive capacity to respond effectively to the inevitable surprises" ✅ (the white paper cites Finkel 2011 for this construction ✅) |

The white paper's historical account of how Safety-I became orthodoxy is worth noting because it is a claim about *why* the old view fit its time: this view "became widespread in the safety critical industries (nuclear, aviation, etc.) between the 1960s and 1980s. At that time performance demands were significantly lower than today and systems simpler and less interdependent" ✅, and it rested on two tacit assumptions — "that systems could be decomposed and that the components of the system functioned in a bimodal manner—either working correctly or incorrectly" ✅. The argument is that both assumptions have stopped holding, and that "everyday clinical work is—and must be—variable and flexible" ✅ (the healthcare instance; the same argument is made for process and aviation work in the 2014 book).

### 4.2 What Changes Operationally

The reframing has very concrete consequences, and this is where a reader can decide whether it is useful without settling any philosophical question. Four changes:

| Domain | What Safety-I management does | What a Safety-II frame changes | The practical test of whether anything actually changed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What is measured** | Adverse outcomes: incidents, near-misses, injuries, losses, downtime — a count of bad things, with a target of zero | The conditions and capacities that produce good outcomes, plus the variability of everyday performance | Can you name a *positive* indicator that is reported to the same forum as the incident count, with the same seriousness, and that it is possible to fail? If not, nothing changed |
| **What is investigated** | The events that went wrong, and afterwards, looking for causes | Also the work that went right — the recoveries, the adaptations, the routine saves — and prospectively, looking for conditions | How many of last year's investigations examined an event that had **no** adverse outcome? |
| **What is rewarded** | Compliance; not deviating; not reporting a problem you created; low incident counts | Detecting and reporting drift; adapting effectively; the recovery that prevented an event | Who was last praised in your organisation for something that did not happen? |
| **How the human is modelled** | As the most variable and therefore most hazardous component, to be constrained by procedure | As the source of the flexibility that absorbs variation | Does the procedure work assume the operator needs to be prevented from improvising, or enabled to judge? |

The white paper also names a **risk of not doing this**: Safety-I efforts "may have unintended consequences because it unintentionally degrades the resources and procedures needed to make things go right" ✅. That sentence is the strongest practical argument in the whole text — an organisation optimising for the absence of adverse events can, in the process, remove the slack, the expertise and the informal practice that were producing the good outcomes. §14's sixth anti-pattern is that failure mode.

### 4.3 The Complementarity Position, and the Honest Caveat

**This is the section where an advocate would overclaim and this guide will not.**

The field's own primary documents take a **complementarity** position, in their own words, and it is not a rhetorical hedge:

> "It is important to emphasise that Safety-I and Safety-II represent **two complementary views of safety rather than two incompatible or conflicting approaches**. Many of the existing practices can therefore continue to be used, although possibly with a different emphasis." ✅ (2015 white paper)

and:

> "The way forward therefore lies in **combining the two ways of thinking**. While many of the existing methods and techniques can continue to be used, the assimilation of a Safety-II view will also require new practices…" ✅

and, on scope:

> "While many adverse events may still be treated by a Safety-I approach without serious consequences, there is a growing number of cases where this approach will not work and will leave us unaware of how everyday actions achieve safety." ✅

So the primary position is **complementarity plus a claim about a growing class of cases where Safety-I alone is insufficient**. And in the 2014 book's own framing, as reported by its reviewer, Safety-II "aims at describing the daily activity and its performance variability instead of producing reports on accidents or incidents as is the case for Safety-I" ✅ — a difference of emphasis and object, not a replacement.

**Now the honest caveat, which must sit in the same section and not be softened.**

- **Safety-II has not replaced Safety-I, and nobody in the field with standing claims it has.** The concepts are complements; the *practice* is mostly Safety-I with some Safety-II vocabulary layered on top (§11.5).
- **The empirical support for Safety-II is substantially weaker than its conceptual appeal.** The field's own output is dominated by conceptual papers, reviews and case studies. There is no body of controlled trials showing that adopting a Safety-II frame reduces harm. §11.3 states this in the field's own terms.
- **The reframing has been directly attacked in the peer-reviewed literature** — see §11.2 — on grounds that include the risk that a "things go right" emphasis becomes complacent about adverse outcomes. That criticism may or may not be right, but it is *published*, and a guide that omitted it would be an advertisement.
- **A "Safety-II programme" that removes counting and targets without adding anything is not a Safety-II programme.** It is the removal of a control. The white paper's own logic — combine, and continue to use the existing methods — forbids this, and the field's practitioners say so.

## 5. Work-as-Imagined versus Work-as-Done

### 5.1 The Distinction and Its Attribution

**Work-as-imagined (WAI)** is what designers, managers, regulators and procedure authors believe happens, or should happen — and it is the basis for design, training, procedure-writing and planning. **Work-as-done (WAD)** is what actually happens when work is performed under real conditions by real people ✅ (this framing is stated in the terms above by the SKYbrary-hosted version of Hollnagel's own explanation — a document whose PDF my extraction backend failed to retrieve twice, so I characterise it from the search-surfaced description rather than from a read copy — ⚠, while the pairing itself is verified through the *Resilient Health Care, Volume 3* chapter titles ✅, e.g. "Simulation: Closing the Gap between Work-as-Imagined and Work-as-Done" pp. 169–178 and "Realigning Work-as-Imagined and Work-as-Done: Can Training Help?" pp. 179–188).

**Attribution, handled carefully because this is genuinely contested.**

| Claim | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| The **WAI/WAD pairing as a named, framed distinction** is Hollnagel's | ✅ — it is the organising theme of a volume he co-edited (*Resilient Health Care, Volume 3*, CRC, eds Braithwaite, Wears & Hollnagel ✅), appears in his FRAM writing ("a set of concepts, a terminology, and a set of methods that make it possible to describe work-as-done rather than work-as-imagined. That is what the FRAM is about" ✅, from his own FRAM book page), and is used throughout the Safety-II material |
| The **idea that formal descriptions of work differ from the work itself** was introduced by resilience engineering | ❌ **rejected.** It is far older. |
| There is an **earlier precedent in francophone ergonomics** — the *prescribed work* versus *real work* distinction (travail prescrit / travail réel) associated with the French-speaking ergonomics tradition (Wisner, Laville, Teiger, Leplat, de Montmollin, Daniellou) | ⚠ **flagged.** This is widely asserted in the contemporary literature and I believe it, but **I did not retrieve a primary francophone source in this pass** and I therefore do not present it as verified. What I *can* evidence is that the francophone ergonomics community engaged with this literature from the start — Jacques Leplat published a review of *Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts* in *Perspectives interdisciplinaires sur le travail et la santé* in 2007 ✅ and later reviewed Dekker's *Safety Differently* in *Activités* ✅. That is evidence of a conversation, not of priority. |
| **Suchman's *Plans and Situated Actions* (Cambridge University Press, 1987)** is an earlier and independent argument that plans do not determine action but are *resources for* situated action | ✅ for the book (1987, CUP, corroborated ✅); ⚠ for the strength of its causal link to WAI/WAD, which no source in this pass states Hollnagel as acknowledging |
| **Hale & Borys** established, in the safety-science literature, that rule-following and safe working are not the same thing, and reviewed the management of safety rules and procedures | ✅ for the papers (*Safety Science* 55, 207–221 and 222–231, 2013 ✅) and the chapter in Bieder & Bourrier's *Trapping Safety into Rules* ✅. **This is the empirically-richer sibling of the WAI/WAD idea** and is under-cited in practitioner writing. |

The honest summary: **Hollnagel named and packaged the distinction; he did not invent the observation; and the observation has at least three older homes — francophone ergonomics, ethnomethodology/situated-action theory, and the safety-rules literature.**

### 5.2 Why Practice Must Differ

The discipline's account of why WAD cannot equal WAI, in its own terms, has four parts, and the first is the one people miss:

1. **Work situations are underspecified.** The white paper puts it as a premise rather than a complaint: "Systems are so complicated that work situations always are underspecified — hence partly unpredictable. Few things can be done unless procedures and tools are adapted to the situation" ✅. A procedure cannot specify the situation, only a class of situations; the residual is where judgement lives.
2. **Resources and time are finite, so adjustments are approximate.** The publisher's description of the founding volume: "the performance of individuals and organizations must continually adjust to current conditions and, because resources and time are finite, **such adjustments are always approximate**" ✅. Every adjustment is a trade-off (§6).
3. **Goal conflicts are structural, not personal.** Work is pursued under multiple simultaneous objectives — production, safety, cost, service, regulatory compliance — and these conflict. The discipline's position (as Wikipedia's account states it) is that people in systems "are always faced with goal conflicts and limited resources, requiring them to constantly make trade-offs while under time pressure" ✅ (secondary source; consistent with the primary material in §6).
4. **Variability is required for success, not merely tolerated.** "Things do not go right because people behave as they are supposed to, but because people can and do adjust what they do to match the conditions of work" ✅.

The consequence for a reader of procedures: **the existence of a gap is not itself a finding.** A gap is information. Sometimes the gap is the reason the work succeeded.

### 5.3 Adaptation versus Drift — the Distinction That Makes This Usable

The most common failure of WAI/WAD thinking in practice is to treat every deviation as a warning sign, which turns the lens into a blame instrument with extra steps. The discipline's own framing distinguishes two kinds of gap, and the distinction is the difference between a useful assessment and an inquisition:

| | **Adaptation (the gap is production)** | **Drift (the gap is erosion of capacity)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What is happening** | Practice adjusts to meet real conditions the procedure could not foresee, and the adjustment *creates* safety or performance | Practice has moved, incrementally and with local justification, away from the conditions under which the system's safety argument holds (§8.4) |
| **Direction of the gap** | The gap is where judgement is adding value | The gap is where margin is being consumed |
| **What it looks like from a desk** | "They don't follow the procedure" | The same thing |
| **How to tell them apart** | Ask what the adjustment was compensating for, and whether the compensation is still sound: is the adaptation *available to others* (documented, taught, monitored) and *reversible*? | Ask whether the adjustment has become the norm, whether anyone can still state the original margin, and whether the reason for the adjustment is still true |
| **What to do** | Support it: resource it, teach it, monitor it, and feed it back into design | Treat it: restore the margin or re-derive the safety argument for the new normal — and investigate *why* the migration was locally rational |
| **Instrument failure mode** | An organisation that reads every adaptation as drift will destroy its own adaptive capacity, and will do so invisibly | An organisation that reads every drift as adaptation will normalise its way to an accident |

A crucial and honest caveat: **the distinction is genuinely hard to draw in the moment, and the same adjustment can start as adaptation and become drift as conditions change.** That is not a defect of the frame; it is the frame's point. What a WAI/WAD comparison produces is a **list of places to look and questions to ask**, not a verdict.

### 5.4 The Consequence for Procedure and Compliance Review

This is the most portable practical consequence in the entire discipline, and it can be stated as a single sentence: **a compliance or procedure review that measures only against work-as-imagined cannot see the system's real capacity.**

Worked through, for how a review typically fails:

| What a procedure review usually does | What it therefore cannot see | What a WAI/WAD review adds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reads the procedure; confirms it exists, is current, is approved and is signed off | Whether the procedure describes anything resembling the work | Observations and interviews that reconstruct the sequence actually performed |
| Samples work items and tests conformance to the procedure | Whether the procedure was *possible* — whether the work item could legally and physically have been done as written in the time available | The time, tools, information and authority actually available at the moment of decision |
| Records deviations as findings (non-compliance) | Which deviations are adaptations that produce the good outcomes | For each significant gap: what condition was it compensating for, and is the compensation still sound? |
| Produces a remediation list of "retrain" and "reissue procedure" | Whether the last procedure rewrite was itself the cause of the current workaround | The history of the workaround — how old it is, who taught it, why it persisted through previous rewrites |
| Measures compliance rate as a control indicator | The organisation's actual capacity to respond, monitor, anticipate and learn (§3) | A four-abilities profile read against the evidence of the work |

Four questions that operationalise this, worth carrying into any review:

1. **Show me the same task three ways:** as written, as taught (what a new joiner is actually told by the person beside them), and as observed (what happens at 02:00 under peak load with one system degraded).
2. **For each difference between them, name the condition it compensates for.** If nobody can name it, that gap is drift-in-progress and needs investigating — *not* for who, but for what changed.
3. **Ask what the procedure is protecting, and whether the protection still exists.** Procedures protect margins; margins erode quietly.
4. **Ask who can see the gap.** If the only people who know the real sequence are the people doing it, the organisation's monitor potential is running on individual memory (§3.2) — which is a single point of failure of an organisational kind.

### 5.5 Non-Software Examples, Alongside Software Ones

The discipline's examples come overwhelmingly from physical, high-consequence domains. Presented here as **illustrative patterns**, attributed where a source supports attribution and clearly marked as this guide's construction where it does not.

| Domain | The WAI/WAD pattern | Evidence status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aviation** | The regulator and the operator's manual describe the flight as a sequence of prescribed actions; the flight crew's actual work is continuous re-planning around weather, fuel, traffic, passenger and commercial pressures, and the "procedure" is one input among several. The field's aviation material includes Arthur Dijkstra's "Safety management in airlines" in the 2006 volume ✅ and the EUROCONTROL white paper's air-traffic-management provenance ✅. **The sharp-end/blunt-end framing came out of exactly this kind of analysis** ✅. | ✅ domain is genuinely a source of the literature |
| **Healthcare** | Clinical work is the field's richest WAI/WAD case: guidelines and care pathways describe a class of patient; the actual patient is not in the class, and the clinical team adapts. The white paper's own example: an intensive-care or emergency setting "cannot be decomposed in a meaningful way and the functions are not bimodal" ✅; and "everyday clinical work is—and must be—variable and flexible" ✅. The *Resilient Health Care* series is built on this ✅. | ✅ — and it is the domain with the largest published WAI/WAD empirical literature, e.g. the medication-dispensing study cited in Cooper's reference list (Ashour, Ashcroft & Phipps, *Applied Ergonomics* 93, 103372, 2021 ✅ citation established) |
| **Rail** | The 2006 volume contains a dedicated rail case — Hale & Heijer's "Is resilience really necessary? the case of railways" ✅ — and the RAG has been applied to railway traffic management ✅ (per Hollnagel's own list). The pattern: timetable, signalling rules and operating procedures describe an orderly railway; the railway that runs is a continuous negotiation around late running, engineering possessions and platform occupancy. | ✅ for the existence of the rail case material; ⚠ for any specific finding, which I did not read |
| **Process plant / offshore** | Nick McDonald's "Organisational resilience and industrial risk" in the 2006 volume ✅; offshore oil-and-gas applications of the RAG ✅ (per Hollnagel's own list, including an offshore logistics and emergency-response study). The pattern: the operating envelope is written for a design basis; the plant runs at conditions and with feedstocks that depart from it, and the board operator's judgement is the compensating element. | ✅ existence; ⚠ findings not read |
| **Nuclear** | Amalberti's "Five system barriers to achieving ultrasafe health care" (*Annals of Internal Medicine* 142(9), 756, 2005 ✅, citation established) is the cross-domain argument about what happens as systems approach ultra-safety — including that **increasing safety can reduce resilience** by encouraging over-standardisation and shrinking the space for expert judgement. This is one of the field's strongest ideas and is usually credited to healthcare writing; its origin here is a safety-research argument about ultra-safe systems generally. | ✅ citation; ⚠ characterisation rests on the citation's title and the field's use of it |
| **Software and IT operations** | The incident response in which the on-call engineer's actual first action is not in the runbook; the change process whose "emergency" path handles a fifth of all changes; the monitoring threshold everyone knows is wrong and had adjusted mentally; the deployment whose real pre-checks live in a chat channel rather than in the pipeline definition. | ⚠ **this guide's construction.** The pattern is real and widely reported in practitioner writing; it is not, as far as this pass established, a body of resilience-engineering *research* on software. §9 is explicit that the discipline's software engagement is thinner than its reputation. |

## 6. The Efficiency-Thoroughness Trade-Off

### 6.1 The Principle and the Book

**ETTO** is the claim that in any real work, the resources devoted to **thoroughness** — safety, completeness, accuracy, verification, double-checking — and those devoted to **efficiency** — speed, throughput, economy, service — must be traded off against each other; that the trade-off is made continuously, by everyone, mostly without noticing; and that the adjustments are **unavoidable rather than symptomatic of indiscipline**.

**Origin, established.** The principle and the book are Hollnagel's: ***The ETTO Principle: Efficiency-Thoroughness Trade-Off — Why Things That Go Right Sometimes Go Wrong***, Ashgate (Farnham), **2009** ✅ — the citation is in his own bibliography and the book is in Crossref (DOI `10.1201/9781315616247`, ISBN 9781315616247, CRC reissue 2017; chapters include "Prologue" and "Efficiency-Thoroughness Trade-Off in Practice" ✅). Hollnagel's site also indexes "The ETTO Principle (2009)" as an idea, with sub-pages on **work-related, individual and collective ETTO rules** and on **"ETTO and TETO"** ✅ — the existence of a divided rule set (work/individual/collective) is itself worth knowing, because practitioner summaries usually compress ETTO into a single aphorism. **I did not read the book or those rule pages**; the rule taxonomy is ⚠ and is not reproduced here beyond naming it.

### 6.2 Why It Is Invisible Until an Outcome Labels It

Three properties of the trade-off explain why it is systematically absent from post-incident accounts:

1. **It is made continuously and at a level below conscious deliberation.** Experienced practitioners make the adjustment as part of competent work, not as a decision. The white paper's sharpest formulation of the complementary point: "**the adjustments become increasingly important to maintain acceptable performance**" ✅ — i.e. the trade-off is *what competence looks like*, not what unconcern looks like.
2. **The same adjustment is efficient on almost every day it is made.** Trading verification for speed usually works: the thing you skipped verifying usually was fine. The adjustment is therefore reinforced by experience, and the reinforcement is rational. **The same adjustment is only labelled an error after an outcome makes it visible in hindsight.**
3. **Investigation is outcome-focused, so the adjustment appears only in the cases where it failed.** Because Safety-I investigation begins from the adverse outcome, the only instances of the trade-off that enter the record are the instances that went wrong. This is the selection-bias point from §1.2, arrived at from the other direction: **the practice of trading thoroughness for efficiency is not rare in your organisation; it is rare in your incident reports.**

This gives the discipline its most uncomfortable and most defensible claim about incident investigation: **the adjustment the investigation identifies as the cause of the incident is the same adjustment that was producing acceptable performance on all the other days, and a corrective action that removes it removes the capability along with the exposure.** That is not an argument for leaving it alone; it is an argument for understanding what it was compensating for before you remove it — which is precisely §5.3's adaptation/drift test.

### 6.3 The Consequence for Incident Investigation

| Investigation practice | Effect on the ETTO finding |
| --- | --- |
| Causes/contributory-factors template, populated from the event | ETTO appears only as "did not follow procedure" — a finding about a person, not about a trade-off |
| "Human error" as a causal category | ETTO is invisible: the error *is* the explanation, so the conditions that made the trade-off rational are never reached |
| Timeline reconstruction that includes workload, staffing, time pressure and concurrent goals | ETTO becomes visible as a **structural** feature — the same trade-off will be found in the organisation's *successful* days too, if anyone looks |
| Interviews that ask "what would have had to be true for the other choice to be the right one?" | Converts a finding about a person into a finding about conditions |
| Investigating an event with **no** adverse outcome (a save) | The only way to observe the trade-off succeeding, and therefore the only way to tell adaptation from drift |

The operating rule this guide would draw: **if your investigation cannot name the goal conflict the operator was resolving, it has not finished.** Naming it is not excusing it. A bank that discovers its payment-operations team routinely bypasses a four-eyes control at end-of-day in order to meet a cut-off has found a real control failure — *and* has found that the cut-off, the staffing model and the control were designed as if they never interact. Both findings are true; only the first is usually written down.

## 7. FRAM and the Analysis Methods

### 7.1 What FRAM Is For

**FRAM — the Functional Resonance Analysis Method — is a method for modelling how a socio-technical system functions, in order to explain how variability in individual functions can combine to produce an outcome.** It is Hollnagel's, and the citation is exact: ***FRAM: The Functional Resonance Analysis Method — Modelling Complex Socio-Technical Systems***, Ashgate (Farnham), **2012** ✅ (the author's own bibliography gives precisely this title and publisher). Its underlying position was set out earlier in his *Barriers and Accident Prevention* (2004) ✅.

**The problem it exists to solve**, in Hollnagel's own framing from the FRAM book's opening: "Virtually all accident investigations and risk assessments are conducted in a state of relative ignorance of the full behaviour of the system. This condition contrasts with the fact that all established approaches to risk assessment require that it is possible to describe the system and the scenarios in detail, i.e., that the system is tractable. Unfortunately, all socio-technical systems are more or less intractable" ✅. And: "This can be due either to the variability of a single function or – more likely and also more importantly – the combination of the variability of multiple functions over time and over space" ✅.

Three design commitments follow, all quoted from the author ✅:

- **Describe the system by what it does, not by what it is made of.** "The differences in perspective become clear when a system is defined in terms of how it functions rather than in terms of its architecture and components" ✅ — a functional rather than structural model.
- **Abandon the normal/failed binary.** "this cannot be simplified to a question of whether the system is in a 'normal' state or a 'failed' state. It is instead a question of the variability of functioning and whether the outcome is acceptable under the existing conditions. But as soon as we say variability, we also acknowledge that any 'failure' will be temporary, hence reversible" ✅.
- **Explain success and failure with one mechanism.** Variability is universal ("by definition they all are" ✅); the question is only whether variability is large enough, or coupled in a way, to defeat the intended outcome.

**Where FRAM is the right lens.** When the outcome to be explained is **not** the failure of a component, when the system cannot be decomposed into independently-behaving parts, when the same functions produced good outcomes on other days, and when the causal story you are being offered is a chain whose links each look individually benign. **Where it is the wrong lens:** when you have a genuine discrete failure (a disk, a mis-configuration, a version mismatch) with a reproducible mechanism — there, a fault tree or a straightforward causal analysis is cheaper, faster and better. FRAM's own framing of the contrast: unlike methods such as the fault tree "developed in 1962 to ensure the safety of the [Minuteman missile systems]" (per the publisher's preview of the FRAM book ✅), FRAM does not assume that outcomes have clearly recognised causes.

### 7.2 The Steps and the Vocabulary, as the Sources Actually Give Them

**This subsection is written defensively, on purpose.** The task of describing FRAM's steps accurately is where inventing steps is easiest, so here is exactly what the sources say, including where they disagree.

**The six aspects.** Each function in a FRAM model is characterised by means of **six aspects**. The FRAM 2.0 manual published on the project's own site gives them as **Input, Output, Requirements, Resources, Control, and Time** ✅. **The widely circulated alternative naming is "Input, Output, Preconditions, Resources, Time, Control"** ⚠. I could not read the 2012 book to settle which naming is authoritative for which edition, and I therefore print **both** and flag the conflict rather than choosing. The general rule for their use is also given by the manual: "an aspect of a function should be described when it is seen as necessary or appropriate by the analysis team, provided there is sufficient information or experience to do so" ✅ — i.e. **you do not have to fill all six**, which is the single most commonly misunderstood feature of the method and the reason FRAM models are simpler than people expect.

**The steps.** The project's "Introduction to FRAM" page states that "a FRAM analysis consists of **four steps**" ✅ — and then lists **five** bullets ✅. That inconsistency is in the source, and I reproduce it rather than tidying it:

| The source's list (verbatim substance) |
| --- |
| 1. "Identify and describe essential system functions, and characterise each function using the six basic characteristics (aspects). In the first version, only use describe the aspects that are necessary or relevant. The description can always be modified later." ✅ |
| 2. "Check the completeness / consistency of the model." ✅ |
| 3. "Characterise the potential variability of the functions in the FRAM model, as well as the possible actual variability of the functions in one or more instances of the model." ✅ |
| 4. "Define the functional resonance based on dependencies / couplings among functions and the potential for functional variability." ✅ |
| 5. "Identify ways to monitor the development of resonance either to dampen variability that may lead to unwanted outcomes or to amplify variability that may lead to wanted outcomes." ✅ |

The sensible reading — and §15 marks it as this guide's reading rather than the source's — is that steps 1–4 are the analytical method and step 5 is the **output/intervention** step, which is why the heading can say four and the list can show five. A reader citing "FRAM's four steps" should know that the fourth and fifth merged-versus-separated distinction is unresolved at source, and should cite the source's own wording.

**The vocabulary, defined as the method uses it.**

| Term | Meaning | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Function** | An activity described as what the system *does*, characterised by the six aspects — not a component and not an organisational unit | ✅ (source: the Introduction page and the book's model argument) |
| **Aspect** | One of the six ways a function is characterised (input, output, requirements/preconditions, resources, control, time) | ✅, with the naming conflict flagged |
| **Coupling / dependency** | The link by which one function's output is another function's input, precondition, resource, control or time constraint | ✅ (source language: "dependencies / couplings among functions") |
| **Variability** | The fact that a function's performance is not constant; the analysis distinguishes *potential* variability (what could vary) from *actual* variability in a specific instance | ✅ (source language) |
| **Instance of the model** | A concrete realisation of the model corresponding to a specific occasion of the work, whose actual variability is characterised separately from the model's potential variability | ✅ (source language: "one or more instances of the model") |
| **Instantiation** | The term used in parts of the FRAM practitioner and tooling literature for producing such an instance | ⚠ **flagged.** The word is in circulation and the *concept* is clearly in the source; **I did not verify the term "instantiation" as the method's own technical vocabulary** and I therefore do not present it as canonical. Use the source's word: *instance*. |
| **Functional resonance** | The outcome-producing combination of variability across coupled functions — the mechanism by which individually tolerable variability can aggregate into an intolerable outcome | ✅ (source language) |
| **FRAM Model Visualiser (FMV)** | Software for building and displaying FRAM models | ✅ **exists** — the project site hosts a tools overview, a November-2020 FMV user map, links to a *FRAM Handbook* for the Visualiser, and a `FRAMily-Community` GitHub organisation ✅. **No other FRAM software is named here, because I did not verify any.** |

**What the method actually requires of you** — and this is the honest cost warning that practitioner summaries omit: FRAM is a **workshop method**. It requires a team with domain knowledge, a facilitator who knows the method, sessions long enough to build and check a model, and access to people who do the work. It is not an algorithm and it is not a checklist. Hollnagel's own model guidance explicitly permits iterative refinement: describe what is necessary now, and "the description can always be modified later" ✅.

### 7.3 What It Produces, and Where It Stops

**What it produces** (per the source's own step list ✅): a functional model of the system of interest; a characterisation of where variability can arise; an explicit account of the couplings through which variability propagates; a description of functional resonance for the outcome under study; and a set of proposals for monitoring the development of resonance in order to dampen unwanted variability or amplify wanted variability.

**What that is good for:** explaining a surprising outcome without a broken component; giving an investigation a causal account that survives the question "but the same thing happens every day"; identifying **where to put monitoring** — which is the step most directly transferable to a bank's control framework; and making the couplings between organisational units visible to people who own only one of them.

**Where it stops**, stated honestly:

- **It is not predictive.** FRAM describes the potential for resonance; it does not compute a probability, and it cannot tell you which of several possible resonances will occur. It is not a substitute for quantitative risk assessment where quantitative risk assessment is meaningful.
- **It is not evidential in the regulatory sense.** It produces an explanation and a set of monitoring proposals, not a control attestation, not a pass/fail, and not a comparable score.
- **Its quality depends almost entirely on the team.** Two competent teams will produce different models of the same system. That is a feature for explanation and a problem for comparison.
- **The causal account it produces is an interpretation, not a finding.** A FRAM model is an argument about how variability couples; it can be wrong, and it is not falsifiable in the way a test result is. Its defence is that the system's own experts built it and can argue with it.
- **The empirical literature on FRAM is young.** There are applications across healthcare, aviation, maritime, rail and industry; Hollnagel's own catalogue includes an offshore-operations application with Petrobras (*OTC*, 2023 ✅) and a recent chapter "A Brief and Unofficial History of the FRAM" (CRC Press, 2025 ✅). **That is a body of applications, not a body of validation studies.**

### 7.4 The Other Named Methods, and How Tested Each Actually Is

| Method | Origin (as established) | What it is | Tested technique or practitioner method? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

**One warning about this table.** The most common error in practitioner summaries is to present every name in this section as one integrated toolkit: "use STPA for hazard analysis, FRAM for resonance, RAG for maturity, Safety-II for culture." These are **different programmes by different authors**, with different assumptions — STAMP's control-theoretic formalism sits at a different level of abstraction from FRAM's functional variability, and neither is subordinate to the other. Choose the one whose assumptions fit your question, and do not pretend they compose into a single method.


## 8. The New View of Human Error

*This is the section a banking, engineering or operations reader will get the most immediate use from, because it changes what an incident review does. Read it against whatever review template your organisation currently uses.*

### 8.1 From Cause to Consequence

The reframing is one sentence long: **"human error" is not a cause; it is a consequence of how work is designed and resourced.** The "error" is the name we give to the point at which an underlying set of conditions became an adverse outcome. In the discipline's own vocabulary, this is the **"new view"** — and following Hollnagel, the field argues that even the new view is not far enough (§8.3).

The primary material is unusually direct here, because Hollnagel wrote it as a position page on his own site, which I read in full ✅. His account of the term's three distinct uses is the most practically useful thing in this section:

> "'Human error' denotes the cause of something; the second that it denotes the event or action itself; and the third that it denotes the outcome of an action." ✅

And his diagnosis of why a term with three meanings is a problem:

> "It is an obstacle for effective and precise communication, it is a problem for measurements and statistics since it leaves uncertain what is actually being counted, and **it is a hindrance for learning since the preoccupation with finding a 'human error' means that the search for alternative – and potentially more effective – explanations is disregarded**." ✅

That last clause is the operational heart of the whole new-view argument. It is not a claim about kindness to operators. It is a **claim about the quality of the investigation**: naming a human error terminates the search, and the things left unexamined are the things that would change next time.

### 8.2 The Old View It Opposes

Hollnagel's account of the old view ✅ is worth quoting because it names the mechanism rather than the attitude:

| The old view holds | Why the field rejects it |
| --- | --- |
| That humans can be described and understood as machines — "specifically as information processing machines or systems" — which "made it possible to talk about 'human error mechanisms' and to model them" ✅ | Because a model of a mechanism implies the mechanism can be measured, and the measurement (Human Reliability Assessment / HRA, "estimate the probability of a 'human error'" ✅) is then treated as knowledge about the work rather than about the model |
| That blame attaches to the individuals "who happen to be present at the wrong time and wrong place" ✅ | Because it locates the explanation at the position of the outcome rather than at the position of the cause |
| That actions are read as expressions of dispositions ✅; that people "have the freedom to choose their actions, hence are responsible for choosing the wrong ones" ✅; and that severity is symmetric between causes and consequences ✅ | Each of the three is a distinct inferential error, and the third is the one that most damages investigations: trivial acts are made to carry catastrophic consequences, and therefore *look* like the cause |

That third mechanism — **the assumed symmetry of severity between causes and consequences** — is the most useful diagnostic in the entire discipline for spotting a bad investigation. If your causal account has a small, mundane act at one end and a large adverse outcome at the other end, and your explanation of why the small act produced the large outcome is *the small act itself*, you have not explained anything. You have described the outcome twice.

### 8.3 Normalisation of Deviance — and Who Actually Owns the Term

**This is the single most misattributed concept in the practitioner literature, and this guide will state it flatly: normalisation of deviance is Diane Vaughan's.** It comes from ***The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA***, **University of Chicago Press, 1996** ✅ — an organisational-sociology study of the *Challenger* decision, which developed the concept to explain how a sequence of acceptable, incremental, technically-justified decisions produced the conditions for a catastrophic failure. The publisher and year are established: University of Chicago Press ✅ via Crossref, and 1996 via the SAGE *Encyclopedia of Criminological Theory* entry, which states that "Vaughan developed the concept of the normalization of deviance in her landmark study… in 1996" ✅.

**The honest flag on the year.** Crossref's own records for this book are internally inconsistent: one record is dated **1995** (DOI `10.7208/chicago/9780226851785.001.0001`, ISBNs 9780226851761/9780226851754/9780226851785) and another **1997** (DOI `10.7208/chicago/9780226346960.001.0001`, ISBNs 9780226346823/9780226346960). The universally cited year is **1996**. I state 1996, note the Crossref inconsistency, and decline to print a specific ISBN, because the records disagree about which belongs to which printing. This is exactly the kind of currency trap §15 warns about, encountered live.

**It is not Hollnagel's concept. It is not Reason's concept. It is not a resilience-engineering concept** — it is organisational sociology that the safety field borrowed, correctly and gratefully. Two adjacent distinctions worth keeping straight:

- **Normalisation of deviance** (Vaughan, 1996): a *social-organisational* process by which a deviation becomes normal, with the weight on the social justification.
- **Migration to the boundary of acceptable performance** (Rasmussen, 1997 ✅): a *system-dynamics* account of work drifting toward the edge of a performance envelope as pressures accumulate — with the emphasis on the envelope and the pressures, not on the social process.
- **Drift into failure** (Dekker, 2011 ✅): the synthesis, framed for safety practice — the slow, incremental movement of organisational practice away from safety, each step locally reasonable, with no broken component to find (§8.4).

All three describe the same family of phenomena from different angles. Using the names interchangeably is the error.

### 8.4 Drift into Failure

**Dekker's, not Hollnagel's** ✅ — *Drift into Failure*, Ashgate, 2011 (Crossref monograph record with chapters dated 28 February 2011 including "Theorizing Drift" and "Features of Drift" ✅; ⚠ the subtitle *From Hunting Broken Components to Understanding Complex Systems* is widely cited but I did not verify it against a catalogue record this pass).

The claim, in the shape the field uses it: **failure in complex systems is typically not the sudden breaking of a component but the slow, incremental migration of practice — each step locally reasonable, each step supported by evidence, each step made by competent people responding to real pressures — until the system is operating outside the envelope its safety argument assumed.** Two consequences follow, and both are operationally significant:

1. **There is no "root cause" to find, and searching for one is the wrong activity.** The drift has no author. The investigation's job is to reconstruct the *descent* — what changed, in what order, and what made each step rational — rather than to locate a culpable act.
2. **The signal was usually available and unremarkable.** Drift is visible in the ordinary record: the workaround that became standard, the exception that became routine, the threshold everyone mentally adjusts, the review that has passed with the same observation three years running. This connects directly to §6.2 (ETTO's invisibility) and §3.2 (the 'monitor' ability): **drift is what an organisation can see if it decides to monitor its own capacities rather than only its outcomes.**

The practical test this guide would use: **if your organisation can point to a control or a margin that has been incrementally relaxed over the last three years with each relaxation individually justified, you are not looking for a risk; you are looking at drift.** Whether it matters is a further question — and that is the point at which the adaptation/drift test in §5.3 earns its keep.

### 8.5 Sharp End, Blunt End

**Attribution, stated precisely.** The sharp end / blunt end framing is **Woods and colleagues'**, from the *Behind Human Error* line — the 1994 CSERIAC report *Behind Human Error: Cognitive Systems, Computers, and Hindsight*, and its successor *Behind Human Error* (Woods, Dekker, Cook, Johannesen & Sarter; Crossref DOI `10.1201/9781315568935` ✅; the ⚠ on edition years is in §2.5). The formulation:

> "At the sharp end, practitioners directly interact with the hazardous process. At the blunt end, regulators, administrators, economic policy makers, and technology suppliers control the resources, constraints, and multiple incentives and demands that sharp end practitioners must [satisfy]." ✅ (from the publisher's own preview of *Behind Human Error*)

And the correlative claim, from the same preview: "The story of both success and failure consists of how sharp-end practice adapts to cope with the complexities of the processes they monitor, manage and control, and how the strategies of the people at the sharp end are shaped by the resources and constraints provided by the blunt end of the system" ✅.

**It is not Hollnagel's coinage.** He uses the terms and attributes the direction of causation correctly — his own page says incorrect actions at the sharp end "could be due to 'error forcing' conditions, often the result of uninformed decisions by the blunt end" ✅ — without claiming the framing. Note also that this is where the concept and the four abilities meet: **the blunt end determines the *monitor*, *anticipate* and *learn* capacity of the sharp end**, which is why an assessment that only observes the sharp end will keep finding the same problem.

### 8.6 What an Investigator Should Change on Monday

This guide is not going to pretend the discipline hands you a template. But six changes are implementable without buying anything:

| Change | What it replaces | The test of whether it took |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Remove "human error" as a causal category from the template.** Make it a prohibited finding, on the grounds Hollnagel gives: it terminates the search ✅. | "Operator error — retrain" | A review report in which the operator's action appears as a *link* in the account, not as its conclusion |
| **Require the goal conflict to be named.** If the investigation cannot state which trade-off was being made (ETTO, §6), it is not finished. | A timeline with no account of the pressures that shaped it | A finding about conditions rather than a finding about a person |
| **Require the blunt-end factor to be named.** What resource, constraint, incentive or supplier decision shaped the sharp end's options (Woods) ✅? | Blame migrating up one level to "management" as a category | A specific, ownable, changeable factor — a staffing model, a cut-off, a tool |
| **Require the drift history.** How old is the practice? Did the same observation appear in previous reviews? (§8.4) | A finding with no time dimension | Evidence that the organisation checked its own record rather than only the event |
| **Investigate at least some events that had no adverse outcome.** The recovery, the save, the near-miss, the unremarkable good day (§4.2). | An evidence base drawn only from the failures | At least one case in which the *same* trade-off is shown succeeding — which is the only way to tell adaptation from drift |
| **Protect the account.** Workarounds will not be reported to a process that punishes them, and an investigation that requires them will be fed a fiction. | Learning that is actually learning | A governance decision, not a cultural aspiration: the finding is about the *design*, and the person is not the subject |

## 9. The Application to Software and IT Operations

### 9.1 The Blameless Post-Incident Review

The clearest and most consequential translation of this discipline into computing practice is the **blameless post-incident review** — the convention that an incident review reconstructs the operators' situation, their information, their goals and their constraints, and that it does not use the review to allocate blame to an individual. The practice is mainstream in large-scale software operations and is closely associated with site reliability engineering and with the web-operations practitioner community.

**Here is the honest statement of provenance, and it matters.** The blameless review is **not** a resilience-engineering invention, and resilience engineering did **not** produce it. It is a practitioner convention of the software-operations world, arrived at independently, that **converges on the same conclusion** this discipline had reached from aviation and healthcare: that blaming the person at the sharp end both (a) fails to explain anything useful and (b) suppresses the information you need next time. The convergence is real and worth stating; the genealogy is not the same. Note also that *Communications of the ACM*'s November 2012 piece "Resilience engineering: learning to embrace failure" ✅ was a discussion with practitioners including John Allspaw — which is precisely a data point for the convergence happening in the computing literature, in the colloquial sense of the phrase, under the same name the safety community was using.

For a banking reader, the point of this subsection is narrower than "do blameless reviews". It is: **a blameless review that does not name the goal conflict, the blunt-end factor and the drift history is only half the discipline** — it has stopped blaming the individual but has not yet explained the outcome. §8.6 is the completing half.

### 9.2 How Complex Systems Actually Fail

The discipline's contribution to software reliability thinking is a set of claims about failure, and they are stated here as claims with their status.

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| In complex socio-technical systems, adverse outcomes often involve **no broken component**; the components each behaved as designed and the interactions produced the outcome | ✅ as the field's central claim (the founding volume's own description: for Resilience Engineering, "'failure' is the result of the adaptations necessary to cope with the complexity of the real world, rather than a breakdown or malfunction" ✅). Corroborated in spirit by the chaos-engineering canon's own observation about interactions between individually-functioning services ✅ |
| **Success and failure have the same source** — "failures are the flip side of successes" ✅ (Hollnagel's own phrasing) | ✅ as the field's position; ⚠ as an empirical generalisation, since the field's evidence is largely case-based (§11.3) |
| **Human performance variability has positive effects as well as negative ones**, and safety is increased by amplifying the positive ones — and therefore "adding controls to mitigate the effects of human variability can reduce safety in certain circumstances" | ✅ as a stated position of the discipline (restated in the encyclopaedic secondary literature ✅); ⚠ as an empirical claim. **This is the claim most likely to be abused in practice**: it is not an argument against controls, and the field does not present it as one |
| **Some failure modes are literally inconceivable in advance** ("fundamental surprise") because the environment is dynamic and perspectives are inherently limited | ✅ as a position of the discipline ✅ (secondary restatement); this is the sharpest contrast with probabilistic risk assessment, which evaluates **conceivable** risks |
| **Expert operators are an important source of resilience**, and expertise is built through experience of dealing with failures | ✅ as a position ✅ (secondary). Operationally: your ability to cope depends on how many of your people have been present at a real recovery — which in a heavily-automated, rarely-failing estate becomes scarce |
| **Risk is unavoidable**, and operators are always trading off risks, so creating safety sometimes requires taking on risk | ✅ as a position ✅ (secondary) |

Read the last two together and you get the claim this discipline would most want a bank's technology leadership to hear: **an organisation that eliminates all failures eliminates the experience that produces the capacity to handle one.**

### 9.3 Production as a System with Operators, Not a Pipeline with Procedures

The reframing of production as a **socio-technical system that people operate and adapt**, rather than a pipeline that executes procedures, is where this discipline and modern reliability practice most clearly agree. The pattern, stated as it would be observed in a bank's estate:

- The deployment pipeline's documented path (`work-as-imagined`) is not the path most changes take. The emergency/exception route, the manual hotfix, the configuration change applied directly under supervision — these are the actual work (work-as-done, §5).
- The on-call engineer's first action is usually not the first step of the runbook. It is a diagnostic move built from experience of *this* service, and the runbook is consulted afterwards to confirm.
- The change-approval control's real function is not what the control description says. It often functions as a **communication and scheduling mechanism** — a known time at which a person who holds context is present — and its safety contribution comes from that, not from the reviewing.

Each of these, examined through the discipline's lens, produces a different corrective action than the compliance lens does. Compliance says: the exception route is a control weakness; close it. The WAI/WAD lens says: **the exception route is handling the cases the standard route was not designed to handle; find out why the standard route cannot handle them, and decide whether it should.** Sometimes the answer is that it should and the exception route can be closed safely — in which case the compliance conclusion is right, but it is now *justified* rather than merely asserted, and the change will survive contact with the work.

### 9.4 The Honest Boundary — a Lens Is Not an Intervention

**State this plainly, because it is the boundary the whole guide is built around:**

> **A chaos experiment is an intervention. This discipline is a lens.** A chaos experiment removes a capability on purpose, under observation, with a hypothesis, to find out what breaks and whether the system survives — it *changes the system* and *produces a test result*. This discipline does not change the system and does not produce a test result; it changes *what you see* when you look at the work, and produces *an explanation and a set of questions*.

The corollary, for the reader who works in [../technology/chaos_engineering_guide.md](../technology/chaos_engineering_guide.md)'s world:

- **Chaos engineering cannot answer a WAI/WAD question.** If you want to know whether the runbook matches the work, injecting a fault and watching what the on-call engineer does will tell you about the *fault's* effects and the *response's* mechanics — not about whether the response resembles the procedure, or about the trade-offs the responder was already making before the fault. You get that from observing, interviewing, and reconstructing — the lens.
- **This discipline cannot answer a chaos-engineering question.** If you want to know whether the payments switch survives an availability-zone loss, no amount of four-abilities assessment or FRAM modelling will tell you. You need the intervention. And an organisation can be an exemplar of adaptive capacity with a purely theoretical failover.
- **They compose, and the composition is the interesting part.** The lens tells you *which* experiments are worth running — because it finds the trade-offs and couplings that the architecture diagram does not show — and the intervention tells you whether the compensating capacity you believed in actually exists. Neither substitutes for the other, and the *Resilience Engineering in Practice* framing about "basic patterns in how adaptive systems fail" (Woods & Branlat ✅) is exactly the material you would use to decide what to test.
- **Where [../technology/zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](../technology/zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) sits.** That guide owns *designed* capacity — the redundancy, the patterns, the availability mathematics, the SLO machinery. This guide studies *enacted* capacity — what the organisation can actually do, as opposed to what its architecture permits. A bank can be excellent at the first and poor at the second, which is the specific pathology §13's worked example describes.

**And the honest statement of the discipline's own software footprint.** The Wikipedia article notes that resilience-engineering researchers have studied the non-safety-critical domain of software operations ✅ (secondary). But the volume of that work is small relative to the discipline's aviation, healthcare and process-industry material, and **this repository contains no SRE guide and no dedicated post-incident-review guide** — the closest material is the blameless-review discussion in the chaos guide's practice sections, and the availability engineering in the zero-downtime guide. That absence is worth naming: this repository has strong coverage of the *intervention* tradition and no coverage of the *post-incident practice* tradition that the safety-science literature actually informs most directly.

## 10. The Safety-Critical Industries

The purpose of this section is not to list domains but to state, per domain, **what the discipline claims it revealed and the honest evidence status of that claim**. The pattern across all five is: **the domains are where the field's ideas came from and where they are applied; the evidence is largely case-based, conceptual and expert-judgement-based; and no domain in this list has produced the kind of controlled evaluation that would settle whether the ideas improve outcomes.**

### 10.1 Aviation

**What it contributed to the discipline.** Aviation and air traffic control are the discipline's deepest source. The **EUROCONTROL** white paper (Hollnagel, Leonhardt, Licu & Shorrock, Brussels, 2013 ✅) is the field's earliest formal Safety-I/Safety-II statement, commissioned by the European organisation for the safety of air navigation; EUROCONTROL's **SKYbrary** hosts and circulates the field's explanatory material, including Hollnagel's WAI/WAD explanation ✅ (hosting verified by the URL and its position in the search index; ⚠ **the PDF itself failed extraction twice in this pass**, so I characterise it from its indexed description only). The 2006 volume includes Arthur Dijkstra's "Safety management in airlines" ✅. The sharp-end/blunt-end framing came out of this tradition's analysis ✅.

**What it claims.** That safety management built on the absence of adverse outcomes cannot see how aviation's ordinary good days are produced, and that the recovery capacity in the system is carried by crews and controllers adapting in real time.

**Honest evidence status.** ⚠ **Adoption is documented; safety-outcome improvement is not.** The verifiable claim is that a major European air-navigation safety body published a white paper adopting the Safety-II frame ✅, and that the field's concepts are circulated in aviation safety training material ✅. I found **no** source in this pass showing a measured safety-outcome improvement attributable to the approach, and I state that as a retrieval gap rather than as a refutation.

### 10.2 Healthcare and the Resilient Health Care Line

**What it is, verified.** The discipline's largest applied programme is healthcare, under the name **Resilient Health Care**. The books are a numbered series — *Resilient Health Care* (2013 ✅), *Volume 2* (2015 ✅), *Volume 3* (2017, the WAI/WAD volume ✅), *Delivering Resilient Health Care* (Routledge, 2018 ✅), *Working Across Boundaries* (2019 ✅), with further volumes in the Routledge series ✅ — and there is now a **Resilient Health Care Society** that co-locates its annual meeting with the REA symposium (the 14th annual meeting was joint with the 11th REA Symposium in Canela, Brazil, October 2025 ✅). The 2015 white paper is branded "The Resilient Health Care Net" and was published simultaneously by the **University of Southern Denmark, the University of Florida and Macquarie University** ✅, and distributed in England through **NHS England**'s sign-up-to-safety channel ✅. Hollnagel's own bibliography places the origin of the healthcare line at 2011 ✅.

**What it claims.** That clinical work is irreducibly variable and must be, that safety in healthcare has improved glacially despite decades of investment, and that the reason is that patient-safety orthodoxy imported a linear, component-failure model that does not fit clinical work. The 2015 white paper's own framing: "the common model that informed early patient safety efforts… was based on linear cause-and-effect, component failure models. Just as any disease must have a cause that can be diagnosed and treated, so will any adverse event have a cause that can be found and fixed" ✅.

**The research base, named.** Sujan, Huang & Braithwaite, "Learning from incidents in health care: Critique from a Safety-II perspective", *Safety Science* 99, 115–121, 2017 ✅ (a critique *from* Safety-II); Cook & Rasmussen's "'Going solid'" in *Quality and Safety in Health Care* ✅ (the system-dynamics account of hospitals running at their capacity limit); the Applied Ergonomics WAI/WAD empirical work, e.g. medication dispensing in community pharmacy (Ashour, Ashcroft & Phipps, 2021 ✅, established through a reference list); Amalberti's *Annals of Internal Medicine* paper on ultra-safe systems ✅.

**Honest evidence status.** ⚠ **This is the field's richest applied literature and still not outcome evidence.** What exists is a large body of case studies, interview studies, conceptual papers and literature reviews, plus a documented institutional adoption (a national health service channel, university publication, a standing society and annual meeting) ✅. What does not exist in anything I retrieved is a controlled or quasi-experimental evaluation showing that adopting these concepts improves patient outcomes. **I am reporting the absence of a retrieval, not the absence of evidence** — and it is worth noting that the field's own critics make the same point (§11.3).

### 10.3 Nuclear Power and the Process Industries

**Nuclear.** The white paper's own history places the Safety-I view's ascent in nuclear and aviation "between the 1960s and 1980s" ✅ — so the domain is the origin of the orthodoxy being argued against, not of the argument. Hollnagel's catalogue includes an edited volume on safer complex industrial environments (2009 ✅). Beyond that, **⚠ I did not retrieve a body of resilience-engineering research grounded in nuclear power operations**, and I decline to narrate one.

**Process industries and offshore.** Better evidenced. The 2006 volume includes Nick McDonald's "Organisational resilience and industrial risk" ✅ and Yushi Fujita's several contributions ✅. The RAG has been applied to offshore oil-and-gas logistics and emergency response ✅ (per Hollnagel's own application list), and his catalogue includes a FRAM application to offshore operations with Petrobras (*OTC*, 2023 ✅). The process-industry pattern the discipline describes — operating envelopes written for a design basis, plant running outside them, board operators' judgement as the compensating element — is the same WAI/WAD pattern as §5.5.

**Honest evidence status.** ✅ for the existence of the applications, ⚠ for any finding, since the applications are theses, conference papers and consultancy reports rather than controlled studies.

### 10.4 Rail

**Verified material.** Andrew Hale and Tom Heijer's "Is resilience really necessary? the case of railways" is one of the founding volume's cases ✅ — note the question mark, which is itself notable: one of the discipline's own foundation chapters disputes the necessity of the concept in that domain. The RAG has been applied to **railway traffic management** ✅ (Rigaud et al., per Hollnagel's own list, and there is a Linköping thesis applying the RAG in air-traffic management ✅). Hale's work on rules and procedures (with Borys ✅) is directly relevant to rail's proceduralisation.

**Honest evidence status.** ⚠ case studies and applications; the founding case is more of a *challenge* to the concept than a demonstration of it.

## 11. The Critique and the Limits

*Everything in this section is presented as the field's own debates, with citations, rather than as this guide's verdict. Where I give a verdict, I say so.*

### 11.1 Conceptually Rich, Operationally Hard

The most consistently reported difficulty is the translation from concepts to practice. The evidence for this is not a hostile source; it is the field's own output. **Patriarca, Bergström, Di Gravio & Costantino, "Resilience engineering: Current status of the research and future challenges", *Safety Science* 102, 79–100, 2018** ✅ is a systematic review of the field, and its title and genre tell you what it concluded: a research field with a status to assess and futures to propose, not a set of settled instruments. **Bergström, van Winsen & Henriqson, "On the rationale of resilience in the domain of safety: A literature review", *RESS* 141, 131–141, 2015** ✅ is the same exercise for the concept's justification. And the fact that the discipline's own assessment instrument is a **questionnaire whose quality depends on the judgement of those applying it** ✅ (§3.4), applied in an opportunistic list of domains that its originator describes as having "no comprehensive list of practical examples" ✅, is a fair summary of the operationalisation problem — stated by the originator, not by a critic.

The RAG/SPM application list is instructive in this respect. Railways, offshore logistics, healthcare (including emergency departments), radiation protection, a prison's performance-management system, Brazilian Army aviation, air-traffic management ✅. Every one of these is a study, a thesis, an agency guideline or a domain application. **None is a randomised comparison, and none is a score you can compare across organisations** — which is the structural reason this discipline resists becoming a management indicator.

### 11.2 The Direct Published Attack on Safety-II

This guide will not pretend the criticism is mild, and it will not invent its contents.

**Cooper, M.D., "The Emperor has no clothes: A critique of Safety-II", *Safety Science* 152, 105047** ✅ — verified through Crossref's full record: Elsevier; **ISSN 0925-7535**; **264 references**; the record's creation date is **24 October 2020** and the print issue is **August 2022**, so the DOI's embedded year (`10.1016/j.ssci.2020.105047`) and the citation year you will see in a reference list do not match — a currency trap worth knowing about. It is, as far as this pass establishes, **the field's most direct published critique of Safety-II, and it is in the same journal that publishes the field's positive work**.

**What I can honestly say about its content, and what I cannot.** I read the Crossref record, not the article. From the record I can verify the title, the journal, the volume, the article number, the year and the scale of its engagement (264 references) ✅. From the **partially-retrieved reference list** I can say that it engages with the resilience-engineering literature directly — including the field's own reviews, Woods' 2015 paper, the RAG's practical applications, the influential "resilient organisation" intervention literature (Cantu et al., *Applied Ergonomics* 90, 103241, 2021 ✅), Safety-Differently/HOP practitioner material, and the concept-dilution literature (e.g. Alexander's etymology of resilience ✅, Bhamra et al.'s resilience literature review ✅, Busch's "can positive developments in safety science and practice also have negative sides?" ✅) — **and I will not characterise its arguments beyond that.** The title states the thesis; the detail belongs to the article, which anyone relying on this should read.

**What it means for a reader.** That Safety-II is a **contested position in the peer-reviewed literature**, not a settled advance. Any internal proposal you make that rests on Safety-II should expect to be challenged on exactly this ground, and should be built so that it survives the challenge — which in practice means: adopt it as a **lens for investigation and resourcing**, not as a replacement for adverse-outcome management, and keep the existing control framework running underneath it (which is what the field's own white paper says to do — §4.3).

**One gap, stated as a gap.** I searched for a published journal reply by Hollnagel to this critique and **did not find one**; the only "reply to the Emperor has no clothes" I located was on an unrelated topic. **An unsuccessful search is not evidence that no reply exists.** What I can verify is that Hollnagel's 2026 material is, in tone and content, a response to the debate rather than an abandonment of the position: his January 2026 *Fifth Paradox of Safety* keynote and post argue that "as organisations invest in metrics, indicators, and quantified safety targets, they risk substituting measurement for understanding. What gets counted is not always what matters, and the act of measuring can reshape practice in ways that undermine the very performance being measured" ✅ — which is a defence of the Safety-II diagnosis, not a retraction of it.

### 11.3 The Evidence Base Is Largely Qualitative and Case-Based

**This is not this guide's opinion; it is a description of what the field's own counts of its output look like, and the labels below are honest ones.**

| What exists | Volume | What it can settle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conceptual and theoretical papers | Large | What the concepts mean |
| Case studies and domain applications | Large (the RAG/SPM list ✅; the FRAM application literature ✅) | That the concepts can be applied and what they produced in that case |
| Literature reviews of the field | Several ✅ (Bergström et al. 2015; Patriarca et al. 2018; Cantu et al. 2021 on intervention and measurement) | What the field has studied. **Note that one of these is a review specifically of "interventions and measurements" ✅ — the field itself recognises measurement as a gap.** |
| Qualitative empirical studies (interviews, observations) | Growing, particularly in healthcare ✅ (e.g. the WAI/WAD pharmacy-dispensing study ✅) | What people say and do in a setting |
| Quantitative comparative studies | **Scarce** | — |
| Controlled or quasi-experimental evaluations of the approach as an intervention | **None retrieved in this pass** | — |

The consequence for how you should use this guide is direct: **use the discipline's concepts as hypotheses and as interrogation tools, and do not present them to a decision-maker as evidence.** A four-abilities assessment is a *structured hypothesis* about where your organisation's capacity is thin. It becomes evidence when you test it — by changing something and observing, which is the intervention tradition's job, not this one's.

### 11.4 Concepts That Resist Measurement

Three structural reasons the field resists measurement, each of which is a *feature* of the thinking and a *problem* for management:

1. **The object of study is capacity, and capacity is observed by what does *not* happen.** A strong respond potential is visible mainly in the absences — the events that did not occur. Counterfactual capacity is not countable, and every attempt to count it (leading indicators, safety climate scores, resilience metrics) reintroduces exactly the proxy-measurement problem Hollnagel's 2026 paradox identifies ✅.
2. **Variability is the unit, and variability has no natural scale.** FRAM deals in "the variability of functioning", and its own framing insists that any failure is "temporary, hence reversible" ✅ — which is a description, not a score.
3. **The frame deliberately refuses decomposition.** The founding statement is that "failure is the result of the adaptations necessary to cope with the complexity of the real world" ✅ — a claim that resists being split into measurable parts without losing what it is about.

The most pointed version of this critique comes from the originator: **the act of measuring can reshape practice in ways that undermine the performance being measured** ✅ (*Fifth Paradox of Safety*, January 2026). Read that as a warning about building a RESILIENCE KPI — including a resilience KPI derived from this guide.

### 11.5 "Resilience" Diluted Into a Management Word

The dilution is documented and the field knows it. The clearest evidence is that **Woods devoted a paper to it**: "Four concepts for resilience and the implications for the future of resilience engineering", *RESS* 141, 5–9, 2015 ✅, whose subject is the proliferation of technical meanings of "resilience" and the need to keep them distinct. The concept-dilution literature is broader still, and Cooper's reference list reaches into it explicitly — Alexander's "Resilience and disaster risk reduction: An etymological journey" ✅ and Bhamra et al.'s "Resilience: the concept, a literature review and future directions" ✅ both appear there. Dekker's 2006 chapter title — **"Resilience engineering: chronicling the emergence of confused consensus"** ✅ — says that the field was already worried about this in its founding volume.

The practical form of the dilution in a corporate setting: "resilience" becomes a word attached to whatever the organisation already wanted to do. A redundancy programme, a business-continuity refresh, a culture campaign, a cost-reduction exercise with a new name, a monitoring platform. The diagnostic question that cuts through it, and the one this guide would use: **does the use of the word change what is measured, what is investigated, or how capacity is resourced?** If none of the three, the word is decoration (§14, anti-pattern 5).

### 11.6 The Field's Own Internal Disputes

A guide that presented this as a consensus would misrepresent it. Five disputes are documented in what this pass retrieved:

| Dispute | Who, and where |
| --- | --- |
| **Is "resilience" even a coherent single concept?** | Woods, *RESS* 141, 5–9, 2015 ✅ — the paper exists precisely because it does not obviously cohere; and Dekker's 2006 chapter on "confused consensus" ✅ |
| **Is safety and resilience the same goal?** | René Amalberti, "Optimum system safety and optimum system resilience: agonistic or antagonistic concepts?", in the 2006 volume ✅ — the founding volume's own chapter poses the question. Amalberti's ultra-safe-systems argument (*Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2005 ✅) supplies the substance: pushing safety very high can shrink the room for the adaptation that supplies resilience |
| **Is resilience engineering necessary in this domain at all?** | Hale & Heijer, "Is resilience really necessary? the case of railways", 2006 ✅ |
| **Does Safety-II withstand scrutiny?** | Cooper, *Safety Science* 152, 105047, 2022 ✅ (§11.2), against the field's own advocacy papers, e.g. Provan, Woods, Dekker & Rae, *RESS* 195, 106740, 2020 ✅ ("How resilience engineering can transform safety practice" — advocacy is the correct word for that title) |
| **Has any of it changed practice, and should it?** | Flinterman, "From Safety-I myths to the improbable normal: reflections on Hollnagel's legacy in safety practice", *Safety Science* 200, 107227, 2026 ✅ — a 2026 stock-take of how the ideas "have travelled from academic debate into — or failed to enter — everyday safety management" (characterised from Hollnagel's own description of the paper ✅, not from the paper itself ⚠), and Hollnagel's 2026 response on measurement ✅ |

**And the field's own honest line about itself**, from the most authoritative primary source available: the 2015 white paper states that Safety-I and Safety-II are "two complementary views", that existing methods "can continue to be used", and that the way forward "lies in combining the two ways of thinking" ✅. That is the position this guide adopts, because it is the position the field's own primary document takes — and because the alternative reading, that Safety-II has superseded Safety-I, is not supported by the field's own texts and is contradicted by the critique in §11.2.

## 12. The Regulated-Enterprise and Banking Angle

*This section cross-references rather than re-derives. The regulatory machinery — DORA, MAS, PRA/FCA, BCBS, impact tolerances, severe-but-plausible scenarios, testing and assurance — is owned by [../banking/operational_resilience_framework_guide.md](../banking/operational_resilience_framework_guide.md). The Singapore instrument set is owned by [../banking/mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md](../banking/mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md). This section adds only the part those guides do not own: what happens when you put a regulator's measurement frame next to a safety scientist's lens.*

### 12.1 What a Regulator Measures, and Why

A supervisor's frame is not a simplification of this discipline; it is a **different instrument with a different purpose**, and it is a defensible one.

| The regulator's requirement | Why it has this shape | What it therefore produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identify **important business services** and set **impact tolerances** | Because harm must be bounded in business terms, and because a service-level construct is auditable and comparable | A partition of the firm into services, each with a tolerance expressed in time and impact |
| Assess **severe-but-plausible** scenarios | Because plausibility is arguable, but *severity* and *plausibility* are at least statable and challengeable | A scenario library, refreshed, with assumptions on record |
| **Test** the ability to remain within tolerance | Because a supervisor must be able to see evidence rather than assurance | Exercise records, test results, remediation tracking |
| Governance and accountability at board level | Because accountability must attach to a named body | Board attestation, allocation of responsibilities |
| Third-party and concentration risk | Because the service may depend on someone outside the perimeter | Outsourcing registers, exit plans, concentration analysis |

That is a **measurement-and-assurance frame**, and the operational-resilience guide's §2, §4–5, §6, §8 and §11 own all of it ✅ (cross-referenced, not restated).

### 12.2 Where the Two Are Complementary

Four places where the discipline and the regulatory frame genuinely complement each other, rather than competing:

| The regulatory frame gives you | This discipline adds | The combined question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Impact tolerances** — a stated maximum acceptable disruption per service | The **four abilities**: whether the organisation can respond to the actual, monitor the critical, anticipate the potential and learn from the factual — *including for scenarios not in the library* | "Within tolerance — but by what capacity, and would that capacity survive a disruption nobody enumerated?" |
| **Severe-but-plausible scenarios** — a disciplined way to enumerate plausible futures | **Anticipate** as a named potential, and the discipline's insistence that some failure modes are inconceivable in advance ✅ (§9.2) | "Which of our severe-but-plausible scenarios are actually *imaginable* scenarios, and what is our capacity for the unimaginable remainder?" |
| **Testing and assurance** — evidence that the plan works | **Work-as-done** (§5): what actually happens when the plan is executed, including the adaptations | "The test passed. Was the sequence that passed the sequence our people would actually perform, or the sequence the test script prescribed?" |
| **Accountability and governance** — a named owner per service | **Blunt end / sharp end** (§8.5): that the resources and constraints set at the blunt end determine what the sharp end can do | "The owner is named; do they control the resourcing that determines whether the tolerance is achievable?" |

### 12.3 Where the Regulatory Frame Is Structurally Blind

**This is the analytical heart of the section, and it is a statement about the instrument, not a criticism of supervisors.** Each row states a structural blindness and why it is structural — i.e. why the regulator could not fix it by trying harder without becoming a different kind of institution.

| Blindness | Why it is structural, not an oversight |
| --- | --- |
| **The regulatory frame cannot see the adaptations that produce the good outcomes.** It sees conformance and non-conformance against a plan and a tolerance | Because a supervisor must assess against a stated standard; and a standard can only describe work-as-imagined. A supervisor who accepted "the procedure was not followed, but the deviation was the reason nothing worse happened" as evidence of strength would have no way to distinguish that from a firm explaining away non-compliance |
| **Impact tolerances bound harm on the critical path.** Capacity that is not on the path of an important business service is invisible to the tolerance | Because tolerances are attached to services by construction. The cross-cutting capacity — expertise, slack, informal coordination between teams — has no service to attach to |
| **Severe-but-plausible scenario selection is done in advance, by the firm.** It cannot test the response to the unanticipated, which is the response that matters most | Because a test must be scoped before it is run. The unanticipated scenario cannot be scoped, and an unscoped exercise is not auditable |
| **Assurance is periodic and retrospective.** A supervisor examines evidence of what was done | Because supervision is an oversight function operating at a distance and in cycles. Between cycles, the capacity can degrade without any rule being broken |
| **Comparability is a requirement.** A supervisor must be able to compare firms and over time | Because this discipline's primary instrument is a questionnaire whose outputs are not comparable across organisations (§3.4, §11.4). A regulator cannot regulate on an instrument that two competent assessors will use differently |
| **Metric-driven compliance can substitute measurement for understanding.** | Because if it is measured, it will be managed — and Hollnagel's own 2026 argument ✅ is that "the act of measuring can reshape practice in ways that undermine the very performance being measured". A supervisor requiring an indicator may thereby degrade the thing the indicator was meant to show |

**The most useful single sentence in this section**, if you take one thing to a governance forum: *a regulator must measure what is measurable, which means the regulatory frame is systematically better at testing the response to anticipated disruption than at revealing the capacity that handles the rest — and no amount of additional testing changes that, because it is a property of measurement, not of effort.*

### 12.4 What a Bank's Programme Could Borrow, and the Caveat

**Six things transfer, none of which requires abandoning the regulatory programme.** They are ordered by cost, cheapest first. **Every figure is illustrative and fictional; Cymbal Bank is the only bank persona used in this repository and no real institution is described anywhere in this section.**

| # | What to borrow | What it looks like in a programme | Illustrative effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Add four-abilities questions to the debrief you already run.** You already run post-exercise debriefs for scenario testing. Add four questions: what did we respond to that we had not planned for; what did we fail to monitor; what did we anticipate correctly; what did we learn that changed something? | Zero new exercises; a modified debrief template and one page added to the results pack | Small — a template change |
| 2 | **Investigate the saves.** Require that a fixed share of incident reviews examine an event with no adverse outcome — a recovery, a near-miss, an unremarkable good day under pressure. | Adds reviews of the kind the programme currently ignores; produces the evidence that distinguishes adaptation from drift (§5.3) | Moderate — process and capability |
| 3 | **One WAI/WAD reconstruction per year for one critical process.** Not all of them: one. Observe it, interview the people who do it, reconstruct the actual sequence, compare it to the procedure, and classify every material gap as adaptation or drift (§5.3) with a named condition for each. | A short study with a written reconstruction and a classified gap list | Moderate — a few weeks of an analyst's time; the discipline's cheapest useful output |
| 4 | **Report workarounds as information rather than as findings.** Give the workaround register a governance home where its purpose is design feedback, with protection for the reporter. | A standing item in the operational-resilience forum: "workarounds observed, and what each is compensating for" | Moderate — and it is a governance decision, not a tooling one |
| 5 | **Monitor the capacities, not only the outcomes.** Withdraw the resilience KPI temptation: instead of scoring resilience, track ordinary capacity facts — how many people have been present at a real recovery in the last year; how many critical processes depend on one person's undocumented knowledge; how many scheduled drills were displaced by change pressure. | A small capacity appendix to the existing operational-resilience report | Moderate — a data exercise with a real risk of becoming a KPI (§11.4) |
| 6 | **A FRAM analysis of one recurrent, non-component failure.** For the incident class that keeps recurring with no broken component — the reconciliation break, the cut-off miss, the batch dependency surprise — model the functions and their couplings, and use it to decide where to put monitoring. | One facilitated workshop series with the people who do the work; a model, a variability description, and monitoring proposals | Large — weeks of skilled facilitation, and it needs a facilitator who knows the method, not a template |

**The caveat, stated plainly and not buried at the end.** A regulator must measure what is measurable, and a bank cannot ask its supervisor to accept a questionnaire in place of evidence. So the borrowing has to be **additive and subordinated**: the four abilities and work-as-done are how the firm decides *what to test, what to resource and what to investigate*; the regulatory programme remains how the firm *demonstrates* that it is in control. Where an item from the table above produces a finding, the finding must be converted into something the existing assurance frame already recognises — a control change, a testable scenario, a remediation with an owner, a resourced capability — or it will not survive its first board review. **A resilience-engineering pilot that cannot be reconciled to the existing reporting line will be read as an interesting diversion, and it will be right.**


## 13. The Cymbal Bank Worked Example

**This section is explicitly FICTIONAL and ILLUSTRATIVE.** Cymbal Bank is a repository persona, not a real institution. Every incident, observation, rating, queue length and cost band below is invented in order to show the **shape** of a four-abilities assessment — nothing here is a benchmark, a survey result, an anonymised real case, or evidence that any technique works. Cymbal Bank is the only bank named in this section; no other institution and no vendor is named anywhere in it.

### 13.1 The Incident That Procedures Did Not Predict

On the last business day of a quarter, the overnight reconciliation between the core ledger and the payments hub produced an unmatched-break count roughly nine times the day's tolerance. The cause the review eventually reconstructed was not exotic: a scheme settlement file arrived late, and when it was reprocessed it arrived **incomplete** — a valid file structure with a short record count. The intake job accepted it, because its validation tested structure, not completeness. The breaks that resulted were cleared through the documented manual-acceptance (override) path, the end-of-day cut-off was met, and no payment was delayed. Two days later a client-visible misposting surfaced from a break that had been accepted on the assumption that every break in the run was a timing artefact.

**Why the procedures did not predict it.** The runbook for a late scheme file has a branch per known condition: file absent, scheme unavailable, cut-off at risk. It has no branch for **file present and incomplete**, because the procedure was written against the assumption that intake validation guaranteed completeness — an assumption that was true when the validation ran unconditionally and stopped being true when the processing window tightened (§5.2, §8.4). The procedure did not fail; it described a system that no longer existed.

**What the review could and could not conclude.** No control breached. Every approval was obtained, every record exists, the tolerance was met. That is precisely the incident class §7.1 and §9.2 describe: an outcome with no broken component, produced by variability in coupled functions, each of which behaved acceptably. Which is why the review was escalated from the assurance programme to a four-abilities assessment.

### 13.2 The Four-Abilities Assessment

The profile is deliberately **asymmetric**, and the asymmetry is the finding. Ratings are structured judgements from observation, interviews and documents — not measurements, and not comparable to any other organisation (§3.4, §11.4).

| Ability | What was observed | The evidence it rests on | Honest rating |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Respond** | The cut-off was met. An unplanned branch (hold, reprocess, accept, re-verify next morning) was designed and executed by the shift team inside about twenty minutes, without waiting for a script. Escalation reached someone empowered to decide. | The incident timeline; the acceptance records; testimony from all three shifts; the fact that no prepared action existed for this condition and an outcome was still produced | **Strong** — and strong in the specific sense that matters: the team could act *without* a prepared action, not merely execute one |
| **Monitor** | The break count was monitored against tolerance. The **file-completeness signal was not monitored at all** — the intake job logged record counts, but nothing compared them to an expected value and nothing displayed them. The condition was noticed because an operator recalled the day's volume "looking light". | The intake log (the signal exists and is discarded); the absence of any dashboard panel on completeness; the fact that detection depended on one person's memory | **Weak-to-partial** — the organisation monitors outcomes and not the capacity that produces them (§3.2) |
| **Anticipate** | The severe-but-plausible library contained "scheme unavailable" and "file not received by cut-off". It did not contain "file received, structurally valid, materially incomplete", and no exercise, review or risk assessment had reasoned about it. | The scenario library index; the absence of a partially-processed-file case in any exercise record for three years | **Weak** — the horizon is the enumerated past plus the obvious future |
| **Learn** | The same signature — a light file on a quarter-end — had occurred twice in the previous eighteen months. Both produced a **procedure addendum**, neither produced a change to intake validation, and neither addendum appears in the team's onboarding material. | The two prior review records; the addenda; the onboarding pack, which contains neither | **Weak** — learning is recorded rather than acted on, and the record decays |

**Why this profile is the expected one for a bank, and not an anomaly.** Responding well is *what the institution has been built and paid to do*: the regulatory programme (§12.1) funds, rehearses, tests and reports recovery capability, and a bank with mature operational resilience will have a genuinely strong respond potential. Anticipating and learning are **not funded, not rehearsed, not measured and have no named owner** in most programmes. So the signature — strong respond, weak anticipate, weak learn, monitor running on individual memory — is what a compliance-shaped resilience programme *predicts*. An organisation that scores uniformly well on all four should be treated with more suspicion than one that scores like this.

### 13.3 Work-as-Imagined versus Work-as-Done for One Critical Process

**The process:** the daily break-reconciliation and exception-acceptance path — the sequence that decides, every evening, whether unmatched items are cleared, held, or accepted so that the cut-off can be met. Five material divergences were found across three ordinary days of observation plus testimony about the incident day.

| # | Work-as-imagined (the procedure) | Work-as-done (what was observed) | Verdict | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "Completeness is validated against expected record count and control totals **before processing begins**." | Validation is run **only when the file is late**. Files that arrive inside the window are processed on arrival and the count is compared afterwards, if at all. | **Drift** — the control's trigger condition silently became *lateness* rather than *arrival* | The control still exists, is still tested, and no longer answers the question it was written to answer. This is the exact mechanism of §8.4 |
| 2 | "Breaks above threshold require maker-checker approval." | The checker is a single senior operator who is also the only person able to release the queue. On the incident day the checker approved 41 acceptances in twelve minutes. | **Adaptation shading into drift** | The control *functioned* — a second person did look. But staffing was sized for mean break volume, not the quarter-end tail, so the four-eyes control was also the queue's only bottleneck. The deviation is not in the act; it is in the sizing that made shallow checking the only workable behaviour |
| 3 | "Escalate to the Payments Operations Lead if the cut-off is at risk." | Escalation went to the **technical duty manager**, because the operations lead's rota ends at 20:00 and the reprocessing question is technical. | **Adaptation** | The documented route could not have answered the question; the actual route could, and did. Legitimate — but undocumented, untaught, and dependent on one person knowing which door to knock on |
| 4 | "Residual breaks above tolerance are reported as an exception and accepted in writing." | Accepted in writing **the following morning**, retrospectively. | **Drift** | The written acceptance became a record of a decision already taken by default. The acceptance form requires a "conditionally accepted" field that the tool does not expose — so the truth could not be written at the time |
| 5 | "Findings are captured as a procedure addendum for publication." | Findings were captured as a message in the team's chat channel; no addendum was raised. | **Drift** | Addendum publication takes about six weeks; the team had already absorbed the learning internally and moved on. The adaptation worked; the organisational memory did not |

**The classification rule applied**, from §5.3: a divergence is an **adaptation** when the adjustment is compensating for a real condition, is available to others, and is reversible; it is **drift** when the adjustment has become the norm, the original margin can no longer be stated, and the condition that justified it is no longer being checked. Divergences 3 and the maker-checker *function* are adaptations. Divergences 1, 4 and 5 are drift, and divergence 2 is the dangerous middle case — an adaptation that is quietly cancelling a control's effect.

### 13.4 The Trade-Offs, and Why Each Was Locally Rational

**Not one of the following is sloppiness.** Each was a competent response to a real constraint, made by an experienced person, and each was *right on almost every day it was made* — which is what ETTO predicts (§6.2) and what makes these trade-offs invisible in an outcome-focused record.

| The trade-off observed | What was traded | The constraint actually faced | Why the choice was locally rational | Where the cost landed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Skipping validation on on-time files | Thoroughness → speed | A processing window that leaves no time budget for validation on a file that arrives on time | Missing the cut-off harms **every** payment in the file, with certainty; an incomplete on-time file is rare. Expected loss favours processing, every evening | On the next day's break queue — which is where the harm eventually appeared |
| Accepting breaks at shallow depth at quarter-end | Accuracy → throughput | A queue north of four hundred items and one qualified approver | Each individual break *was* a timing artefact; the adjustment was validated by experience on the previous 40 occasions. This is ETTO's reinforcement mechanism, not negligence | On the single break that was not an artefact |
| Routing escalation to the duty manager | Documentation → reachability | An on-call rota that ends at 20:00, and a question the documented owner could not answer | The escalation reached somebody who could act, before the cut-off. The alternative was an unanswered page | On the learning system — the documented route was never corrected, so the next shift learns it by accident too |
| Accepting the residual retrospectively | Evidence quality → continuity of service | An approval tool that does not expose the state the approver needs to record | Recording the truth at 22:40 would have blocked the cut-off. The tool made the honest record impossible at the moment it was needed | On the audit trail — a reader of the trail sees a compliant, timely, complete acceptance |
| Keeping the learning in the team channel | Organisational memory → team autonomy | A six-week publication cycle and a team that had already moved on | The learning *was* captured, by the people who needed it, immediately. Publishing late would have taught nobody anything | On resilience itself — the capacity now exists in four people and cannot be monitored, taught or replaced |

**The sentence to carry out of this table:** every one of these trade-offs is invisible to a review that begins with the outcome and asks whether the procedure was followed, and every one of them is visible to a review that begins with the work and asks what the operator was resolving (§6.3, §8.6). The corrective actions the two reviews produce are different in kind — "retrain and reissue" against "fix the intake, size the tail, expose the field".

### 13.5 What the Existing Regulatory Programme Could and Could Not See

The programme is competent at what it is built for. This table is a statement about the **instrument and its measurement frame** (§12.3), not about effort.

| Instrument the programme has | What it saw in this incident | What it is structurally unable to see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Important business services and **impact tolerances** | The service stayed **within tolerance** — the cut-off was met, the client impact was one misposting discovered late | That the tolerance was met by *consuming margin* — the validation control, the check depth, the honest record — none of which is an attribute of a service and therefore none of which appears anywhere in the tolerance framework |
| The **severe-but-plausible scenario library** | That the incident was not any scenario in the library, and that no book of scenarios would have contained it | Scenario enumeration happens in advance, by the firm, from a plausibility judgement. The "structurally valid, materially incomplete file" condition was **imaginable and unimagined** — and the programme has no instrument for the imaginable-but-omitted |
| **Testing and assurance** — control tests and exercise records | That the override path passed its control test, with a clean result and a signed record | The test script prescribed a low-volume business day. The test therefore validated the **work-as-imagined** sequence and told the programme nothing about quarter-end behaviour (§12.2) |
| **Incident and operational-risk reporting** | That **no control breached** — deviations were recorded, approvals obtained, timelines met | The adaptation/drift distinction does not exist in the taxonomy. "Deviation" is a single category and the programme cannot record the sentence *"the deviation is why the outcome was no worse"* — so every adaptation is filed as a defect or not filed at all |
| **Third-party and concentration registers** | That the exception-management tool is a material vendor dependency, with an exit plan | That the tool's **missing field** is why the written acceptance was retrospective. The register records a *design* dependency; the consequence of that dependency is a *behaviour*, and behaviours are not what the register tracks |
| **Governance, ownership and board attestation** | That the service has a named owner and an accountable executive | That the owner controls neither the **staffing model** nor the **cut-off window** — the two blunt-end factors that produced every trade-off in §13.4 (§8.5). The accountability is real; the control it implies is not |

**The one-sentence version:** the programme can demonstrate that the firm stayed within tolerance and cannot tell you what it spent to do so, because what it spent — margin, judgement, honest records — is not a service attribute and therefore has nowhere to sit in the framework.

### 13.6 Recommendations, With Illustrative Costs

**Every cost band below is illustrative and relative, not a quotation.** They are expressed as bands rather than figures because a figure would be invented, and an invented figure in a recommendation table is exactly the kind of plausible-looking content this guide exists to refuse.

| # | Recommendation | What it changes | Illustrative cost band (fictional) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Instrument the intake, not the outcome.** Compare record count and control totals to expected values automatically on arrival, and alarm on the difference before processing. | Removes divergence 1 permanently: validation stops being a discretionary step an operator can be timed out of | **Low** — a change to one intake job and one dashboard panel, inside an existing team |
| 2 | **Expose the state the approver needs.** Add the "conditionally accepted" field and make retrospective acceptance a recorded exception rather than the only possible record. | Removes divergence 4; converts an invisible margin-consuming practice into a monitored one | **Low** — a configuration change in an existing tool, plus a decision to accept the visibility |
| 3 | **Size the quarter-end tail, not the mean.** Staff the approval queue for the observed tail and list the single-qualified-approver dependency as a named capacity risk. | Removes divergence 2's cause rather than its symptom. This is a resourcing decision and it is the one recommendation most likely to be declined | **Medium** — a staffing decision inside an existing budget cycle |
| 4 | **Require every incident review to name the goal conflict and the blunt-end factor.** Adopt §8.6's template change: no review closes without stating which trade-off was being resolved and which resource, constraint or incentive shaped the options. | Makes §13.4's table a standing output instead of a one-off exercise; the finding becomes about design, not about a person | **Low** — a template change and one page of review guidance |
| 5 | **Classify every workaround as adaptation or drift, with a named compensating condition**, in the standing operational-resilience forum (§12.4 item 4). | Converts the workaround register from a defect list into design feedback, and gives drift a place to be seen before it is an incident | **Low-to-medium** — a governance decision, plus analyst time per cycle |
| 6 | **One FRAM analysis of the recurring class.** Model the functions and couplings of the "light file on a quarter-end" class — three occurrences, no broken component — and use the model to decide where monitoring belongs. | Produces an explanation that survives the question "but this happens most days"; replaces the third identical addendum | **High** — a facilitated workshop series with the people who do the work, and a facilitator who knows the method |

### 13.7 What the Exercise Did NOT Establish

Stated flatly, because this is where an assessment is most often over-read:

- **No causal proof.** The assessment explains the *shape* of the incident. It does not establish that the drift in §13.3 caused the misposting, and it did not exclude alternative accounts of the same evidence. It is an explanation, not a finding (§7.3).
- **No measured effect.** None of §13.6 has been implemented. There is no before-and-after, no control, and no basis for a claim that any recommendation would reduce incident frequency or severity. The discipline's own literature offers no controlled evaluation to borrow confidence from (§10.5, §11.3).
- **One process, one team, one site, four days.** The WAI/WAD reconstruction is a single critical process, observed over three ordinary days plus testimony. Nothing in §13.3 generalises to the rest of the estate.
- **The ratings are judgements, not measurements.** Two competent assessors would produce a different four-abilities profile from the same evidence. RAG/SPM is not validated as a psychometric instrument and produces no score comparable across organisations (§3.4, §11.4).
- **The profile is a structured hypothesis, not a score.** "Weak on anticipate" is a hypothesis about where capacity is thin, to be tested by changing something and observing — which is the intervention tradition's job, not this one's (§9.4).
- **No baseline.** There was no prior profile, so the ratings describe the present and say nothing about improvement or deterioration over time.
- **Nothing about typicality.** Cymbal Bank is fictional and this exercise was constructed to demonstrate a method. It is not evidence about banks.
- **It did not find a person at fault — and it did not prove nobody was.** It found that the breach-shaped findings and the capacity-shaped findings point at different, non-overlapping corrective actions, and that only one of the two sets was visible to the existing programme.

## 14. The Anti-Patterns

Six ways this discipline is adopted, funded and performed without anything changing. Each has a **guardrail**, and every guardrail is a testable action with a pass/fail you can evaluate against last quarter's artefacts — not a sentiment, and not a value.

| # | Anti-pattern | Symptom | Cause | Guardrail (a testable action) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **The procedure rewritten after an incident without asking why it was worked around** | Post-incident actions are wording changes: a step added, a sign-off inserted, a "must" strengthened. The same workaround reappears within a quarter | The review asked *what was not followed* rather than *what the old step was compensating for* (§5.4). Rewriting the procedure is the cheapest action that looks like a response | **No procedure may be reissued after an incident unless the review produces a written statement of the condition the previous version was failing to accommodate.** Test: does the change alter a *condition* (a tool, a rota, a time window, a staffing level) or only a *sentence*? If only a sentence, do not publish |
| 2 | **The safety campaign that measures zero adverse events as success** | The dashboard celebrates a falling incident count; near-miss and recovery reporting falls too; the organisation becomes quieter, not safer | Counting adverse outcomes is the only measurement available, so absence of outcomes is read as presence of safety (§4.2). Hollnagel's own warning: Safety-I efforts can "unintentionally degrade the resources and procedures needed to make things go right" ✅ | **Report at least one capacity indicator to the same forum as the incident count, with the same seriousness, and make it possible to fail.** Test: can you name a positive indicator that was reported as *worse* this quarter and treated as information? If everything trends well, the indicator is decorative |
| 3 | **The resilience programme that is a plan-testing exercise with a different name** | The programme charter speaks of the four abilities; the annual calendar is scenario exercises, control tests and remediation tracking. No investigation, resourcing or monitoring decision has changed | Resilience is adopted as a label for the existing testing obligation (§11.5), which is already funded and already auditable. Renaming is free; changing the work is not | **Define the programme by the decisions it changes, and name three.** Test: name a decision made in the last twelve months — an investigation commissioned, a resource committed, a signal put under monitoring — that this programme caused. If the answer is an exercise, it is a plan test |
| 4 | **The post-incident review that stops at the individual who acted** | Reviews close with "operator error", "did not follow procedure", "retrain", or "management oversight"; corrective actions assign training and reminders | "Human error" terminates the search, as the field's own primary material says ✅ (§8.1). The individual is the most legible causal candidate in the record, and the conditions are not | **Prohibit "human error" as a causal category in the review template, and require the goal conflict and the blunt-end factor (§8.6).** Test: read the last five review reports — does the operator's action appear as a *link* in the account, or as its conclusion? |
| 5 | **The concept adopted as vocabulary without changing what is measured** | Strategy decks, capability frameworks and job descriptions carry "safety differently", "resilience", "learning organisation"; the metrics appendix, the incident taxonomy and the risk register are unchanged | Vocabulary is inexpensive and signals modernity; measurement change is expensive and creates exposure (§11.4). The dilution literature exists because this is the field's normal fate — Dekker's founding-volume chapter is literally titled "chronicling the emergence of confused consensus" ✅ | **Name the measure, the taxonomy field or the resourcing line that changed.** Test: if the word appears in the strategy deck but not in the metrics appendix, the investigation taxonomy or a budget line, it is decoration. One of the three must change or the adoption is cancelled |
| 6 | **The organisation that celebrates adaptation while under-resourcing it** | The workaround register is celebrated; the people who work around the process are described as the reason the service holds; nobody's job is to make the adaptation unnecessary, and no budget line exists for it | Adaptation is visible as heroism and invisible as unpaid capacity. The four abilities are admired rhetorically while the funding goes to the tested and auditable response capability (§13.2) | **For every adaptation named in the register, record a budget line, a named owner and a monitoring signal.** Test: how many entries in last quarter's workaround register have all three? The gap is the measure of how much adaptive capacity is being supplied free |

**The pattern underneath all six**, stated once: the discipline is attractive precisely in the places where it is least costly to adopt — the vocabulary, the framing, the workshop — and least attractive where it would actually change something, which is in what an investigation asks, what a dashboard reports and who gets a budget line. Anti-patterns 3 and 5 are the same failure reached from different directions.

### 16.1 What Could Not Be Verified

Three kinds of gap are recorded here, and they are **not the same kind of thing**. Conflating them is how a limit of the instrument becomes a claim about the world.

**First — the paywalled works whose citations were established but which were not read.** Most of this literature is not open access, and the guide distinguishes throughout between **"full text read"** and **"citation established, full text not read"** (§2.5). The consequence must be stated in the direction it actually cuts: for every work marked *citation established*, the **existence and metadata** — title, author, year, publisher or journal, volume, pages — are verified against Crossref, a publisher catalogue or the author's own bibliography; any **characterisation of its argument** in this guide is second-hand. That applies to the ETTO monograph, the FRAM method chapters, *Safety-I and Safety-II* (2014), *Safety-II in Practice* (2017), the RAG epilogue itself, Dekker's *Drift into Failure*, Vaughan, Woods' 2015 paper, both 2015 literature reviews, Provan et al. 2020, Sujan et al. 2017 and Cooper's critique. ### 16.2 Glossary

Every decoder term from §1.4, plus the methods and the terms used in §13–§15. Alphabetical, one line each.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **AcciMap** | Rasmussen's layered model placing contributing factors at their correct level — government, regulator, company, management, sharp end — rather than at the operator (§7.4). |
| **Blunt end** | Where regulators, administrators, economic decision-makers and technology suppliers set the resources, constraints and competing demands the sharp end must satisfy (Woods and colleagues, §8.5). |

### 16.4 Closing Summary

Four conclusions, each of which this guide is prepared to defend on the evidence it has cited, and none of which is larger than that evidence.

1. **This discipline is a lens on why work succeeds, not a plan-test.** Its unit of analysis is the socio-technical system and the adaptive capacity of the people inside it (§1.3), and its characteristic output is an explanation and a set of questions — not a pass, a fail, a score or an attestation (§7.3, §9.4). That is not a limitation to be overcome; it is what makes it able to see the adaptations that a conformance frame cannot, and it is why an organisation can hold a clean assurance record and still be surprised by the incident class in §13.1.

2. **It complements Safety-I rather than replacing it — and the source says so, not the critics.** The field's own primary document calls the two "two complementary views of safety rather than two incompatible or conflicting approaches" and states that existing methods "can continue to be used", with the way forward lying in "combining the two ways of thinking" ✅ (§4.3). The replacement reading is unsupported by the field's own texts and is directly contested in the peer-reviewed literature (§11.2). A programme that withdraws adverse-outcome management in the name of Safety-II has removed a control, not adopted a frame.

3. **Its evidence base is largely qualitative, case-based and expert-judgement-based, and it should be described that way to any decision-maker.** There are conceptual papers, literature reviews, case studies and a growing qualitative empirical literature; there are scarce quantitative comparisons and — in this pass — **no controlled or quasi-experimental evaluation of the approach as an intervention** (§10.5, §11.3). Use these concepts as structured hypotheses and interrogation tools. Do not present them as evidence, and do not build a resilience KPI out of them: the originator's own 2026 argument is that measuring can reshape practice in ways that undermine the performance being measured (§11.4).

4. **It is the corrective to a compliance programme that can only see work-as-imagined.** The regulatory frame must measure what is measurable, which means it is systematically better at testing the response to anticipated disruption than at revealing the capacity that handles the rest — a property of measurement, not of effort (§12.3). What this discipline adds is not an alternative assurance regime but a way of finding out **what the work actually is**: what the runbook leaves out, which trade-offs are being made and why each is locally rational, where the margin is being consumed, and which of the gaps are adaptations that produce the good outcomes and which are drift. Everything else in this guide is machinery for that one question. The instruction is small and unfashionable: stop asking whether the plan was followed, and start asking what the people doing the work had to adjust in order to produce the good outcome — that is, learn to see work as done.
