## 1. The OB Overview

### 1.1 The Definition: What OB Is (verified)

**Organizational behavior (OB)** is the study of human behavior in organizational settings — the interface between human behavior and the organization, and the organization itself (verified — the standard field definition, Moorhead & Griffin, as quoted in the Wikipedia "Organizational behavior" article). The best-known textbook formulation, from Robbins & Judge's *Organizational Behavior* (the standard MBA text, now in its 18th+ edition), is tighter: **the study of what people do in an organization and how that behavior affects the organization's performance** (verified — standard textbook definition).

Three things follow from the definition that matter for a practitioner:

- **It is a science, not a folk art.** OB is built on systematic research — experiments, field studies, meta-analyses — not on anecdotes about what "obviously" motivates people (framed; the whole §8 exists because the obvious answer was wrong).
- **It is multi-level by construction.** People behave as individuals, as members of groups, and inside organizations — and behavior at each level changes the other two (verified — the three-level structure is the discipline's organizing principle; see §1.2).
- **It is descriptive first, prescriptive second.** OB's first job is to explain and predict behavior (why did the reorg stall?); its second is to inform management practice (what to do about it). The discipline deliberately separates "what is" from "what ought to be" — which is exactly why the same fact base can serve a manager, a consultant, and an architect (framed).

**A short genealogy (verified milestones):** the discipline's roots are usually traced through three movements — **scientific management** (Taylor, 1911 — the rationalist view of workers as efficient machine-parts), the **human relations movement** (born from the Hawthorne studies of the 1920s–30s, §8.1 — the discovery that attention and social relations change productivity), and **organizational psychology** (the postwar consolidation of individual/group/organization research into a named field; the term "organizational behavior" gained currency in the 1960s as the field's academic identity) (verified — the genealogy is standard; the exact decade of the field's naming is flagged, as sources vary between the late 1950s and the 1960s). Chester Barnard's *The Functions of the Executive* (1938) is the classic bridge: he recognized that individuals behave differently in organizational roles than they do outside them (verified — cited in the Wikipedia OB article).

### 1.2 The Three Levels: Individual, Group, Organization (verified)

OB's signature structure is its **three levels of analysis** — the individual, the group, and the organization (verified — Robbins & Judge organize the entire textbook around these three levels; the Wikipedia OB article gives the same tripartite structure as micro/meso/macro):

| Level | The question it answers | Canonical content |
|-------|-------------------------|-------------------|
| **Individual (micro)** | What does each person bring in? | Personality, perception, attitudes, motivation, learning, decision-making (§2, §3) |
| **Group (meso)** | What happens when people interact? | Teams, dynamics, conflict, power, politics, leadership (§4, §5) |
| **Organization (macro)** | What shapes the container itself? | Culture, structure, design, change, HR systems (§6, §7) |

The levels **nest**: individual attitudes feed group dynamics, group dynamics become organizational culture, and culture flows back down to shape individual perception (framed — this nesting is the whole point of the multi-level design; a change at any level ripples through the other two). The classic studies in §8 are each, in effect, a demonstration that a lower-level explanation (personality, "bad apples") is incomplete — the situation at a higher level does most of the work (framed — the situationist thesis, §8).

### 1.3 The Overview Table

| Level | Focus | Notes |
|-------|-------|-------|
| **Individual** | Personality (Big Five), perception, attitudes, motivation, individual decision-making | The micro level: what the person carries in — traits, biases, needs, goals (§2–§3) |
| **Group** | Teams, dynamics, conflict, power, politics, leadership | The meso level: what emerges when people coordinate — norms, roles, coalitions (§4–§5) |
| **Organization** | Culture, structure, change | The macro level: the container — shared assumptions and the systems that transmit them (§6–§7) |
| **The connector** | The classic studies | The empirical foundation: Hawthorne, Milgram, Asch, Stanford — why the levels matter (§8) |
| **The application** | Diagnosis + intervention | OB as a lens: read any organizational situation at all three levels before acting (§9) |

### 1.4 The Methods and the "Why" (verified taxonomy; framed application)

**How OB knows what it knows (verified — the standard methodological toolkit):** the discipline is empirical, and its evidence comes in a hierarchy the practitioner should recognize:

- **Lab experiments and field experiments** — controlled manipulation (Milgram, Asch, §8) or in-situ manipulation (Hawthorne) to establish causation; the gold standard, with realism as the trade-off.
- **Surveys and questionnaires** — the workhorse for attitudes, satisfaction, culture, and climate measurement (the Hofstede IBM surveys are the biggest famous example, §6.2); cheap and broad, but correlation, not causation, and vulnerable to response bias.
- **Case studies and qualitative work** — depth over breadth; hypothesis-generating (the Hawthorne interviewing program is the ancestor).
- **Meta-analysis** — the modern crown jewel: statistically combining many studies to estimate true effect sizes (the conscientiousness–performance and transformational-leadership findings are meta-analytic, §2.1, §5.3).

**The two cautions every practitioner should internalize (framed):** (1) **correlation is not causation** — job satisfaction correlates with performance, but the causal direction has been debated for decades (the "happy-productive worker" question is genuinely unresolved); (2) **effect sizes are usually moderate** — the most robust OB findings explain meaningful but partial variance, which is why no single theory in §3 is a complete answer and why the multi-level lens of §9 is non-negotiable.

**Why a solution architect should study OB (framed):** an architect's day is spent inside the human system — stakeholder alignment, RFP politics, team formation, cross-office coordination, change adoption. The applied people-skills cluster of this repo ([communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md), [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md), [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md)) is the craft; this guide is the science under the craft — the vocabulary and the evidence base that turn intuitions ("this reorg feels wrong") into diagnoses ("instrumentality is broken and the culture gap is unaddressed").

## 2. The Individual Level

The micro level is the unit of analysis a manager meets first: every reorg, every hiring decision, every performance conversation begins with a specific human being. Three building blocks dominate the canon: **personality** (what they are), **perception** (what they see), and **attitudes** (what they feel and intend).

### 2.1 The Personality: The Big Five (verified; origin flagged)

**Personality** is the relatively stable pattern of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that distinguishes one person from another (verified — standard definition). OB's consensus model is the **Big Five** (five-factor model, FFM), the mnemonic **OCEAN** (verified — the five factors are canonical and were re-verified for this guide):

| Factor | The trait | Predicts (in OB research) |
|--------|-----------|---------------------------|
| **O**penness to experience | Creativity, curiosity, willingness to entertain new ideas | Adaptability to change; innovation |
| **C**onscientiousness | Self-control, diligence, attention to detail, organization | **The strongest predictor of job performance across occupations** (verified — the meta-analytic finding is one of the most replicated in OB) |
| **E**xtraversion | Energy, sociability, assertiveness, positive emotion | Leadership emergence; sales performance; comfort in teams |
| **A**greeableness | Kindness, cooperation, trust, helpfulness | Teamwork quality; customer-service performance; lower conflict |
| **N**euroticism | Anxiety, irritability, emotional instability | Burnout risk; counterproductive work behavior; lower job satisfaction |

**The origin (flagged):** the Big Five has no single birth year. The **lexical hypothesis** program starts with Allport & Odbert's 1936 dictionary study (~4,500 trait terms); the five-factor structure emerges repeatedly in factor analyses from the 1940s–60s (Fiske 1949; Tupes & Christal 1961; Norman 1963); the name "Big Five" is coined by **Lewis Goldberg in 1981**; and the modern instruments (Costa & McCrae's NEO inventories) crystallize in the 1980s (verified — the multi-step history is the standard account; flag the specific years as attributed, not re-verified paper-by-paper for this edition). The Big Five's advantage over MBTI/DISC is evidence: it is dimensional, cross-culturally replicated, and predicts external outcomes — see [personality_assessments_guide.md](personality_assessments_guide.md) §4 for the evidence-level treatment.

**The practitioner's caution (framed):** traits predict *tendencies across situations*, not behavior in any one situation. Conscientiousness predicts performance on average; it does not tell you how a specific developer will react to a specific deadline. Personality is input to judgment, not a sorting machine — and the same person reads differently under stress (the "dark side" of strengths — see [personality_assessments_guide.md](personality_assessments_guide.md) §5 on Hogan).

### 2.2 The Perception (verified)

**Perception** is the process by which people select, organize, and interpret sensory information to give meaning to their environment (verified — standard definition). OB cares because **behavior is based on perception, not on objective reality** — two people looking at the same incident report will genuinely see different events (framed; this is the discipline's fundamental stance).

The canonical machinery:

- **Perceptual selectivity:** people cannot process everything, so they filter — by salience, recency, novelty, and their own needs and expectations. Selective perception means a banker scanning a risk dashboard sees the numbers their book is exposed to; a developer scanning the same dashboard sees the data-quality issues (framed — mechanism verified, application framed).
- **Perceptual errors (verified — the standard catalog):**
  - **Stereotyping** — judging someone by the group they belong to (the most consequential error in hiring and promotion).
  - **Halo effect** — letting one positive trait (good presentation skills) color the whole assessment (Thorndike's 1920 original finding; the phenomenon is verified).
  - **Horns effect** — the negative mirror of halo.
  - **Selective perception / confirmation** — noticing what fits existing beliefs.
  - **Projection** — attributing one's own traits to others.
  - **Contrast effect** — evaluating someone relative to who preceded them (the "following a great candidate" interview bias).
- **Attribution theory (verified — Kelley's 1967 covariation model is the standard reference):** we explain others' behavior by **internal** causes (ability, effort) or **external** causes (luck, task difficulty), using consistency, distinctiveness, and consensus information. Two systematic errors dominate:
  - **Fundamental attribution error** — we over-attribute *others'* failures to character and *their* successes to luck; and reverse it for ourselves.
  - **Self-serving bias** — we claim credit for successes and blame the situation for failures.

**Why it matters at work (framed):** performance reviews, 360 feedback, promotion decisions, and reorg rumor-mills are all perception machines. The 360-degree instrument exists precisely because single-rater perception is systematically biased — see [360_management_guide.md](360_management_guide.md).

### 2.3 The Attitudes (verified)

**Attitudes** are evaluative statements — favorable or unfavorable — about objects, people, or events (verified — standard definition). The **ABC model** decomposes any attitude into three components: **Affect** (feelings), **Behavior** (intention to act), and **Cognition** (beliefs) (verified — the tri-component model is standard). OB's central attitude finding is that **the link between attitudes and behavior is real but loose and situation-dependent** (verified — the classic attitude-behavior research: measured attitudes predict behavior moderately, not perfectly).

The work-relevant attitude set:

| Attitude | What it is | OB research status |
|----------|-----------|--------------------|
| **Job satisfaction** | How much you like your job | Correlates with performance modestly; with turnover and absenteeism more strongly (verified — the satisfaction–performance link is positive but small-to-moderate; the turnover link is robust) |
| **Organizational commitment** | Identification with and loyalty to the organization (affective, continuance, normative) | Predicts retention better than satisfaction (verified — standard account) |
| **Job involvement / engagement** | Psychological identification with and absorption in the work | Engagement is the contemporary umbrella construct — the applied layer lives in [grow_team_guide.md](grow_team_guide.md) and [the_managers_path_research.md](the_managers_path_research.md) |
| **Perceived organizational support** | Belief that the organization values your contribution and cares about your wellbeing | Predicts commitment, satisfaction, and citizenship (verified — standard account) |
| **Organizational citizenship behavior (OCB)** | Discretionary behavior that helps the organization beyond the job description | The behavioral output of positive attitudes (verified — standard account) |
| **Counterproductive work behavior (CWB)** | Voluntary behavior that harms the organization (theft, sabotage, withdrawal) | The dark mirror of OCB (verified — standard account) |

**Cognitive dissonance (verified — Festinger, 1957):** when attitudes and behavior conflict, people feel psychological discomfort and change *something* — usually the easier target, which is the attitude, not the behavior. This is why a person who is publicly committed to a reorg ("I led the town hall") will privately rationalize its flaws. Managers who want commitment should get *public* behavior first — behavior pulls attitudes along, faster than attitudes pull behavior (framed — mechanism verified, application framed).

### 2.4 The Individual Table

| Dimension | What it is | Why it matters | Canonical reference |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|---------------------|
| **Personality (Big Five)** | Stable trait profile: OCEAN | Conscientiousness predicts performance; traits are tendencies, not verdicts | Allport–Odbert 1936 → Goldberg 1981 → Costa & McCrae 1980s (flagged origin) |
| **Perception** | Selecting/interpreting stimuli; errors and attributions | Behavior follows perceived reality, not objective reality | Kelley 1967 (attribution); Thorndike 1920 (halo) |
| **Attitudes** | Affect + behavior + cognition about the job | Satisfaction/commitment drive retention; behavior changes attitudes faster than the reverse | Festinger 1957 (dissonance); ABC model |
| **Values** | Trans-situational beliefs about desirable end-states | The deep filter underneath attitudes; person-organization fit predicts retention | Rokeach 1973 (values inventory — standard, not re-verified) |

## 3. The Motivation

Motivation is the force that energizes, directs, and sustains behavior toward a goal (verified — the standard definition). The canon is a stack of six theories, each answering a different question: **what** people need (Maslow, McClelland), **what** makes work satisfying (Herzberg), **how** people calculate effort (Vroom, Adams), and **how** goals channel effort (Locke). They are complements, not competitors (framed).

### 3.1 The Maslow Hierarchy of Needs (verified — 1943)

**Abraham Maslow**, *"A Theory of Human Motivation,"* *Psychological Review* 50(4), July 1943, pp. 370–396 (verified — the primary source was re-confirmed this edition; the paper is in the public-domain classics collection at York University).

The **hierarchy of needs**: human motivation is arranged in five need levels, and the lower levels dominate until satisfied:

1. **Physiological** — food, water, shelter, sleep.
2. **Safety** — security, stability, freedom from threat.
3. **Love/belonging** — friendship, family, being accepted.
4. **Esteem** — achievement, recognition, respect (internal and external).
5. **Self-actualization** — becoming everything one is capable of becoming.

**The core claim:** a satisfied need ceases to motivate; people progress up the hierarchy as lower needs are gratified (verified — the theory as Maslow published it; note the caricature version that implies a rigid staircase is a later simplification — Maslow himself allowed needs to be only partially satisfied at multiple levels simultaneously, flagged).

**The critique (verified — the theory's empirical record is weak; this is consensus in the OB literature):** the strict ordering is difficult to test and has not held up in research; people do pursue esteem while hungry (artists, revolutionaries). Its enduring value is *heuristic*: it is the best-known reminder that **different people at different moments need different things** — and that the same incentive (a bonus, a title, a parking space) lands differently depending on the recipient's level (framed).

**Banking read (framed):** a bonus is esteem-and-safety currency; for a junior analyst in Singapore, it may also be physiological-and-safety currency (rent). The same bonus instrument is therefore motivating and *de*-motivating in the same room. Maslow explains why money is never a universal lever.

### 3.2 The Herzberg Two-Factor Theory (verified — 1959)

**Frederick Herzberg**, with Bernard Mausner and Barbara Bloch Snyderman, *The Motivation to Work* (1959) — based on interviews with ~200 engineers and accountants in Pittsburgh (verified — the 1959 Wiley book and the study design were re-confirmed; the famous HBR article "One More Time: How Do You Motivate Employees?" followed in 1968).

**The two-factor (motivation–hygiene) claim:** satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposites on one scale — they are produced by *different* sets of factors:

| Factor type | What it contains | Effect |
|-------------|------------------|--------|
| **Motivators** (intrinsic) | Achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth | Produce **satisfaction** and motivation when present; their absence produces "not satisfied," not dissatisfaction |
| **Hygiene factors** (extrinsic) | Company policy, supervision, working conditions, salary, status, job security, relationships | Produce **dissatisfaction** when inadequate; fixing them removes pain but does not motivate |

**The memorable line:** *the opposite of satisfaction is not dissatisfaction — it is no satisfaction; the opposite of dissatisfaction is not satisfaction — it is no dissatisfaction* (framed — this is the theory's own framing).

**The critique (verified — the theory is one of the most-criticized in OB):** the methodology (critical-incident interviews) invites the observed pattern — people credit themselves for successes and blame the environment for failures; later research finds satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not cleanly two-factor. Its durable contribution is the **intrinsic/extrinsic distinction**, which survives in engagement research and in every modern "motivation is not the same as compensation" argument (framed).

**Banking read (framed):** pay-for-performance in a bank is a hygiene factor that has become the whole conversation. Herzberg predicts the bonus conversation can only ever *stop* dissatisfaction, never *start* motivation — the work itself (a desk, a mandate, a book to run) is what motivates.

### 3.3 The McClelland Acquired Needs (verified — 1961)

**David C. McClelland**, *The Achieving Society* (1961, Harvard professor; the three-needs framework; extended in *Human Motivation*, 1987) (verified — the 1961 book attribution was re-confirmed).

**The three learned needs:**

- **Need for achievement (n-Ach)** — the drive to excel against standards. High-n-Ach people prefer moderately difficult, winnable goals, want rapid feedback, and are *not* necessarily good team leaders (they can be individualists).
- **Need for power (n-Pow)** — the drive to influence others. *Personal* power (domination) versus *institutional/social* power (mobilizing others for organizational goals) — the latter predicts managerial success.
- **Need for affiliation (n-Aff)** — the drive for close, friendly relationships. High n-Aff people avoid conflict and can be poor at difficult personnel decisions.

**The claim:** needs are **acquired** through life experience, not innate — and therefore measurable (the Thematic Apperception Test tradition) and, in principle, trainable (framed — the training claim is the controversial part; the needs taxonomy itself is standard).

**Banking read (framed):** sales and trading desks select for n-Ach; the hierarchy selects for institutional n-Pow; support functions skew n-Aff. The same reorg message lands as *opportunity* (high n-Ach), *threat to status* (high n-Pow), or *loss of trusted relationships* (high n-Aff) — one message, three reactions.

### 3.4 The Vroom Expectancy Theory (verified — 1964)

**Victor H. Vroom**, *Work and Motivation* (Wiley, 1964) (verified — the book and the E×I×V formulation were re-confirmed).

**Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence** (M = E × I × V):

- **Expectancy (E)** — belief that *effort → performance*: "if I try, can I do it?" (killed by impossible targets, missing skills, or a belief that effort doesn't matter).
- **Instrumentality (I)** — belief that *performance → outcome*: "if I perform, will I get the reward?" (killed by opaque bonus formulas and "performance doesn't matter here" cultures).
- **Valence (V)** — the value of the outcome *to me*: "do I even want the reward?" (killed by misreading what people actually value).

**The multiplicative claim (framed — the theory's key design feature):** any factor at zero zeroes the whole product. A huge bonus (high V) fails to motivate if people believe performance is not actually measured (low I) — which is precisely the pathology of discretionary bonus pools in banks.

**The critique (verified — the theory's assumptions are demanding):** people rarely calculate E, I, V explicitly; the model predicts *choice* better than *effort*; and it says nothing about what makes outcomes valent. But it remains the best diagnostic for "why is nobody motivated?" — ask the three questions in order (framed).

### 3.5 The Locke Goal-Setting Theory (verified — 1968)

**Edwin A. Locke**, *"Toward a Theory of Task Motivation and Incentives,"* *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance* 3(2), 1968, pp. 157–189 (verified — the primary source was re-confirmed); consolidated with Gary Latham in *A Theory of Goal Setting & Task Performance* (1990).

**The claim (verified — one of the most replicated findings in the applied psychology literature):** specific and difficult goals, accepted by the person and paired with feedback, produce higher performance than vague "do your best" goals. The mechanism: goals direct attention, mobilize effort, prolong persistence, and drive strategy development.

**The moderators (verified — standard account):** goal commitment, feedback, task complexity (hard goals backfire on novel/complex tasks where learning is the goal), and self-efficacy.

**Banking read (framed):** this is the theory under every annual scorecard and every "stretch target." It explains both halves of the phenomenon: why a specific, hard but reachable target concentrates a team — and why an unreachable target, or one set with no feedback loop, silently produces gaming, sandbagging, or disengagement.

### 3.6 The Adams Equity Theory (verified — 1963)

**J. Stacy Adams**, *"Toward an Understanding of Inequity,"* *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 67(5), 1963, pp. 422–436 (verified — the primary source was re-confirmed, including the General Electric/Crotonville affiliation).

**The claim:** people compare their **outcome/input ratio** to a **referent other** (a colleague, a market benchmark, their own past). Perceived **inequity** — my ratio is worse (or even better) than the reference — produces tension, and people restore equity by changing inputs (work less), changing outcomes (ask for more), distorting perceptions ("their job is harder"), changing the referent, or leaving.

**The key insight (framed):** fairness is **comparative and perceptual**, not absolute. The market-priced hire earning more than the tenured incumbent — the classic bank integration wound — is not a compensation problem; it is an equity-theory problem, and it will be "solved" by the incumbent in ways the comp model did not predict (withdrawal, politics, exit).

### 3.7 The Motivation Table

| Theory | Origin | Idea |
|--------|--------|------|
| **Maslow — hierarchy of needs** | 1943, *Psychological Review* | Five need levels; satisfied needs stop motivating; people rise as lower needs are met (heuristic value > empirical support) |
| **Herzberg — two-factor** | 1959, *The Motivation to Work* | Motivators (intrinsic) cause satisfaction; hygiene factors (extrinsic) only prevent dissatisfaction |
| **McClelland — acquired needs** | 1961, *The Achieving Society* | Learned needs for achievement, power, affiliation drive behavior; measurable, situationally relevant |
| **Vroom — expectancy** | 1964, *Work and Motivation* | M = E × I × V; motivation dies if any of effort→performance, performance→reward, or reward value is zero |
| **Locke — goal-setting** | 1968, *Org. Behavior & Human Performance* | Specific, difficult, accepted goals + feedback beat "do your best"; the most replicated performance finding |
| **Adams — equity** | 1963, *J. Abnormal & Social Psychology* | People compare outcome/input ratios to referents; perceived inequity is resolved behaviorally, often destructively |

**The stack in one sentence (framed):** give people what they actually need (Maslow), make the work itself motivating (Herzberg), match the incentive to the need profile (McClelland), make the effort→performance→reward chain believable (Vroom), aim the effort with specific challenging goals (Locke), and make the whole distribution visibly fair (Adams). Any single theory is a partial lens; the six together are a diagnostic kit.

## 4. The Group Level

The meso level is where individual behavior becomes organizational behavior: groups have properties — norms, roles, cohesion — that no member possesses alone (framed — the classic "the whole is different from the sum" stance of the field).

### 4.1 The Teams (verified; Tuckman standard)

A **team** is a group whose members have complementary skills, shared purpose and goals, mutual accountability, and interdependent work (verified — the standard team definition in the OB literature). Teams differ from mere groups in one word: **interdependence** (framed — this is the standard distinction).

**The canonical lifecycle (verified — Tuckman's model is the standard account; the 1965 paper is *"Developmental Sequence in Small Groups,"* *Psychological Bulletin*; the fifth stage was added with Jensen in 1977 — flagged: not re-verified paper-by-paper this edition):**

| Stage | What happens | The manager's job |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Forming** | Orientation, polite uncertainty, dependency on the leader | Provide structure, purpose, introductions |
| **Storming** | Conflict over roles, methods, leadership; the hardest stage | Normalize conflict; don't suppress it — channel it |
| **Norming** | Cohesion, shared norms, mutual trust | Reinforce norms; step back |
| **Performing** | High interdependence, results focus | Clear obstacles; protect the team |
| **Adjourning** (Tuckman & Jensen 1977) | Wrapping up, disengagement, sometimes grief | Debrief, recognize, re-deploy |

**Why teams fail (framed):** teams are over-declared and under-built — a "team" with no interdependence is a committee; storming skipped is storming deferred. The applied operating-model layer — squads, missions, working agreements — lives in [team_lead_methodologies_guide.md](team_lead_methodologies_guide.md).

### 4.2 The Dynamics (verified)

**Group dynamics** are the forces that operate within groups: norms, roles, status, cohesion, and the emergent behaviors they produce (verified — standard definition).

- **Norms** — the unwritten acceptable-behavior standards members share. Norms are enforced socially (approval, exclusion) and are *remarkably* sticky: the bank wiring room at Hawthorne (§8.1) ran informal output quotas that overrode the company's piece-rate incentives — a norm that survived the incentive system (verified — the bank wiring observation room finding is standard).
- **Roles** — expected behavior patterns attached to positions; **role conflict** (two incompatible expectations) and **role ambiguity** (unclear expectations) are documented stress and satisfaction killers (verified — standard account).
- **Status** — the socially defined rank within the group. High-status members get more latitude and are evaluated less harshly (the status halo); status hierarchies form fast and resist change — one reason reorgs land hard (framed).
- **Cohesion** — the glue of liking and shared identity. Cohesion improves performance *when the group's norms align with organizational goals* — and amplifies sabotage when they don't (verified — the norms-cohesion interaction is the standard account).
- **Groupthink (verified — Janis, 1972; the concept is standard, the case evidence is debated):** cohesive groups under pressure with a directive leader can converge prematurely — suppressing dissent, stereotyping outsiders, and rationalizing warnings. The antidotes: devil's advocate, outside experts, sub-group deliberation, and a leader who withholds their own view.
- **Social loafing (verified — the Ringelmann effect, 1913; modern replication by Latané et al.):** people exert less effort in groups than alone. The antidote is accountability: identifiable individual contribution. This is the research justification for pair work, explicit task owners, and code-review trails (framed).

### 4.3 The Conflict (verified)

**Conflict** is a process in which one party perceives that another has negatively affected, or will negatively affect, something the first party cares about (verified — the standard definition). The OB canon's core claim: **conflict is not inherently bad** (verified — the functional/dysfunctional distinction is standard).

- **Functional conflict** — task conflict over how to do the work: improves decision quality, surfaces assumptions, prevents groupthink.
- **Dysfunctional conflict** — relationship conflict over who people are: corrodes trust, hijacks attention, destroys performance.
- **The empirical nuance (framed):** moderate task conflict helps on complex, non-routine work; any relationship conflict hurts almost everywhere; and task conflict frequently *degrades* into relationship conflict under pressure — the line is easier to draw in theory than in a heated room.

**The resolution toolkit (verified — the Thomas–Kilmann five modes, TKI, 1974; the five-mode taxonomy is standard):**

| Mode | Assertiveness / cooperativeness | Use when |
|------|--------------------------------|---------|
| **Competing** | High / low | Quick, unpopular decisions; protecting vital interests |
| **Collaborating** | High / high | Both sets of interests matter; integration possible |
| **Compromising** | Mid / mid | Time-pressed, equal-power, temporarily acceptable |
| **Avoiding** | Low / low | Trivial issues; cooling-off; no chance to win |
| **Accommodating** | Low / high | You're wrong; preserving harmony; building credit |

The applied negotiation layer — how these modes play out in real stakeholder management — lives in [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) and [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md).

### 4.4 The Power (verified — French & Raven)

**Power** is the capacity of A to influence B's behavior despite B's resistance (verified — the standard definition). The canonical taxonomy is **French & Raven's five bases** (1959; the six-base version adds informational power) (verified — the attribution is standard; flagged: not re-verified against the original 1959 paper this edition):

| Base | Source | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| **Coercive** | Fear of punishment | Effective short-term; breeds resistance and surveillance costs |
| **Reward** | Ability to give valued outcomes | The bonus, the promotion, the good project; strongest when contingent and credible |
| **Legitimate** | Formal position | The org chart's power; the *most* used, the *least* renewable |
| **Expert** | Knowledge and skill | The architect's and the trader's natural base; hard to fake, decays when stale |
| **Referent** | Identification with and admiration of the person | The strongest base; charisma, trust, networks — [managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md](managing_up_down_sideways_guide.md) territory |

**The research findings that matter (verified — standard account):** people respond best to expert and referent power, worst to coercive; the bases are substitutable (a credible expert can lead without legitimate power — the staff engineer effect); and power corrupts attention — the powerful listen less, take more risks, and over-weight their own perspective (the power-paradox literature, standard).

### 4.5 The Politics (verified)

**Organizational politics** are activities not formally sanctioned by the organization that individuals use to influence the allocation of scarce resources — "the use of power to affect decision making" (verified — the standard definition). The OB stance is realist: **politics is inevitable wherever resources are scarce and decisions are discretionary** (framed — the canonical claim).

The catalog:

- **Political behavior** — coalition building, networking, impression management, information control, agenda setting, image building. Impressions *are* the currency of promotion: perception (§2.2) plus politics equals career outcomes (framed).
- **Impression management** — self-presentation tactics (self-promotion, ingratiation, exemplification, supplication) — verified as a standard construct (Jones & Pittman 1982, standard attribution, flagged: not re-verified).
- **Machiavellianism** — the personality dimension of cynical manipulation; high-Mach people flourish in loosely structured, face-to-face, high-discretion environments and wilt under rules and accountability (verified — standard account; the personality construct appears alongside the Big Five in the OB canon).
- **The honest framing (framed):** politics is not "bad people doing bad things" — it is the informal influence system that every formal structure leaks. The naive manager who refuses to play politics simply cedes the field to people whose politics are worse aligned with the organization's goals. The professional move is not to be apolitical but to be *constructively* political — build coalitions for the outcome, not for the turf (§9).

## 5. The Leadership

Leadership is the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals (verified — the standard definition). The canon's arc: **trait** theories (who is born to lead) → **behavioral** theories (what leaders do) → **contingency** theories (what works depends on the situation) → modern **relational** theories (transformational, servant, LMX). Each generation answered the previous one's failure (framed).

### 5.1 The Trait Theories (verified)

The earliest systematic research asked: what distinguishes leaders from followers? The trait program's own verdict (verified — Stogdill's 1948 review and the 1970s synthesis are the standard account): **no single trait reliably separates leaders from non-leaders across situations**, but the *leader emergence* evidence supports a short list:

- **Drive and energy**, **self-confidence**, **emotional stability**, **integrity/honesty**, **cognitive ability**, **sociability** (extraversion is the single most consistent correlate — verified, standard account).
- **The modern revival (framed):** trait thinking returns in two respectable forms — (1) *leader emergence* vs *leader effectiveness* are different outcomes (traits predict emergence better than effectiveness), and (2) the **dark triad** (narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy) research shows toxic traits reliably predict derailment — the trait lens inverted (verified — the dark-triad literature is standard).

**The practical reading (framed):** traits are *entry conditions*, not destiny. The research kills the "born leader" myth in its pure form — but it does not kill selection: hiring and promoting for energy, integrity, and emotional stability is evidence-based, and ignoring the dark triad is a known derailment pattern (see [360_management_guide.md](360_management_guide.md) on derailment).

### 5.2 The Behavioral Theories (verified)

If traits fail, maybe leadership is a *learnable behavior pattern*. The two great research programs (verified — standard account; both ran in the 1940s–50s):

- **Ohio State studies** — two independent behavioral dimensions: **initiating structure** (task focus: defining roles, schedules, standards) and **consideration** (relationship focus: trust, respect, warmth). Their finding: *both* matter; the ideal is high-high, but the effects are situation-dependent (consideration predicts satisfaction; structure predicts performance, unevenly).
- **Michigan studies** — the mirror-image finding: **production-oriented** vs **employee-oriented** supervisors; employee orientation predicted higher group productivity and satisfaction in most studies.
- **Blake & Mouton's Managerial Grid** (1964, verified standard attribution): plots concern for people against concern for production in a 9×9 grid; the "9,9 team leader" is the normative ideal — a framework that survives mainly as a teaching device and as the ancestor of the leadership *style* vocabulary (framed).

**The verdict (framed):** behavior is more learnable than traits — which is why every leadership development program since the 1950s teaches behavior. But no behavioral style works in every situation, which is exactly the gap the contingency theories filled.

### 5.3 The Contingency Theories (verified — Fiedler)

The contingency turn: *the best leadership style depends on the situation*. The flagship is **Fred Fiedler's contingency model** (verified — *A Theory of Leadership Effectiveness*, McGraw-Hill, 1967; the LPC scale and the three situational variables were re-confirmed via the Wikipedia treatment this edition).

**The model:**

- **Leadership style is measured by the Least Preferred Coworker (LPC) scale:** rate the person you least enjoyed working with. High-LPC leaders (rate even the worst coworker somewhat positively) are **relationship-motivated**; low-LPC leaders are **task-motivated**.
- **Style is treated as relatively fixed** — Fiedler's radical conclusion: do not try to change the leader, **engineer the situation** to match the leader.
- **Situational favorability** is determined by three variables, in order of weight:
  1. **Leader–member relations** — trust and loyalty between leader and group (the most important).
  2. **Task structure** — how clearly defined the task is.
  3. **Position power** — the leader's formal authority to reward and punish.

**The finding:** task-motivated (low-LPC) leaders perform best in *very favorable* and *very unfavorable* situations; relationship-motivated (high-LPC) leaders perform best in *moderately favorable* situations. The curve is famously non-monotonic — the counterintuitive result that made the theory famous (framed — the empirical support is mixed by modern standards, but the *logic* is the discipline's cornerstone).

**The other contingency schools (verified — standard attributions):**

- **Hersey–Blanchard situational leadership** — match directive/supportive behavior to the follower's readiness on the task; the full treatment lives in [situational_leadership_slii_guide.md](situational_leadership_slii_guide.md) (the applied deep-dive of this repo's people-skills cluster).
- **House's path–goal theory** (1971) — the leader's job is to *clear the path*: clarify goals, remove obstacles, and match style (directive, supportive, participative, achievement-oriented) to follower and task characteristics. Directly compatible with expectancy theory (§3.4) — motivation theory wearing a leadership costume (framed).
- **LMX (leader–member exchange)** — leaders develop differentiated relationships with in-group vs out-group members; in-group members get trust and latitude and reciprocate with loyalty and performance. The uncomfortable implication: favoritism is not a bug, it is the mechanism — the job is to make the in-group boundary porous and fair (framed).
- **Transformational vs transactional** (Burns 1978; Bass 1985 — verified standard attribution): transactional leadership exchanges rewards for effort (the compliance economy); transformational leadership elevates followers' goals and identity via idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration — the most-researched modern paradigm, with meta-analytic support for performance effects (verified — standard account).

### 5.4 The Leadership Table

| Theory family | Core idea | Canonical reference | Status |
|---------------|-----------|---------------------|--------|
| **Trait** | Leaders differ from followers on stable characteristics (energy, confidence, integrity, sociability) | Stogdill 1948 (review); dark triad revival | Predicts emergence better than effectiveness (verified) |
| **Behavioral** | Leadership is learnable behavior: initiating structure + consideration; people vs production | Ohio State; Michigan; Blake–Mouton 1964 | Style vocabulary survives; no universal best style (verified) |
| **Contingency** | Effectiveness depends on matching style to situation; engineer the situation, not the person | **Fiedler 1967** (LPC); Hersey–Blanchard; House 1971; LMX | The field's cornerstone logic (verified; empirical support mixed) |

## 6. The Organization Level

The macro level studies the container: the culture, structure, and systems that persist long after any individual leaves. Two frameworks dominate — **Schein** for what culture *is*, and **Hofstede** for how it varies across nations (the cross-border dimension that matters daily in a global bank).

### 6.1 The Culture: Schein's Three Levels (verified)

**Edgar Schein**, *Organizational Culture and Leadership* (1985, first edition; the three-level model is in every edition) (verified — the three levels were re-confirmed against multiple secondary treatments this edition; the 1985 first-edition date is the standard attribution).

**Culture** is "a pattern of shared basic assumptions learned by a group as it solved its problems of external adaptation and internal integration... taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel" (verified — Schein's own definition, standard citation). **The three levels:**

| Level | What it is | Visible? | The bank example |
|-------|-----------|----------|------------------|
| **1. Artifacts** | Visible structures, processes, dress, language, rituals, architecture | Visible but hard to decode | The trading floor layout, the desk badges, the suit code, the town-hall format, "the open door" |
| **2. Espoused values** | Stated strategies, goals, philosophies — what the org *says* it believes | Stated (strategy docs, value posters, town halls) | "Client first," "One Bank," the code of conduct |
| **3. Basic underlying assumptions** | Taken-for-granted beliefs that drop out of conscious awareness — the actual operating system | Invisible; the source of the other two levels | "Revenue is the only thing that counts," "risk is a cost center," "the desk wins the argument" |

**The core teaching (framed):** the *gap between level 2 and level 3 is the culture gap* — what people say versus what they assume. Every new-hire's first six months is an exercise in decoding level 3 from levels 1–2; every failed culture-change program is a campaign that worked on espoused values and never touched underlying assumptions (see §7 for why that fails, and [strategic_management_guide.md](strategic_management_guide.md) for the strategy-side overlap).

**Why culture is hard to change (verified — Schein's mechanism is the standard account):** assumptions are *validated by history* — they persist because the group that held them survived and succeeded. A trading culture of individual accountability exists because it printed money; telling it the world has changed (regulation, client franchise) asks the group to abandon the belief that made it successful. That is why culture change is identity change, not poster change (framed).

### 6.2 The Hofstede Dimensions (verified — 1980)

**Geert Hofstede**, *Culture's Consequences: International Differences in Work-Related Values* (Sage, 1980) — derived from factor analysis of ~116,000 IBM employee-value surveys across ~40 countries, collected 1967–1973 (verified — the IBM survey period, the factor-analytic method, and the four original dimensions were re-confirmed via the Wikipedia treatment this edition).

**The original four dimensions (verified):**

| Dimension | The question it answers | Low vs high |
|-----------|------------------------|-------------|
| **Power distance** | How much inequality in power is accepted? | Low: flat, consultative (Nordics); High: hierarchical, deference to rank (many Asian and Latin cultures) |
| **Individualism–collectivism** | Are people self-directed or group-embedded? | Individualist: contracts, personal accountability (US, UK); Collectivist: loyalty, in-group harmony (much of Asia, Latin America) |
| **Masculinity–femininity** | Are the dominant values assertiveness/competition or care/consensus? | Masculine: earnings, recognition, challenge (Japan); Feminine: cooperation, security, quality of life (Nordics) |
| **Uncertainty avoidance** | How comfortable is the society with ambiguity? | Low: comfort with risk, fewer rules; High: need for formal rules, planning, job security (Greece, Portugal, Japan) |

**The later additions (verified):** **long-term orientation** (the fifth dimension, added after Hofstede's Hong Kong research with Michael Harris Bond — Confucian work dynamism) and **indulgence vs restraint** (the sixth, added in the 2010 edition).

**The critiques (flagged — verified as existing, not adjudicated):** the sample is one company (IBM) in one era (1967–73); dimensions are ecological (country-level), and applying them to individuals is the **ecological fallacy**; and the modern GLOBE program (House et al., 2004) both extends and disputes the framework. Use Hofstede for *national-culture comparison and expectation-setting*, not for predicting any individual (framed).

**Banking read (framed):** the same reorg memo lands differently by office — Singapore and Paris read hierarchy and uncertainty differently; the HK/SG desk and the Paris support function have different default assumptions about whose voice matters in a meeting. Hofstede explains why "one bank" communication is a myth and why local change agents are not optional (§9).

### 6.3 The Culture Table

| Framework | Origin | Core idea | Use it to |
|-----------|--------|-----------|-----------|
| **Schein three levels** | 1985, *Organizational Culture and Leadership* | Culture = artifacts → espoused values → underlying assumptions; level 3 is the operating system | Diagnose the gap between what an org says and what it assumes; design culture change that reaches level 3 |
| **Hofstede dimensions** | 1980, *Culture's Consequences* (IBM data 1967–73) | National culture varies on measurable dimensions: power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance (+ LTO, indulgence) | Set expectations across offices and regions; design global change with local variance |
| **Culture vs climate (flagged)** | Climate = the shared perception of "the way it is around here" (measurable, transient); culture = the deeper assumptions (slow, durable) | Standard distinction, attribution contested | Avoid buying "culture surveys" that actually measure climate and calling it culture |

## 7. The Change

Change is the applied test of the whole discipline: every OB topic — perception, motivation, power, culture — shows up in a reorg. The canon offers two complementary models: **Lewin**'s minimalist field-theory framing and **Kotter**'s full operating sequence. They are the same insight at two resolutions (framed).

### 7.1 The Lewin 3-Step Model (verified — 1947; the exact label flagged)

**Kurt Lewin**, *"Frontiers in Group Dynamics,"* *Human Relations* 1(1), June 1947, pp. 5–41 (the paper is the standard attribution; the "unfreeze–change–refreeze" phrasing as such is a posthumous popularization — flagged, see below).

**The three steps (the standard account):**

1. **Unfreezing** — melt the status quo: surface the forces that hold current behavior in place, create felt urgency and readiness. Without unfreezing, "change" is just noise layered on a frozen system.
2. **Changing (moving)** — shift behavior, processes, structures toward the new state; Lewin's field-theory insight: change is more effective when you *reduce restraining forces* (fear, ambiguity, loyalty to the old way) than when you merely push harder with driving forces.
3. **Refreezing** — stabilize the new state so it survives: reinforce with norms, rewards, and culture so the change becomes the new default.

**The flag (verified — the scholarly dispute is real):** Lewin died in 1947; the neat three-step label appears in the secondary literature (most influentially via Schein's later work on change and his 1999 defense of the Lewin attribution). Scholars debate whether Lewin himself ever framed it so simply — but the underlying field-theory logic (unfreeze → move → refreeze as a *psychological* sequence, not just a procedural one) is genuinely his (framed — the mechanism is the attribution; the label is the flag).

**The durable insight (framed):** every failure of change management is a failure of unfreezing or refreezing — starting to move a system that was never unfrozen, or moving it and letting it snap back because nothing re-froze it (norms, incentives, rituals unchanged).

### 7.2 The Kotter 8 Steps (verified — 1996)

**John P. Kotter**, *Leading Change* (Harvard Business School Press, 1996) — built on his study of ~100 companies' transformation efforts and his 1995 HBR article "Leading Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail" (verified — the 1996 book and the eight-step sequence were re-confirmed this edition; Kotter's firm now teaches an evolved "8 accelerators" version from *Accelerate*, 2014 — flagged as the later evolution).

**The 8 steps (verified — the canonical sequence):**

| # | Step | The failure it prevents (Kotter's own diagnoses) |
|---|------|--------------------------------------------------|
| 1 | **Create a sense of urgency** | Complacency — change starts because people *feel* the burning platform |
| 2 | **Build the guiding coalition** | A change led by one person alone; no powerful cross-functional group |
| 3 | **Form a strategic vision and initiatives** | Confusion — no picture of the future worth moving toward |
| 4 | **Communicate the vision** | Under-communication by a factor of ten; vision lives in one deck |
| 5 | **Empower others to act (remove barriers)** | Obstacles — processes, bosses, structures that block the new behavior |
| 6 | **Generate short-term wins** | Death by horizon — visible progress within 6–18 months |
| 7 | **Consolidate gains, produce more change** | Declaring victory too soon; momentum lost |
| 8 | **Anchor new approaches in the culture** | The change reverts — it was never wired into norms and assumptions (Schein's level 3, §6.1) |

**The meta-claim (framed — Kotter's own framing):** the eight steps are really two halves — steps 1–4 *create the climate for change* (the unfreezing), steps 5–6 *engage and enable the organization* (the moving), and steps 7–8 *implement and sustain* (the refreezing). Kotter's 8-step is Lewin's 3-step with the operating detail filled in — which is why they are taught together (verified — Kotter himself maps to the Lewin stages in his materials; the mapping is standard in the change-management literature).

**The consulting read (framed):** the McKinsey/consulting translation of this canon is the "change management workstream" — stakeholder mapping, communication plans, readiness assessments, quick wins — see [mckinsey_approach_guide.md](mckinsey_approach_guide.md) and [management_consulting_skills_guide.md](management_consulting_skills_guide.md) for the applied discipline.

### 7.3 The Change Table

| Model | Origin | The sequence | The failure mode it targets |
|-------|--------|--------------|-----------------------------|
| **Lewin 3-step** | 1947, *Human Relations* ("Frontiers in Group Dynamics") | Unfreeze → change → refreeze | Moving a frozen system; change that snaps back |
| **Kotter 8 steps** | 1996, *Leading Change* | Urgency → coalition → vision → communicate → empower → quick wins → consolidate → anchor | Every step maps to a documented failure: complacency, solo leadership, no vision, under-communication, barriers, no wins, premature victory, no anchoring |
| **The OB synthesis (framed)** | This guide | Perceive (unfreeze, §2.2) → Motivate (move, §3) → Culture (refreeze, §6.1) | The three levels in one motion — see §9 |

## 8. The Classic Studies

OB and its sibling social psychology were founded by a set of experiments that were surprising, famous, and — in three of the four — ethically indefensible by modern standards. They matter not as folklore but because each one overturned a comfortable assumption about why people behave as they do (framed).

### 8.1 The Hawthorne Studies (verified — 1924–1932)

**What happened (verified — the HBS Baker Library Hawthorne collection and the standard account were re-confirmed):** a series of experiments at the **Western Electric Hawthorne Works** in Chicago (1924–1932), investigating whether working conditions affect productivity:

| Phase | Years | What it was | Finding |
|-------|-------|-------------|---------|
| Illumination experiments | 1924–1927 | Varied factory lighting | Productivity rose in *both* the test and the control groups — lighting wasn't the driver |
| **Relay assembly test room** | **1927–1932** | Small group of women assembling relays; conditions varied (breaks, hours, pay) | Productivity rose *almost regardless of the change* — the famous "whatever we do, output goes up" result |
| Mass interviewing program | 1928–1930 | 21,000+ employee interviews | Workers' emotional and social concerns surfaced as central; interviewing itself improved morale |
| Bank wiring observation room | 1931–1932 | Observed a group of men wiring banks of switches | Informal group norms **overrode the piece-rate incentive**: the group set its own output ceiling and policed it |

**The interpretation (verified — the standard account):** productivity rose because the workers were being *studied* — given attention, consulted, made to feel special. **Elton Mayo** (Harvard) joined from 1928 onward and framed the result as the birth of the human relations movement: **social factors and attention matter at least as much as pay and conditions**.

**The honest flags (verified — the modern critique is standard):** (1) the "**Hawthorne effect**" as a named phenomenon was coined by **Henry A. Landsberger in 1958**, re-analyzing the studies — Mayo himself did not use the term; (2) the relay-room findings are confounded (two participants were replaced mid-study; pay rules changed; the group was small); (3) modern re-analysis and the 2009 *Nature* debate (Klein et al.; the replication attempts) show the effect is real but fragile — the term is now used more loosely than the evidence justifies (flagged). None of that changes the historical verdict: **the Hawthorne studies moved the field's center of gravity from the machine to the human** (framed).

### 8.2 The Milgram Obedience Studies (verified — 1961/1963)

**What happened (verified — the Wikipedia treatment and the primary literature were re-confirmed):** **Stanley Milgram**, Yale, 1961 — beginning in **August 1961**, three months after the Eichmann trial opened, to test whether ordinary people would obey an authority ordering them to harm another person. Participants were assigned the role of "teacher" and instructed to administer electric shocks — fake, but believed real — to a "learner" (a confederate) for errors, escalating to levels labeled lethal.

**The finding (verified):** in the baseline condition, **every participant went to 300 volts, and 65% went all the way to 450 volts** — despite audible distress (pounding on the wall from 300V, silence after 315V). Published as *"Behavioral Study of Obedience"* in the *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*, 1963; the full account is *Obedience to Authority* (1974).

**The OB reading (framed):** Milgram's subjects were not sadists — they were people in a situation (a legitimate authority, a gradual escalation, no exit ritual) that made refusal structurally hard. The experiment is the canonical demonstration of the **power of the situation over disposition** — the same lesson as Stanford (§8.4) and the same mechanism that makes well-intentioned people go along with a toxic team norm, a bullying desk head, or a compliance breach "because that's how it works here" (framed — the transfer to organizations is interpretation, not Milgram's own claim).

**The ethics flag (verified):** severe emotional distress was inflicted; modern IRB standards would not permit the design. It survives as the discipline's most-cited cautionary tale on both obedience and research ethics.

### 8.3 The Asch Conformity Studies (verified — 1951/1956)

**What happened (verified — the Wikipedia treatment and the standard account were re-confirmed):** **Solomon Asch**, Swarthmore — line-judgment experiments (1951 initial series; 1956 extension) in which a naive participant sat with confederates who unanimously gave an *obviously wrong* answer about which comparison line matched a standard line (18 trials; 12 critical trials with wrong majority answers).

**The finding (verified — the standard reported figures):** participants conformed to the unanimous wrong majority on **about one-third of the critical trials on average (~32–37%)**, and roughly **three-quarters conformed at least once**; a stubborn ~25% never conformed. With even **one dissenting partner**, conformity collapsed to near zero — the most practically important variation.

**The interpretation (verified — the standard distinction):** **normative influence** (wanting to fit in, fear of looking foolish — the dominant driver) vs **informational influence** (genuinely doubting one's own eyes). Asch's subjects privately knew the right answer; they publicly gave the group's answer.

**The OB reading (framed):** this is the experimental bedrock for why a room of senior bankers or architects can unanimously approve a flawed design or a flawed risk report — unanimity is not agreement, it is conformity pressure. The organizational antidotes are the groupthink ones (§4.2): dissent by design, anonymous input, and a leader who signals that the wrong answer is safe to give (see [communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md](communication_stakeholder_management_skills_guide.md) for the applied layer).

### 8.4 The Stanford Prison Experiment (verified — 1971, Zimbardo)

**What happened (verified — the Wikipedia treatment was re-confirmed; the study dates August 14–21, 1971):** **Philip Zimbardo**, Stanford University — a simulated prison in the psychology building's basement. **24 male college students** (recruited by newspaper ad, paid $15/day), screened for psychological stability, randomly assigned to **guard** or **prisoner** roles for a planned two-week study.

**What happened next (verified — the standard account):** within days, guards escalated to humiliation, psychological abuse, and arbitrary domination; prisoners showed acute stress and passivity; the study — famously — **was terminated after 6 days** (the Wikipedia treatment dates it August 14–21, 1971; the 6-day termination is the standard account). Zimbardo's own conclusion: **the situation, not the people, produced the brutality** — randomly assigned, psychologically normal students became abusive or passive within a role structure.

**The flags (verified — the critiques are standard and important):** (1) the experiment is disputed on method — participant selection may have biased toward aggression-prone volunteers; the guards' behavior was partly coached/encouraged; the "prisoner" arrest process was theatrical (Le Texier's 2019 archival analysis is the fullest critique — flagged); (2) a BBC 2002 replication produced the opposite pattern, showing that *leadership and norms* within the situation, not the situation alone, determine outcomes (Reicher & Haslam — flagged as a single prominent replication); (3) Zimbardo's later book *The Lucifer Effect* (2007) extends the situationist thesis to Abu Ghraib (framed).

**The OB reading (framed):** with Milgram, Stanford is the canonical demonstration of **role and structure overwhelming disposition** — why the OB canon insists on designing *systems* (norms, checks, culture) rather than merely hiring "good people." For a bank: the desks, the P&L mandates, and the risk hierarchy are roles too — which is why conduct risk lives in culture and incentives (§6.1, §7), not just in hiring screens.

### 8.5 The Studies Table

| Study | Year | Finding | The assumption it overturned |
|-------|------|---------|------------------------------|
| **Hawthorne** (Western Electric, Mayo) | 1924–1932 (relay room 1927–1932) | Attention and social relations raise output; informal norms can override incentives | Workers are rational economic machines ("scientific management") |
| **Milgram** (Yale) | 1961 (published 1963) | 65% delivered the maximum 450V shock under authority; all went to 300V | Ordinary people resist authority when it demands harm |
| **Asch** (Swarthmore) | 1951 (extended 1956) | ~1/3 average conformity to an obviously wrong unanimous majority; one dissenter breaks it | People say what they see; groups aggregate true judgment |
| **Stanford** (Zimbardo) | 1971 | Randomly assigned roles produced abusive guards and passive prisoners in 6 days | "Good people" stay good regardless of role and situation |

**The through-line (framed):** all four studies say the same thing from different angles — **behavior is more situational than dispositional**. That single finding is the intellectual justification for the entire OB enterprise: if personality alone explained behavior, management would be selection and nothing else. Because situations matter, organizations are *designable* — and that is the discipline's whole reason to exist.

## 9. The Worked Example: An OB Lens on a Bank Reorg

The test of a lens is whether it changes what you see. Here is a realistic scenario in the familiar context — a Crédit Agricole CIB-style reorg — read through the OB canon of §1–§8. The scenario is fictional but assembled from the standard anatomy of bank restructurings (framed); the OB mechanisms are the verified material of the earlier sections.

### 9.1 The Scenario (framed)

A global investment bank (call it CACIB-style: a Paris HQ, a London hub, an Asia hub in Singapore/HK) announces a **front-to-back reorg of its Global Markets division**: the flow FX/rates business and the e-trading/platform team are merged into a single "Digital Markets" unit; the Singapore and HK sales desks are consolidated under one regional head; the middle-office support function reports into the new unit instead of the COO line. Six months in: headcount attrition up, two senior desks in open conflict over mandate, the platform team is quietly hoarding its roadmap, and the announced "One Digital Markets" identity exists only on PowerPoint. Why, in OB terms, is this happening — and what would a practitioner do?

### 9.2 The Analysis Design: Reading the Reorg at Three Levels (framed)

**Level 1 — The individual level (§2, §3):**

- **Perception (§2.2):** the same reorg is perceived as *opportunity* (the e-trading engineers — higher status, more scope), *threat* (the FX flow salespeople — their book's status declines), and *irrelevant* (the middle office — they have been reorged four times). Attribution errors run hot: the FX desk attributes the merger to political failure at the top (internal attribution to bad character), while the platform team attributes it to their own success (self-serving bias). **Diagnosis:** no shared perception of the change exists; there are three different reorgs happening in three different heads.
- **Attitudes (§2.3):** cognitive dissonance is doing its work in reverse — people publicly committed to the old structure rationalize their loyalty ("the old model was actually client-centric"), and the org chart change has not been accompanied by the behavior change that pulls attitudes along.
- **Motivation (§3):** Vroom's chain is broken at instrumentality — nobody believes performance in the new structure is measured yet, so the announced bonus pool motivates nobody (§3.4). Equity is screaming — the platform hires came in at market rates above tenured incumbents doing the same work in the merged unit (§3.6). Herzberg predicts the all-bonus conversation will not motivate; only the *work* (a real mandate, a real book) will (§3.2).

**Level 2 — The group level (§4, §5):**

- **Teams and dynamics (§4.1–4.2):** the merged unit skipped storming entirely — desks were "integrated" by fiat on day one, so the storming is happening six months late, in public, as mandate wars. Norms collide: the desk's norm is individual accountability and quick decisions; the platform's norm is review, testing, and consensus. Status is the real currency — the merger is being fought as a status war, which is why it looks so personal (§4.2).
- **Conflict (§4.3):** what began as task conflict (how to run a joint book) has degraded into relationship conflict (who is competent) — the canonical functional-to-dysfunctional slide. The Thomas–Kilmann modes in play: competing on both sides, avoiding by the middle office, and no one authorized to collaborate.
- **Power and politics (§4.4–4.5):** the formal org chart gave the new head legitimate power but not expert or referent power in either camp — and the informal system filled the vacuum: the platform team's information control (hoarding the roadmap) and the desks' coalition building are politics, but they are *responsive* politics — the system rewards them because the formal structure did not answer the resource questions. The honest political move is not to suppress this but to redirect it at the joint outcome (§4.5).

**Level 3 — The organization level (§6, §7):**

- **Culture (§6.1):** the merger announcement lived at Schein's level 2 (espoused values: "One Digital Markets," "client first") while levels 1 and 3 never moved — artifacts (desk locations, reporting lines, title conventions) and assumptions (revenue is king; risk is a cost center; the desk wins the argument) are untouched. The two units do not share underlying assumptions about *whose judgment wins* — that is the real integration problem, invisible to the org chart.
- **Change (§7):** judged against Kotter, the reorg failed steps 1, 4, 5, 7, 8 in sequence: no urgency was created (it was announced as a "synergy program," not a burning platform), the vision was under-communicated by a factor of ten, barriers (the competing mandates, the unmerged P&L) were never removed, no short-term win was engineered, and nothing was anchored — no norms, no rituals, no incentives were rewired, so the change will refreeze into the *old* culture (Lewin's refreezing skipped, §7.1).

**The synthesis (framed):** the reorg was executed at one level (the org chart) and judged at one level (financial), but it lived at all three — and the levels fought back. That is not a failure of the plan; it is the plan's failure to include the human system the plan was about.

### 9.3 The Lessons (framed)

1. **Perception is the project.** Before the org chart moves, the story must move: a change that is perceived as three different changes is three changes. Kotter steps 1–4 are perception work (§2.2, §7.2).
2. **Instrumentality first, money second.** Fix the effort→performance→reward chain (Vroom) and the equity wounds (Adams) before the bonus conversation; otherwise the compensation system actively demotivates (§3.4, §3.6).
3. **Let the group storm, then build the norm.** Integration by fiat defers storming, not avoids it. Engineer the conflict to stay at the task level (Thomas–Kilmann: collaborate on the joint book) and build the new team's norm deliberately (Tuckman, §4.1).
4. **Power follows the unanswered question.** If the formal structure does not decide, the informal system will — so decide the mandate, the P&L attribution, and the roadmap ownership explicitly, and give the new head *expert* standing in both camps, not just a title (§4.4).
5. **Culture is the deliverable, not a slide.** The change is complete only when level-3 assumptions shift — which requires new artifacts (where people sit, who chairs what), new rewards, and time. Kotter's step 8 is not the last step; it is the point (§6.1, §7.2).
6. **The situation is the lever.** Hawthorne, Milgram, Asch, and Stanford agree: behavior tracks the situation. Redesign the situations — meetings, mandates, incentives, rituals — and the people follow (§8).
7. **Diagnose before you prescribe.** The consulting discipline applies: read the situation at all three levels, name the mechanisms with the canon's vocabulary, then act — see [mckinsey_approach_guide.md](mckinsey_approach_guide.md) and [management_consulting_skills_guide.md](management_consulting_skills_guide.md) for the applied version of this reflex, and [strategic_management_guide.md](strategic_management_guide.md) for the strategy-side frame (culture as execution).

### 9.4 The Reorg Playbook (framed — the §9.2 analysis compressed into action)

The same analysis, turned into a sequence a practitioner can actually run — each row pairs a Kotter step (§7.2) with the OB mechanism that makes or breaks it:

| # | Kotter step | The OB mechanism to activate (from §2–§6) | The concrete move |
|---|-------------|-------------------------------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | Urgency | Perception (§2.2) — the story lands differently per desk; unfreezing needs felt need (Lewin, §7.1) | Three different town halls, three different burning platforms — not one slide |
| 2 | Coalition | Power (§4.4) — expert and referent power from *both* camps, not just legitimate authority | Co-lead the design phase with one respected name from each desk |
| 3–4 | Vision and communication | Attitudes (§2.3) — public behavior pulls attitudes; perception shapes reality | Get visible early commitment from coalition members; repeat the message 10× |
| 5 | Empower | Motivation (§3) — instrumentality: performance→reward must be credible | Fix the P&L attribution and the mandate *first*; bonus conversation second |
| 6 | Quick wins | Goal-setting (Locke, §3.5) — specific, difficult, achievable with feedback | One jointly delivered client win or platform release inside 90 days, loudly credited |
| 7 | Consolidate | Dynamics (§4.2) — norms and roles need deliberate construction | New rituals (joint desk huddles, shared roadmap reviews) replace old ones |
| 8 | Anchor | Culture (Schein, §6.1) — level 3 assumptions are the deliverable | Change where people sit, who chairs what, and what gets rewarded — then wait a year |

**The one-line rule (framed):** a reorg is not done when the org chart is drawn; it is done when the *assumptions* have moved — and until then, run the playbook.

## 10. The Summary: The Human System

### 10.1 The One-Page Cheat Sheet (framed — the compression the whole guide is built to deliver)

- **The discipline:** OB = the study of what people do in organizations and how that affects performance (Robbins & Judge); multi-level by construction — **individual → group → organization** (verified).
- **The individual (§2):** personality is the Big Five (OCEAN — conscientiousness predicts performance; origin: lexical hypothesis → Goldberg's "Big Five" 1981 → Costa & McCrae, flagged); perception is behavior's real substrate (stereotyping, halo, fundamental attribution error); attitudes (satisfaction, commitment, engagement) predict retention more than performance; **behavior changes attitudes faster than the reverse** (Festinger 1957).
- **The motivation (§3):** Maslow 1943 (needs stack; heuristic), Herzberg 1959 (motivators vs hygiene), McClelland 1961 (achievement/power/affiliation), Vroom 1964 (**M = E × I × V**), Locke 1968 (specific + hard goals + feedback — the most replicated finding), Adams 1963 (equity is comparative and perceptual).
- **The group (§4):** teams need interdependence and storming (Tuckman 1965); norms beat incentives (Hawthorne); conflict is functional until it turns personal (Thomas–Kilmann 1974); power has five bases (French & Raven 1959 — expert and referent beat coercive); politics is the informal system — manage it constructively or it manages you.
- **The leadership (§5):** traits predict emergence, not effectiveness; behavior is learnable (Ohio State, Michigan, Blake–Mouton); **contingency is the cornerstone — Fiedler 1967: match the leader to the situation, engineer the situation, don't just exhort the leader**; modern transformational leadership is the researched paradigm (Burns 1978, Bass 1985).
- **The organization (§6):** culture is Schein's three levels — artifacts, espoused values, **underlying assumptions** (the real operating system); nations differ measurably (Hofstede 1980 — power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance + LTO, indulgence); the culture gap is the gap between level 2 and level 3.
- **The change (§7):** Lewin 1947 — unfreeze, change, refreeze (label flagged); Kotter 1996 — 8 steps from urgency to anchoring; **the sequence fails at unfreezing or at refreezing, never in the middle**.
- **The studies (§8):** Hawthorne (1924–32: attention changes output), Milgram (1961: 65% at 450V), Asch (1951: ~1/3 conform to the unanimous wrong answer), Stanford (1971: roles made the men) — **behavior is more situational than dispositional; design the situation**.
- **The method (§9):** read every situation at three levels — what do the individuals perceive, what are the group dynamics, what are the cultural assumptions — then act on the situation, not just the structure.

### 10.2 The Final Word: The Human System

**The final word — "the human system" (framed):** every organization is two systems running at once — the **formal system** (the org chart, the processes, the P&L, the strategy deck) and the **human system** (perception, needs, norms, status, power, culture, assumptions). The formal system is drawn on paper; the human system is the one that actually executes, resists, games, or embraces whatever the paper says. OB is the discipline that studies the human system with the same seriousness that finance studies the balance sheet — and its entire corpus, from the Hawthorne wiring room to the Stanford basement, is one lesson repeated: **the human system runs on situations, not on instructions; on perceived fairness, not on announced fairness; on culture, not on posters.** A reorg that treats people as org-chart coordinates fails not because people are irrational but because it never engaged the system they actually live in. The professional move — for the architect, the manager, the consultant — is not to fight the human system and not to worship it, but to **read it at all three levels, design its situations, and let the behavior follow**. That is the discipline's whole offer: organizations are human systems, and human systems are designable.

## 12. Glossary

- **OB (Organizational Behavior):** the discipline studying human behavior in organizational settings — the interface between human behavior and the organization, and the organization itself.
- **Organizational behavior:** full name of OB; "the study of what people do in an organization and how that behavior affects the organization's performance" (Robbins & Judge).
- **Individual level:** the micro level of OB — personality, perception, attitudes, motivation, individual decision-making.
- **Group level:** the meso level of OB — teams, dynamics, conflict, power, politics, leadership.
- **Organization level:** the macro level of OB — culture, structure, design, change, HR systems.
- **Personality:** the relatively stable pattern of thoughts, feelings, and behaviors distinguishing one person from another.
- **Big Five:** the five-factor model of personality — OCEAN: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, neuroticism; the scientific consensus model (name coined by Goldberg, 1981).
- **Perception:** the process of selecting, organizing, and interpreting sensory information — behavior follows perceived, not objective, reality.
- **Attitude:** an evaluative statement about an object, person, or event — affect, behavior, and cognition (the ABC model).
- **Motivation:** the force that energizes, directs, and sustains behavior toward a goal.
- **Maslow:** Abraham Maslow — author of the hierarchy of needs (1943, *Psychological Review*).
- **Hierarchy of needs:** Maslow's five-level need stack — physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization; satisfied needs stop motivating.
- **Herzberg:** Frederick Herzberg — author of the two-factor theory (1959, *The Motivation to Work*).
- **Two-factor:** Herzberg's motivators (intrinsic — cause satisfaction) vs hygiene factors (extrinsic — only prevent dissatisfaction).
- **McClelland:** David McClelland — author of the acquired-needs theory (1961, *The Achieving Society*).
- **Vroom:** Victor Vroom — author of expectancy theory (1964, *Work and Motivation*).
- **Expectancy:** Vroom's E — belief that effort leads to performance (one leg of M = E × I × V).
- **Locke:** Edwin Locke — author of goal-setting theory (1968, *Org. Behavior and Human Performance*).
- **Goal-setting:** the finding that specific, difficult, accepted goals with feedback outperform "do your best" — the most replicated performance finding in applied psychology.
- **Adams:** J. Stacy Adams — author of equity theory (1963, *J. Abnormal and Social Psychology*).
- **Equity:** perceived fairness of one's outcome/input ratio against a referent other; inequity is resolved behaviorally, often destructively.
- **Team:** an interdependent group with complementary skills, shared goals, and mutual accountability.
- **Group dynamics:** the forces operating within groups — norms, roles, status, cohesion — and their emergent effects (groupthink, social loafing).
- **Conflict:** a process in which one party perceives another as negatively affecting what it cares about; functional (task) vs dysfunctional (relationship).
- **Power:** the capacity of A to influence B's behavior despite resistance; five bases: coercive, reward, legitimate, expert, referent (French & Raven).
- **Politics:** informal influence over the allocation of scarce resources; the inevitable shadow of formal structure.
- **Leadership:** the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals.
- **Trait (leadership):** the approach that leaders differ from followers on stable characteristics; predicts emergence more than effectiveness (Stogdill 1948).
- **Behavioral (leadership):** the approach that leadership is learnable behavior — initiating structure and consideration (Ohio State), people vs production (Michigan), the managerial grid (Blake–Mouton 1964).
- **Contingency (leadership):** the stance that the best style depends on the situation — Fiedler's model is the flagship.
- **Fiedler:** Fred Fiedler — contingency model (1967); LPC scale; match leader style to situational favorability (leader-member relations, task structure, position power); engineer the situation, not the person.
- **Culture:** a pattern of shared basic assumptions learned as a group solved its adaptation problems (Schein).
- **Schein:** Edgar Schein — three-level culture model (1985, *Organizational Culture and Leadership*): artifacts, espoused values, basic underlying assumptions.
- **Hofstede:** Geert Hofstede — national cultural dimensions (1980, *Culture's Consequences*; IBM data 1967–73): power distance, individualism, masculinity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation, indulgence.
- **Change:** organizational transformation; the Lewin 3-step and Kotter 8-step models are the canon.
- **Lewin:** Kurt Lewin — field theory and the unfreeze–change–refreeze model (1947, "Frontiers in Group Dynamics").
- **Kotter:** John Kotter — 8-step change model (1996, *Leading Change*): urgency → coalition → vision → communicate → empower → quick wins → consolidate → anchor.
- **Hawthorne:** the Western Electric studies (1924–1932) — attention and social relations change output; the "Hawthorne effect" (term coined 1958 by Landsberger); birth of the human relations movement.
- **Milgram:** Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments (1961, published 1963) — 65% delivered the maximum shock; the power of authority and situation.
- **Asch:** Solomon Asch's conformity experiments (1951, extended 1956) — ~1/3 average conformity to a unanimous wrong majority; one dissenter breaks it.
- **Stanford:** the Stanford prison experiment (1971, Zimbardo) — random roles produced abuse and passivity; terminated after 6 days; the situationist thesis.
- **Zimbardo:** Philip Zimbardo — Stanford prison experiment director; *The Lucifer Effect* (2007); the power of roles and situations.
