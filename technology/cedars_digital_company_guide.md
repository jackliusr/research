### 1.7 The Thin-Public-Footprint Note — a Method Disclaimer

Cedars Digital's public footprint has three layers, and the guide's honesty discipline tracks them strictly:

1. **The registry layer (verified).** UEN, incorporation date, entity type, status, address, SSIC codes, paid-up capital — all public, all cross-confirmed by multiple ACRA-derived aggregators. This layer is solid.
2. **The self-published layer (verified *as claims*, not as facts).** The website (cedars-digital.com) and the company's own press releases are real, extractable sources — but they are the company speaking about itself. Quotes from this layer are always labelled "verified as a website claim" or "company-reported".
3. **The independent layer (empty).** No independent newsroom coverage (The Business Times, Tech in Asia, Eco-Business, Straits Times, DealStreetAsia — none surfaced), no funding-round announcements, no independent certification listings, no customer case studies in third-party media. Every claim that would normally be corroborated by this layer is **flagged ⚠**.

The ISO/IEC 42001 claim is the perfect illustration: the *standard* is verified (repo's AI-trust guide §2.3), the *announcement* is real (a PR Newswire APAC wire release dated 26 June 2026, source: CEDARS DIGITAL PTE LTD, republished by wire aggregators), but the *certification itself* is attested only by the company's own release and ceremony photographs. That is the honest state of the record, and §3 reads it accordingly.

**The absence list — what the research pass did *not* find.** The thin-footprint method is as much about absences as presences. This pass explicitly searched for, and did not find: any independent newsroom coverage (The Business Times, Tech in Asia, Eco-Business, The Straits Times, DealStreetAsia, fintechnews.sg, technode.global — all empty for this firm); any funding-round or investor announcement; any SGS or Deloitte publication acknowledging the certification or the partnership; any certificate-listing entry in a certification-body directory; any named customer case study in third-party media; any ACRA-filed financial information (expected — private company); any SG job-board or hiring footprint; and any professional-profile corroboration of the named leadership (CEO Torrent Chin, COO Salmon Sim, Director FuMing Chen) beyond the company's own pages. ### 3.2 The Standard Behind the Claim — ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (Verified facts)

The verified facts about the standard itself come from [ai_trust_assessments_guide.md](ai_trust_assessments_guide.md) §2.3, which this guide treats as authoritative (its own verification log checked ISO/IEC 42001:2023 against primary sources in August 2026):

- **ISO/IEC 42001:2023**, *Information technology — Artificial intelligence — Management system*, was **published in December 2023** by ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, and is **the world's first certifiable AI management system (AIMS) standard** — "the world's first international standard for Artificial Intelligence Management Systems," as the Cedars release itself accurately puts it.
- It follows the ISO **"Annex SL" high-level structure** (clauses 4–10: context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, improvement), so organizations already running ISO 9001/27001 management systems can extend rather than rebuild — a point that matters for a company that already claims ISO/IEC 27001:2022.
- The AI-specific requirements: an **AI policy**, an **AI risk assessment** process, an **AI system impact assessment** (required before deployment and on significant change), lifecycle-oriented planning, and an **Annex A control set** covering data quality, documentation, transparency, incident management, and third-party AI.
- It is **certifiable** — an accredited certification body can certify an organization's AIMS.
- It is designed to **interoperate with the EU AI Act** (the regulatory frame) and the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (the risk-management frame) — and the Cedars release itself cites exactly those two companion frameworks ("*As AI regulations and governance frameworks continue to evolve globally—including the EU AI Act and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework*").

The cross-reference cuts both ways: the company's *framing* of the standard is accurate, and the *existence* of the standard is verified — but the *certification* is a company claim.

### 3.3 What an AIMS Actually Requires

For a solution architect reading the claim, the substance of 42001 is the management system, not the certificate. A certified AIMS (per the verified §2.3 facts) requires, as a system: documented context and interested-party analysis (cl. 4); leadership commitment and an AI policy (cl. 5); planning with AI risk assessment and objectives (cl. 6); support — competence, awareness, documented information (cl. 7); operation — the AI system impact assessment and lifecycle controls (cl. 8); performance evaluation — monitoring, internal audit, management review (cl. 9); and improvement (cl. 10), plus the Annex A control set (data quality, documentation, transparency, incident management, third-party AI). The distinctive artefacts a 42001-grounded vendor should be able to show a buyer are therefore: the **AI policy**, the **AI risk register**, the **AI system impact assessments** for each deployed AI feature (e.g., each CarbonM AI capability), the **Annex-A-style control evidence**, and the **internal-audit and management-review records**. None of those artefacts are public for Cedars Digital — the guide notes the claim, and §7's worked example designs exactly the trust journey a buyer would run to elicit them.

**Mapping the standard to the company's claimed AI features.** Read constructively, the public product claims imply which AI features a Cedars Digital AIMS would have to cover — and therefore which impact assessments and risk entries a buyer should expect: (1) the **invoice/emission classification** AI implied by "AI Guidance" and "Data Synchronization" (the home page's feature row); (2) the **anomaly/quality detection** implied by the claim of fixing "inaccurate data entries" that "many systems struggle with"; (3) the **gap-filling estimation** implied by "AI-driven, scalable solutions" that "calculate your carbon emissions" from minimal inputs ("electricity, gas, or fuel data… in just a few clicks"); (4) the **predictive/recommendation layer** implied by the "Neuralink Database" claims ("learns and evolves, offering predictive insights and strategic recommendations"); and (5) any **generative report summarisation** behind "auto report" and "actionable business intelligence". For each of these, a genuine AIMS would hold a risk assessment and — before deployment — an AI system impact assessment. The mapping is analytical (built from the company's own words); the point is that the *standard's* requirements are knowable and checkable even though the *company's* artefacts are not public. Cross-ref [ai_trust_assessments_guide.md](ai_trust_assessments_guide.md) §2.3 and §7 for the assessment-process counterpart.

## 7. The Worked Example — an AI Carbon-Footprint-Verification Product Design

### 7.1 The Scenario — the ISO-42001-Grounded Trust Journey

**The familiar context:** you are a Solution Architect at a European-headquartered investment bank in Singapore. The bank's sustainability office must report financed emissions (Scope 3, Category 15) and needs a vendor to verify the carbon footprints of the *bank's own* operations (data centres, offices, travel) and, eventually, of counterparties. A shortlist candidate is a young Singapore AI-carbon vendor in the style of Cedars Digital: AI-accelerated carbon-footprint calculation, a carbon-BOM data play, ISO/IEC 27001 and 42001 certification claims, SGS as certification body and Deloitte as advisory partner. The bank's AI-governance policy (FEAT-aligned; EU-AI-Act-aware; NIST-RMF-flavoured — see [ai_trust_assessments_guide.md](ai_trust_assessments_guide.md) §2–§3) says: **no AI-enabled vendor system is adopted without a trust assessment**. Your job: design the *assessment product* — the AI carbon-footprint-verification design that the vendor would have to pass, and the trust journey the bank runs to give it a verified footprint.

This worked example is **fictional and normative**: it designs the *shape* of an ISO-42001-grounded AI carbon-verification system and the *procurement trust journey* around it, using the public blueprint Cedars Digital publishes (CarbonM/CM2: corporate carbon accounting, product carbon footprint, supply-chain carbon management; Measure→Report→Target→Decarbonize; the 42001 AIMS claims; the SGS/Deloitte governance framing). It makes no claim about what Cedars Digital has actually built — it shows what a buyer would design *for* such a vendor, and what the vendor's own claims imply it should be able to evidence.

### 7.2 The Design — VeriCarbon

**VeriCarbon** is the assessment product: an AI-assisted carbon-footprint verification pipeline wrapped in an ISO/IEC 42001-grounded AI Management System, plus the bank-side trust journey that audits it. The design composes the repo's three governance frames: **42001 as the management-system wrapper** (verified facts, §3.2), **AI Verify as the technical testing layer** ([ai_verify_guide.md](ai_verify_guide.md)), and **FEAT/NIST as the bank-side expectations** ([ai_trust_assessments_guide.md](ai_trust_assessments_guide.md)).

**The pipeline (five stages):**

1. **Intake** — the bank pushes source data (utility bills, fuel logs, travel feeds, cloud-provider emissions, data-centre PUE) via API or file drop. Every source gets a provenance record (source, timestamp, owner, hash).
2. **Normalise** — an ingestion layer maps source schemas to a canonical GHG inventory model (Scope 1/2/3 categories; the GHG Protocol framing the vendor's own pages reference). AI is used for *classification* (mapping free-text invoices to emission categories) and *anomaly detection* (outlier readings flagged for human review).
3. **Calculate** — emission factors applied from a managed factor database (the vendor's "carbon BOM" concept: product-level and activity-level factors). AI assists with factor selection and with *gap-filling estimation* where data is missing — **every AI-estimated value is marked as such**.
4. **Verify** — the verification stage is the product's namesake: an evidence check that calculated footprints trace to source data (lineage), that estimates are flagged, and that the report is audit-ready (audit trail, versioning, sign-off workflow).
5. **Report & monitor** — audit-ready reports (the vendor's "Report" step) plus continuous monitoring: new data re-runs the pipeline, and drift in factors or methodology triggers re-verification.

**The ISO-42001-grounded trust journey (what the bank runs before go-live):**

| Stage | Bank action | Evidence the vendor must produce | Frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| T0 — Claim intake | Collect the vendor's certification, partnership and product claims; demand the certificate number | ISO/IEC 42001 certificate number + scope; SGS directory cross-check; ISO 27001 certificate; Deloitte advisory scope letter | ISO 42001 (§3) |
| T1 — AIMS document review | Audit the management system on paper | AI policy; AI risk register; AI system impact assessments (one per AI feature: classifier, anomaly detector, gap-filler); Annex-A-style control evidence (data quality, documentation, transparency, incident management, third-party AI); internal-audit and management-review records | ISO 42001 cl. 4–10 (§3.3) |
| T2 — Technical testing | Run the AI features through the toolkit | Fairness/explainability/robustness/transparency test results for each model; lineage and estimation-flagging demonstrated on a test dataset | AI Verify (§6.4; [ai_verify_guide.md](ai_verify_guide.md)) |
| T3 — Data & controls review | Assess the carbon-data governance | Factor-database governance (versioning, provenance, review); source-data provenance; the "50M+ Carbon BOM data" claim evidenced by data-quality metrics, not a number on a webpage | Data governance ([data_architect_skillgaps_guide.md](data_architect_skillgaps_guide.md)) |
| T4 — Independent verification | Commission an independent check of a sample footprint | A sample of calculated footprints traced end-to-end to source data; estimates re-derived independently | Verification discipline (§7.2.4) |
| T5 — Ongoing assurance | Contract the surveillance | Surveillance-audit schedule for the 42001 certificate (certification is a live system); periodic re-runs of T2; incident-reporting SLAs | ISO 42001 cl. 9–10 (§4.6) |

### 7.3 The Lessons

1. **The ISO-42001 claim converts into a concrete evidence list.** The verified facts of the standard (§3.2) tell a buyer exactly what artefacts a certified vendor must hold: AI policy, risk register, impact assessments, Annex-A controls, audit records. The trust journey (T0–T5) is just those artefacts elicited in procurement order. A genuine certification makes T0–T1 easy; a marketing claim collapses there.
2. **Certification ≠ product quality ≠ regulatory clearance.** 42001 certifies a *management system*; it says nothing about calculation accuracy, factor quality, or bank-specific FEAT compliance. The bank's own verification stages (T2–T4) carry that weight.
3. **The thin-footprint discipline is the buyer's friend.** Every ⚠ in this guide (§2.7, §3.5, §4.5, §5.4) is a procurement question. "Trusted by 90+ international companies" without named, contactable references is a due-diligence gap; the logo wall is not a reference list. The bank should demand named references and — for the counterparty use case — references in its own sector.
4. **Estimation transparency is the heart of carbon-AI trust.** Carbon verification's failure mode is silent estimation masquerading as measurement. A vendor whose product flags every AI-estimated value is architecturally honest; a vendor whose marketing implies measurement where AI estimates occur is not — regardless of certificates.
5. **Certification claims age.** The 42001 claim (June 2026) will need surveillance audits; a buyer should contract the *schedule*, not just the certificate's existence. The same applies to the 27001 claim.
6. **The governance stack composes.** 42001 (management system) + AI Verify (technical tests) + FEAT/NIST (bank expectations) + the bank's own model-risk process ([../banking/risk_management_models_guide.md](../banking/risk_management_models_guide.md)) is a single, composable trust architecture — this is the exact composition the AI-assurance cluster documents ([ai_trust_assessments_guide.md](ai_trust_assessments_guide.md) §2.6).
7. **The vendor's marketing strategy is readable from its claims.** A three-year-old company with a serviced-office address and no financials cannot compete on track record — so it competes on *certification architecture* (42001 + 27001), *named advisory gravity* (Deloitte, SGS) and *data scale claims* (50M+ BOM). A buyer who understands that is a buyer who knows exactly which artefacts to request and which claims to discount until evidenced. The claims table in §2.7 is the vendor's strategy in one page.
8. **Geography is a procurement variable, not just a registry field.** The SG-domiciled / Taiwan-flavoured operation (§1.8, §4.4) matters for data-residency promises, processing locations and contract-governing-law comfort. The trust journey should include a "where is the data actually processed" stage — a question the registry cannot answer and the website's digital-first funnel (trial portal, demo requests) does not volunteer.

|---|---|---|---|

| AI-estimated values silently blended with measured data | Medium | High — misreported footprint | Contractual estimation-flagging requirement; T2 testing; T4 re-derivation |

### 7.5 The Worked-Example Bottom Line

The design's conclusion is not "buy" or "don't buy" — it is **"make the vendor's claims do the work."** Cedars Digital's public strategy (certification claims, named advisers, scale stats) is precisely the evidence set a buyer would want to verify anyway; the trust journey simply operationalises the verification. If the SGS certificate number checks out, the AIMS artefacts exist, the AI features pass the toolkit tests and the sample footprint re-derives independently, then the thin public footprint stops mattering — the vendor has closed the gap with evidence. If any stage stalls, the claims were the point all along, and the bank's answer is no. Either outcome is a *verified* footprint: the vendor's real one, or the claim's absence. That is the honest end-state this series' discipline exists to produce — the same end-state the Bond and Bidot guides reach for their subjects, applied here to an AI-carbon vendor whose entire thesis is the word "verified."
