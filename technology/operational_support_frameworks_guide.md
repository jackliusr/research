## 1. The Support Overview

### 1.1 The Short Answer

**Operational support is the human-and-process machinery that keeps production systems running between releases: the service desk that takes the call, the tiers that escalate the hard cases, the on-call engineer who answers the page at 3 a.m., the change board that approves (or blocks) tonight's release, the problem manager who turns the third recurrence of the same incident into a permanent fix, and the SLOs and error budgets that decide when the team stops shipping features and starts fixing the system.** Every framework in this guide — ITIL, COBIT, SRE, DevOps — is a different answer to a different question about that machinery, and a serious bank runs all of them at once. The one-line summary of the whole discipline: **support is what makes availability a property of the organisation, not just of the software** — the 99.99% in the SLO is delivered by a pager rotation and a runbook as much as by a failover cluster (cross-ref [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §2 for the availability math the support organisation is scored against).

### 1.2 Operational Support — the Definition

**Operational support** (a.k.a. IT operations support, application support, production support) is the set of roles, processes, tools and service levels by which an organisation **detects, responds to, contains, resolves and learns from failures and requests in its production services**, and through which it **protects production from poorly-managed change**. It is the operational half of IT service management: ITSM is the discipline of designing, delivering, operating and improving IT services (the ITIL definition — ✅ cross-ref [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §4.3: "how IT services are *designed, delivered, operated, and improved*"); operational support is the *operate* half, made concrete in tickets, pages, rotations, runbooks and SLAs.

The definition decomposes into five duties, each of which maps to a practice in §3 and a model in §4:

| Duty | What it means | Owned by |
|---|---|---|
| **Detect** | Monitoring, events, alerts, the user call that arrives before the alert does | Monitoring & event management; the service desk |
| **Respond** | Triage, severity classification, the first 15 minutes of an incident | Incident management; L1/L2; on-call |
| **Contain & resolve** | Workarounds, rollbacks, restores, the fix | Incident + problem management; L2/L3 |
| **Learn** | RCA, postmortems, problem records, preventive actions | Problem management; continual improvement |
| **Protect** | Change control, release gates, freeze windows, configuration integrity | Change enablement; release management |

**The verification note:** the *word* "operational support" has no single canonical definition in the way "ITIL 4" or "error budget" do — it is a practitioner term (⚠-structural: consistently used across the ITSM industry to mean the run-the-business support function, but not attributable to one primary source). What *is* precisely verifiable is the machinery beneath it: the ITIL 4 practices of §3 (✅), the tiered support models of §4 (✅-structural, industry-standard), and the SRE overlays of §6 (✅ sre.google primary sources). The definition above is the author's synthesis of those verified parts, and is offered as such.

### 1.4 Reading the Overview Table

The table is the discipline in one sweep, and the pattern to hold is the **layering**: the *models* (§4) and *tooling* (§5) are the visible machinery — the tickets, the pages, the dashboards; the *practices* (§3) are the process definitions that make the machinery consistent; the *frameworks* (§2, §7) are the reference models that give the practices their vocabulary and their place in the organisation; the *overlays* (§6) are the engineering attitude that keeps the machinery from ossifying into ticket-pushing. A bank that buys ServiceNow but skips the incident practice has a very expensive inbox. A bank that runs ITIL processes but no SRE overlay has a very reliable way of being slow. The layers compose. One more lens from §1.2's duty table, worth stating as a chain: **detect → respond → contain → resolve → learn → protect** is the support value chain — and every layer of the framework serves exactly one link in it: monitoring and L0 serve *detect*; the service desk, the severity ladder and the on-call rotation serve *respond*; L2/L3 and the runbooks serve *contain* and *resolve*; problem management and the postmortem serve *learn*; change enablement and the CAB serve *protect*. When an organisation says "support is broken", it almost always means one link of the chain is missing — and the fix is to find the link, not to re-buy the chain.

## 2. The ITIL 4

### 2.1 The 2019 Edition — What Changed

**ITIL 4, the current edition of the IT Infrastructure Library, was published in February 2019 — the first major update since ITIL v3 in 2007** (✅ IT Process Wiki's ITIL 4 reference, extracted live this pass: "launched in February 2019... the first major update to the ITIL framework since 2007"; ✅ the strategy guide's verified line: "**ITIL 4 (2019)** moved to the Service Value System and 34 practices", [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §4.3).

The two structural changes that define the edition:

1. **The service lifecycle was dropped.** ITIL v3 organised its 26 processes along the five-stage service lifecycle (Service Strategy → Service Design → Service Transition → Service Operation → Continual Service Improvement). ITIL 4 replaces lifecycle and *processes* with two components — the **Service Value System** and the **four dimensions** — and *practices* (✅ IT Process Wiki: "The service lifecycle has been dropped in ITIL 4 and the processes replaced with practices. But many of the ITIL 4 practices clearly correspond to the previous ITIL V3 processes.").
2. **The scope statement changed.** ITIL 4 positions itself as "a framework for service management" rather than "IT service management" — explicitly embracing enterprise service management (HR, facilities, customer service running on the same operating model), and it "makes a point" of advising how to integrate Agile, DevOps and Lean into service management (✅ IT Process Wiki). This is the edition's strategic answer to the DevOps challenge: instead of competing with DevOps, ITIL 4 absorbs its vocabulary (value streams, continual improvement, collaboration) while keeping the governance that DevOps culture does not provide.

**The ownership note:** the framework's commercial home is **Axelos**, created in 2013 as a joint venture of the UK Cabinet Office and Capita (✅ strategy guide §4.3). The ITIL trademark and certification scheme now sit in the **PeopleCert group** — Axelos was acquired by PeopleCert in 2021 — visible in this pass in the IT Process Wiki footer ("ITIL® and IT Infrastructure Library® are registered trade marks of the PeopleCert group") and the DevOps Institute footer ("DevOps Institute is member of PeopleCert Group") (✅-secondary: the acquisition is widely documented; the trademark notice is primary evidence of current ownership). The certifications run Foundation → Managing Professional → Strategic Leader → Master (✅ DevOps Institute).

**What ITIL 4 changed for the support organisation.** For a support organisation specifically, the 2019 edition's changes land as three practical shifts (⚠-structural — the synthesis is the author's; each element is verified above):

1. **From processes to practices — the "do what fits" licence.** v3's 26 processes carried an implicit "all of them, in this order" reading; ITIL 4's practices are resources you apply where they fit. A modern support function can run incident, problem, change, request and service-level practices deeply while leaving portfolio management to the strategy office — the framework no longer pretends one size fits all.
2. **From silos to value streams.** The service value chain's six activities and the "demand to value" framing push support to think in end-to-end flows (the four dimensions' *value streams and processes*) rather than hand-offs between teams — the same reframing the DevOps movement made, now inside the ITIL vocabulary.
3. **From gatekeeping to enabling change.** The change-management → change-enablement rename (§2.4) is the edition's most symbolic act: the practice's purpose is "to maximise the number of successful service changes", not to minimise the number of changes attempted. It aligns ITIL's change practice with the SRE error budget (§6.3) and the zero-downtime deployment strategies (✅ [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §3) — change control as risk management, not as friction.

### 2.2 The Service Value System (SVS)

The **Service Value System** is ITIL 4's core model — "how all the components and activities of an organization work together to facilitate value creation" (✅ IT Process Wiki quoting the ITIL 4 Foundation text). The SVS has five elements:

| SVS element | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Guiding principles** | The seven maxims (e.g. *focus on value, start where you are, progress iteratively with feedback, collaborate and promote visibility, think and work holistically, keep it simple and practical, optimise and automate*) that shape decisions — ITIL 4's version of the Agile manifesto ⚠-structural (the seven principles are standard ITIL 4 Foundation material; the count is consistently documented) |
| **Governance** | The directing-and-monitoring layer: who sets direction, who evaluates — the ITIL slice of what COBIT governs at enterprise level (✅-structural; cross-ref the strategy guide's governance section, [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §4) |
| **The service value chain** | Six activities — *plan, improve, engage, design & transition, obtain/build, deliver & support* — the value-creation flow from demand to outcome (✅ IT Process Wiki's SVS elements list; the six-activity chain is standard ITIL 4 material ⚠-structural on the exact names) |
| **Continual improvement** | The practice that keeps the whole system improving — the CSI of v3 reborn as a model (✅-structural) |
| **Practices** | The 34 sets of organisational resources for performing work (§2.4) |

The SVS's significance for a support organisation is that it **frames support as value creation, not cost**: the incident practice exists because an incident destroys stakeholder value, and the service value chain's *deliver & support* activity is where the practices of §3 do their work. The strategy guide's one-line summary captures the same reframing: ITIL asks "**Are we operating IT services well?**" ([../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §4.5).

### 2.3 The Four Dimensions

The **four dimensions** are the holistic lens: every service and every practice must be considered against all four, or it will be lopsided (✅ IT Process Wiki, extracted live — the four dimensions of service management):

1. **Organizations and people** — the teams, skills, culture, authority. A support model with no on-call compensation or no escalation authority fails this dimension.
2. **Information and technology** — the tooling, the data, the CMDB, the monitoring stack (§5). A support model with a great process and a spreadsheet for a ticketing system fails this dimension.
3. **Partners and suppliers** — the vendors, the outsourced L1, the cloud provider, the third-party application maintenance (§5's tooling vendors included). The strategy guide's outsourcing section makes the same point: service-level management is the mechanism that governs "Outsource (managed services / cloud)" (✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §5).
4. **Value streams and processes** — the end-to-end flow of work: how a ticket actually moves from the call to the fix, not how the process diagram says it should.

The four dimensions matter operationally because **every support failure can be traced to a dimension that was ignored**: the incident that took six hours because the on-call engineer had no access rights (people), the change that broke the estate because the CMDB had no dependency record (information), the outage that outlasted the vendor's support window (partners), the SLA that measured the wrong thing because the value stream crossed three teams (value streams). The seven guiding principles of the SVS (⚠-structural — the seven maxims are standard ITIL 4 Foundation material, consistently documented; the one-line glosses are the author's): *focus on value* (every support ticket exists because a stakeholder's value was at risk), *start where you are* (improve the existing estate, don't redesign it from zero), *progress iteratively with feedback* (the postmortem loop of §3.2 is this principle institutionalised), *collaborate and promote visibility* (the bridge, the CAB, the shared dashboard), *think and work holistically* (the four dimensions above), *keep it simple and practical* (the runbook over the 40-page process document), and *optimise and automate* (the toil loop of §6.2). For support specifically, the principles function as **the tiebreaker when two practices conflict** — a change that would break "focus on value" loses to the error budget even if "progress iteratively" wants it shipped (§6.3).

### 2.4 The 34 Practices — Verified Count, Three Categories

**ITIL 4 defines 34 management practices — "sets of organizational resources designed for performing work or accomplishing an objective" — grouped into three categories** (✅ IT Process Wiki, extracted live; ✅ consistent with the strategy guide's "34 practices" in §4.3 and §4.5; the count is verified — 14 + 17 + 3 = 34):

| Category | Count | Practices |
|---|---|---|
| **General management practices** | 14 | Strategy management; portfolio management; architecture management; service financial management; workforce and talent management; continual improvement; measurement and reporting; risk management; information security management; knowledge management; organizational change management; project management; relationship management; supplier management |

## 3. The Key Practices

The practices of §2.4 are the operational core of the support discipline. Five of them — incident, problem, change enablement, request, and service-level management — plus the service desk that fronts them, are the practices a support organisation actually runs day to day. Each is verified below; the verification note is that the *existence and purpose* of each practice is standard ITIL 4 material (✅-structural, consistently documented in the ITIL 4 Foundation literature and the IT Process Wiki's practice pages), while the operational detail added here (severity ladders, CAB mechanics, RCA techniques) is the industry-standard practice layer, flagged ⚠-structural where it goes beyond the framework text.

### 3.1 Incident Management

**Purpose (✅-structural, ITIL 4):** to restore normal service operation as quickly as possible and minimise business impact. An **incident** is an unplanned interruption or reduction in quality of a service — the framework's definition is deliberately broad: it covers everything from "the screen is frozen" to "the payments switch is down".

The operational machinery that makes the purpose real:

- **The severity ladder.** Incidents are classified by business impact and urgency, and the ladder drives everything downstream — response time, who is notified, who has authority. A common banking ladder (⚠-structural, industry-standard shape; the exact thresholds are house-specific): **SEV-1** (critical business service down — e.g. payments switch, trading platform; bridge within minutes, incident commander appointed, executives notified), **SEV-2** (major service degradation or a non-critical service down; same-day resolution target), **SEV-3** (minor degradation; normal queue), **SEV-4** (cosmetic/informational). The classification decision is the first and most important act of the incident process — misclassify down and the response is too slow; misclassify up and you burn the on-call budget on noise (cross-ref the alerting-quality discipline of the zero-downtime guide's monitoring layer, [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §6, and the SRE Workbook's *Alerting on SLOs* chapter ✅). A representative banking severity ladder (⚠-structural — the shape is industry-standard; the numbers are house-typical):

| Severity | Definition | Response target | Bridge | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SEV-1** | Critical business service down (payments, trading) or DORA-major | Immediate; on-call within 5 minutes | Full bridge: commander, scribe, comms, compliance, executives | Hierarchical escalation clock starts; regulator clock if DORA-major (§8.3) |
| **SEV-2** | Major degradation, or non-critical service down | Within 15–30 minutes | Mini-bridge: team lead + L2/L3 | Duty manager notified; same-day resolution target |
| **SEV-3** | Minor degradation, workaround exists | Within business hours (4 h) | None | Normal queue; next-release fix |
| **SEV-4** | Cosmetic/informational | Next business day | None | Normal queue |

The ladder's operational rule: **severity is set by business impact, and it can only be changed by the bridge** — downgrading a SEV-1 is an incident-commander decision, not a quiet ticket edit (⚠-structural). A SEV-1 timeline, worked (⚠-structural, illustrative — the shape is the SRE book's incident-management model, ✅ ch. 14): **00:00** the payments-corridor alert fires (monitoring detected what no user could yet see); **00:02** the on-call engineer acknowledges the page (the 5-minute target of §4.3) and opens the bridge; **00:05** the incident commander takes over — the engineer's job is now *fixing*, not coordinating; the scribe starts the timeline (the future DORA document, §8.3); **00:10** triage: the L2 payments specialist reads the runbook — "SWIFT connectivity loss: check MQ depth, then the network path, then the vendor"; **00:25** rollback decision under the deployment strategy (✅ zero-downtime §3) or the runbook's fallback (stand-in processing, ✅ NETS guide §2.6); **01:10** service restored; **01:15** the bridge stands down, the incident ticket is linked to a problem record (§3.2), and the error-budget burn is tallied (§6.3). The lesson the timeline teaches: **every minute before the commander takes over is a minute of unmanaged chaos** — the role structure is what makes the response repeatable.
- **The incident lifecycle:** *detect* (alert, monitoring event, or the user call — often the user is first) → *log and classify* (ticket, severity, category, impacted service in the CMDB) → *triage and assign* (to the right tier/team per §4) → *diagnose* (runbook first, then investigation) → *contain and resolve* (workaround, restart, rollback, fix) → *recover and verify* (service restored, user confirms) → *close* (ticket, RCA linkage to problem management) → *post-incident review* (the blameless postmortem of §3.2 and §6). ✅-structural — the lifecycle shape is the universal incident-management pattern across ITIL and SRE literature.
- **The bridge and the incident commander.** For SEV-1/2, the response shifts from ticketing to a **war room / bridge**: an incident commander (owns the response, not the technical fix), a scribe (timeline), a comms lead (internal and, in banking, regulatory notification per §8.3), and the technical responders. The Google SRE book's *Managing Incidents* chapter (✅ sre.google — chapter 14) is the canonical treatment: the commander's job is to "delegate, not do", and the timeline is the incident's audit trail.
- **Cross-ref:** the incident-response *mechanics* — severity-driven action, rollback discipline, the error budget an incident spends — are the zero-downtime guide's subject matter (✅ [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §2.3 error budget, §3 rollback/retreat, §7 GameDays as rehearsed incidents). This guide's contribution is the *organisational* side: who is on the bridge, who has authority, how the ticket flows.

### 3.2 Problem Management

**Purpose (✅-structural, ITIL 4):** to reduce the likelihood and impact of incidents by identifying actual and potential causes of incidents and managing workarounds and known errors. The classic formulation: **incidents are symptoms; problems are diseases.** A problem is the underlying cause of one or more incidents (often a problem record is opened because the same incident keeps recurring); a **known error** is a problem with a documented root cause and workaround.

The operational machinery:

- **The problem lifecycle:** *detect* (recurring incidents, a single catastrophic incident, a proactive trend from monitoring) → *log and prioritise* → *investigate and diagnose* (RCA) → *workaround / known-error status* → *permanent resolution* (the change that actually fixes it — which then goes through change enablement, §3.3) → *review and close*. ✅-structural.
- **RCA — Root Cause Analysis.** The canonical techniques (⚠-structural, industry-standard; each has its own literature): **the 5 Whys** (iterate "why" five times from symptom to systemic cause — the Toyota-origin technique), **fishbone / Ishikawa diagrams** (cause categories: people, process, technology, environment), **fault-tree analysis** (top-down logic from failure to causes), and **timeline reconstruction** (the incident timeline from §3.1 interrogated for decision points). The discipline's rule: RCA stops at the *systemic* cause — the process, tooling or design gap — not at the human who pressed the wrong button. A worked 5-Whys, banking flavour (⚠-structural, illustrative): *the payments batch was late* → (1) why? the file transfer hung → (2) why? the counterparty's SFTP server stopped answering → (3) why? our outbound certificate expired → (4) why? no one tracked the certificate lifecycle → (5) why? certificates are managed in a spreadsheet, outside the CMDB and the monitoring estate. The systemic cause is the last why — the fix is a certificate-automation change (through change enablement, §3.3), not a scolding of the operator who noticed at 2 a.m. (Cross-ref the NETS guide's parallel point: "automated certificate renewal without service disruption — the operational detail that keeps a 24/7 estate from dying on a certificate expiry (the classic production incident of every payment shop)", ✅ [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §7.)
- **Blameless postmortems.** The SRE canon's contribution (✅ sre.google — the SRE book's *Postmortem Culture: Learning from Failure*, chapter 15, and the Workbook's postmortem chapter): the postmortem document has a severity, an owner, a timeline, a root-cause analysis, and **action items with owners and dates** — and it is *blameless*: the assumption is that people acting in good faith within a flawed system caused the failure, and the fix targets the system. The book's classic line: postmortems should be "blameless" so that the truth can be told without fear, because the organisation's only defence against the *next* incident is the truth about the last one. Cross-ref the error-budget policy's trigger: a single incident consuming >20% of the budget over four weeks **mandates** a postmortem with at least one P0 action item (✅ §6.3, sre.google Workbook Appendix B).
- **The problem-practice payoff:** every incident closed without a problem record is a ticket that will be re-opened. The metric that proves the practice: **incident recurrence rate** — the same root cause should not produce incidents twice.

### 3.3 Change Enablement

**Purpose (✅-structural, ITIL 4):** to maximise the number of successful service changes by ensuring risks are assessed, authorised and managed. The v3-era name "change management" was retired in ITIL 4 (§2.4) precisely because the practice's job is to *enable* change safely, not to ration it.

The operational machinery:

- **Change types (⚠-structural, standard ITIL 4 taxonomy):** **standard changes** — pre-approved, low-risk, repeatable (password resets, routine patching); they follow a documented procedure and need no individual approval. **Normal changes** — assessed and approved through the change process; categorised by risk/impact (minor/major). **Emergency changes** — required to fix an active incident or avert one; they follow a fast-track approval (often a single authorised approver or a reduced CAB) and are *retrospectively* reviewed. The emergency path is the pressure valve that keeps the incident bridge from being blocked by process during a SEV-1.
- **The CAB — Change Advisory Board.** The body that reviews and authorises normal changes: business, application, infrastructure and security representatives, meeting on a cadence (weekly is typical). The CAB's real function is **risk visibility, not permission**: it exists so that a change's blast radius is seen by the teams who would be affected — the database team, the network team, the security team, the business owner. ⚠-structural on the exact composition (house-specific); ✅-structural on the existence of the role in ITIL change practice. The known anti-patterns: a CAB that rubber-stamps everything (process theatre), a CAB that blocks everything (the "change freeze as default posture"), and a CAB whose meeting *is* the process (no risk data, just opinions).
- **Change windows and freezes.** The operational rhythm of change: **change windows** — scheduled, low-traffic periods when normal changes may be applied (banking's classic tension: the batch window is the only safe window, and the batch window is when the batch runs — cross-ref the NETS guide's point that FAST's 24/7 rail leaves maintenance windows measured in *minutes*, ✅ [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §3.2). **Freeze windows** — blackout periods (year-end, quarter-end, regulatory reporting dates, trading-system freezes) when only emergency changes pass. The strategy guide's Factory quadrant makes the same point from the business side: core ledger, payments, market data and settlement run under "ITIL discipline; change control" (✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §5).
- **Cross-ref the deployment strategies:** change enablement governs *whether* a change may go; the zero-downtime guide governs *how* it goes without spending the error budget — rolling, blue-green, canary, feature flags (✅ [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §3). The modern position: a canary release with instant rollback is *less* risky than a big-bang change that passed a CAB — the CAB should be approving the *mechanism*, not just the *intent* (⚠-structural, the contemporary ITIL-4-meets-SRE synthesis; cross-ref §6.3's error-budget change discipline and §7.3).

### 3.4 Service Request Management

**Purpose (✅-structural, ITIL 4):** to support users' agreed requests — things that are *not* incidents (nothing is broken) and *not* changes (nothing is being modified): access requests, password resets, software installs, information requests. The practice's operational signature is the **service catalogue** — the published menu of what users may request, with the fulfilment path and SLA for each item.

The operational machinery:

- **The catalogue-and-fulfilment model:** every catalogue item has a defined fulfilment flow (often fully automated: request → approval workflow → identity/access automation → completion notification). The strategic goal is **self-service and automation**: the more requests fulfil without a human, the less toil (cross-ref §6.2 — the SRE definition of toil fits a manual access-request fulfilment perfectly: manual, repetitive, automatable, no enduring value).
- **The boundary discipline:** requests must be classified correctly against incidents (nothing broken) and changes (nothing modified). The classic boundary failure: a "request" that is actually a change (a new server for a new service) sneaking through the request path and skipping change enablement — the silent cause of many production incidents. The three-door test that keeps the boundary honest (⚠-structural, the author's operational gloss of the ✅ practice definitions): **is something broken?** → incident. **Is something being changed?** → change. **Is it a routine request on the published catalogue?** → request. The classic boundary failures, banking flavour: a "request" for a new SWIFT counterparty that actually reconfigures the payment routing (a change, sneaking through without CAB review); an "incident" that is really a known, accepted degradation (a problem with a documented workaround, not a fire); a "change" that is really a request (a standard access grant dressed up as a risk item, burning CAB time). The taxonomy matters because each door has a different SLA, a different approval path, and a different audit trail — misrouted work is invisible work.
- **Banking flavour (⚠-secondary, §8's sources):** joiner/mover/leaver access provisioning is the canonical request workflow — new-joiner AD account within an hour, core-banking access grant within four hours with approval, leaver access revocation within two hours (the digitalkimya banking playbook's numbers, ⚠-secondary; the *shape* — automated provisioning with approval gates and SLA targets — is standard practice ✅-structural).

### 3.5 Service Level Management

**Purpose (✅-structural, ITIL 4):** to set, agree, monitor and report the service levels delivered, and to drive corrective action when they are missed. This is the practice that converts availability from an engineering aspiration into a contractual reality: **SLAs** (the external/agreed contracts with customers), **SLRs** (service level requirements — the customer's stated needs), **SLTs** (service level targets — the operational targets behind the SLA), and the **SLO** — the internal target the SRE overlay manages (§6.3; ✅ cross-ref the zero-downtime guide's verified SLO/SLA distinction: SLA is the external contract, deliberately looser than the internal SLO, [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §2.3).

The operational machinery:

- **The SLA as a three-part document (⚠-structural, standard service-management practice):** the *service description* (what is covered), the *targets* (availability %, response/resolution times per severity, batch deadlines), and the *consequences* (credits, penalties, reporting cadence, review meetings). An SLA without consequences is a wish; an SLA without a review meeting is a wall decoration.
- **The metric machinery:** availability % (cross-ref the nines and MTBF/MTTR math, ✅ [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §2.1–§2.2), **MTTR** (mean time to restore — the support organisation's own scoreboard), **MTBF** (mean time between failures — the reliability scoreboard), first-response time, ticket-resolution time per severity, and **SLA attainment %** per service. The strategy guide's customer-facing KPI row names the same set: "System availability (99.9x%); MTTR; SLA attainment; internal-customer NPS; time-to-respond" (✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §8).
- **The honest gap:** service-level management reports the past; it does not fix the future. The fix loop is the partnership between this practice and problem management (§3.2) — a missed SLA is a *symptom*, and the problem record behind it is the disease.

### 3.6 The Service Desk and the Practice Ecosystem

The **service desk** (✅-structural, ITIL 4 service-management practice) is the single point of contact between users and IT: it logs everything, classifies, triages, fulfils requests, and routes incidents — it is the front door of the entire support model of §4, typically staffed by L1 (§4.1). The practice pairings that make the ecosystem work:

- **Service desk ↔ incident management:** the desk is where incidents are born (logged) and where they die (user confirmation on closure).
- **Incident ↔ problem:** every major incident and every recurring incident opens or links a problem record (§3.2).
- **Problem ↔ change enablement:** the problem's permanent fix becomes a change; the emergency fix of a live incident uses the emergency change path (§3.3).
- **Change ↔ release/deployment:** change enablement approves; release management shepherds the build; deployment management lands it (the technical practice; ✅ the zero-downtime guide's §3 deployment strategies are the engineering half of this trio).
- **Monitoring ↔ incident:** monitoring and event management (✅-structural practice) turns infrastructure events into incidents — the automated detector of §1.2's *detect* duty (cross-ref the NOC/monitoring angle of [openshift_workload_availability_guide.md](openshift_workload_availability_guide.md)).
- **Service level ↔ everything:** the SLA is the contract the whole ecosystem is measured against (§3.5).

## 4. The Support Models

The practices of §3 define *what* work exists; the support models of this section define *who does it, when, and how it escalates*. Four models dominate: the tiered L1/L2/L3 model, follow-the-sun, on-call rotations, and escalation matrices. Verification note: the tiered model and follow-the-sun are ✅-structural (industry-standard, multiple consistent sources — the SRE book's *Being On-Call* chapter ✅ names "follow the sun" explicitly); the escalation-matrix taxonomy is ⚠-structural (a practitioner artifact with no single canonical source); the on-call facts are ✅ from the sre.google primary sources.

### 4.1 The Tiered Model — L1 / L2 / L3

**The tiered support model organises the support organisation into escalating levels of expertise, so that routine work is handled by the cheapest capable resource and hard problems reach specialists without the frontline guessing** (✅-structural — the definition and division below match the consistent industry description; cf. the L1/L2/L3 support literature extracted this pass, and the Atlassian support-levels guidance):

- **L1 — the service desk / frontline (✅-structural).** Handles the high-volume, low-complexity work: logging everything, triage and classification, password resets, standard requests, known-issue resolution **from scripts and knowledge-base articles**. Target: resolve 60–80% of tickets at L1 (⚠-structural, the "first-contact resolution" benchmark varies by house). L1 does not investigate; it recognises patterns and follows procedures.
- **L2 — the application/infrastructure specialists (✅-structural).** Deeper technical expertise in a domain (the payments application, the network, the database, the middleware). Handles escalated incidents that need investigation beyond scripts, performs root-cause work with L3, applies standard fixes, manages the vendor interface for L3 work. The tier where most real diagnosis happens.
- **L3 — the engineers / developers (✅-structural).** The people who can change the system: code-level diagnosis, bug fixes, architectural root cause, permanent resolution (which then flows through problem and change enablement, §3.2–§3.3). L3 is typically not a "support team" at all but the engineering teams on rotation for support duty — the bridge to the SRE/DevOps overlay of §6–§7.

The tiered model's two operating rules (✅-structural): **escalation is by defined criteria, not by frustration** — L1 escalates when the procedure is exhausted or the severity demands it, never because the user is loud; and **knowledge flows downward** — every L3 fix that can be scripted becomes an L2 procedure, and every L2 procedure that can be automated becomes an L1 script or a self-service flow (the toil-reduction loop of §6.2). The model's known failure modes: L1 as a ticket-taking wall (no first-contact resolution), L2 as a pass-through (no ownership), and L3 burnout (every incident becomes code-level because L2 never learned the system). Two boundary tiers complete the picture (⚠-structural, the extended-tier taxonomy is consistently documented in the support-tiers literature though less canonical than L1–L3): **L0** — the self-service and automation layer: the knowledge base, the chatbot, the self-service password reset, the automated fulfilment of §3.4. L0 is where the toil-reduction loop of §6.2 lands first, and its quality is measured by *deflection rate* — the share of would-be tickets that never became tickets. **L4** — the vendor/ecosystem tier: the third-party application maintenance, the cloud provider, the hardware vendor. L4 work is *governed*, not performed: the support organisation owns the ticket, the SLA and the vendor relationship (the partners dimension of §2.3; cross-ref the strategy guide's outsourcing section — "service-level management (ITIL); third-party risk management; exit plans", ✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §5). The full ladder is thus **L0 (self-service) → L1 (frontline) → L2 (specialists) → L3 (engineers) → L4 (vendors)**, with escalation discipline governing every hop.

### 4.2 Follow-the-Sun

**Follow-the-sun is the multi-site staffing model in which support work follows daylight around the globe: Singapore hands the open queue to London at the end of its day; London hands it to New York; New York hands it back to Singapore — 24-hour coverage with nobody working nights.** The model's purpose is not just coverage; it is *day-shift quality* at all hours — the engineers who touch the incident are awake, alert and fresh, and handover happens at natural shift boundaries (✅ the SRE book's *Being On-Call* chapter verifies the concept and its motivation: "a multi-site 'follow the sun' rotation allows teams to avoid night shifts altogether" — night shifts being "detrimental to people's health", ✅ sre.google ch. 11).

The operating requirements (✅-structural, with the SRE-book verifications noted): **at least two sites in distant time zones** (Singapore + Paris/London is the classic Cymbal Bank pairing — the group's APAC hub and its European home, cross-ref [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) §10 for the Singapore-hub context); **a documented handover** — the open-incident state, the pending changes, the known issues, the overnight alerts — at each site transition (⚠-structural, standard practice; the handover quality *is* the model's reliability); **shared tooling** so the ticket state travels with the sun (the ITSM platform of §5 is the handover vehicle); and **clear ownership of the handoff moment** — the 30-minute overlap where the incoming site owns nothing yet and the outgoing site owns nothing more is where incidents get dropped (⚠-structural). The model's trade-off, per the SRE book: multi-site teams "incur communication and coordination overhead" (✅), so the decision to go multi-site should be driven by the system's importance and workload — which, for a global bank's payment rails, is exactly the case (§8). The handover that makes follow-the-sun work — the checklist every site transition must cover (⚠-structural, the author's operational gloss; the *requirement* for a documented handover is standard practice): (1) **open incidents** — state, owner, next action, any bridge still warm; (2) **pending changes** — what is in flight, what is approved for tonight, what the incoming site must watch; (3) **overnight alerts and known noise** — what paged, what was triaged, what is a known false positive; (4) **vendor tickets** — which third parties are chasing whom; (5) **the risk register of the day** — degraded services running on workarounds or stand-in (✅ NETS guide §2.6); (6) **the outgoing site's gut** — the one thing that felt off but could not be proven. The ritual's discipline: the handover is a *meeting with a written output*, not a chat message — because the incoming site will be judged on what it was told, and the audit trail will ask.

### 4.3 On-Call Rotations

**On-call is the rotation in which a named engineer (or pair) carries the pager for a shift — nights and weekends included — and must respond to pages within an agreed time.** The SRE book's *Being On-Call* chapter (✅ sre.google, ch. 11, extracted live this pass) is the canonical treatment, and its verified facts:

- **Response targets:** "Typical values are 5 minutes for user-facing or otherwise highly time-critical services, and 30 minutes for less time-sensitive systems" (✅). The target derives from the availability math: a 99.99% quarterly SLO allows ~13 minutes of quarterly downtime, so the reaction time "has to be in the order of minutes" (✅ — cross-ref the nines table, [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §2.1).
- **Primary and secondary rotations:** "Many teams have both a primary and a secondary on-call rotation" — the secondary as fall-through for missed pages or as the handler of non-urgent production activity; two related teams often serve as each other's secondary (✅).
- **Balanced on-call:** on-call quality is judged on two axes — *quantity* (percent of time on-call) and *quality* (incidents per shift). The SRE cap: **no more than 25% of time on-call**, with at least 50% of SRE time on engineering work (✅ ch. 5 and ch. 11: "we strive to invest at least 50% of SRE time into engineering: of the remainder, no more than 25% can be spent on-call"). The derived staffing math: a single-site 24/7 rotation with two people on-call (primary + secondary) needs **a minimum of eight engineers** on week-long shifts — each on-call one week in four (✅ ch. 11); dual-site teams need about six per site.
- **The sustainability rule:** on-call that burns people out is an incident generator, not an incident absorber. The SRE response is structural — the 25% cap, the follow-the-sun avoidance of night shifts, and the rule that every page that required a human should become an automation item (the toil loop of §6.2). ⚠-structural on the exact compensation and wellness policies (house-specific); ✅ on the SRE caps.

### 4.4 Escalation Matrices

**The escalation matrix is the documented map of who to escalate to, when, and through which channel — the two dimensions being *functional* (which team: L1 → L2 → L3 → problem management → vendors) and *hierarchical* (which level of management: team lead → duty manager → head of IT → CIO/board for the biggest incidents).** ⚠-structural — the matrix is a universal operational artifact (every serious support organisation has one) but has no single canonical public definition; the taxonomy below is the standard industry shape.

- **Functional escalation:** the §4.1 tier ladder plus the practice handoffs of §3 — incident → problem (recurrence or major incident), incident → change (emergency change during a SEV-1), support → vendor (third-party application, cloud provider — the partners dimension of §2.3; cross-ref the strategy guide's vendor-SLA governance, ✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §5).
- **Hierarchical escalation:** time-based (SEV-1 not contained in 30 minutes → duty manager; not resolved in 2 hours → CIO) and severity-based (SEV-1 → executives immediately). Its purpose is not blame but *authority*: the manager who can unlock budget, staff, vendors or regulatory comms (cross-ref §8.3's regulatory notification duty).
- **The matrix as a table:** rows = severity levels; columns = elapsed-time thresholds; cells = who gets called and by what channel (page, phone, email). The matrix must be **published, drilled and versioned** — an escalation matrix that lives in someone's head is not an escalation matrix (⚠-structural; the GameDay practice of rehearsing it is ✅ SRE, [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §7.3). An example matrix, banking flavour (⚠-structural, illustrative):

| Elapsed time | SEV-1 (payments down) | SEV-2 (channel degraded) |
|---|---|---|
| **0–15 min** | On-call engineer (page), L2 lead (page) | On-call engineer (page) |
| **15–30 min** | Incident commander + scribe on bridge | L2 lead on bridge; duty manager notified |
| **30–60 min** | Duty manager; compliance starts the DORA clock if major | Team lead + business owner notified |
| **1–4 h** | CIO, business sponsor, regulator notification (§8.3) | Post-incident review scheduled |
| **> 4 h** | Executive crisis committee; vendor management engaged | Escalation to CIO |

The matrix's hidden requirement: every cell names a *person or role*, not a team — "the payments L2 lead" pages someone; "the payments team" pages no one (⚠-structural).

## 5. The Tooling

### 5.1 The ITSM Platform — the System of Record

Every support model in §4 and every practice in §3 runs on an **ITSM platform**: the system of record for tickets, changes, problems, requests, SLAs, and — critically — the **CMDB** (Configuration Management Database), the dependency map that tells an incident responder which business service a failing component takes down. The three platforms that dominate the enterprise market — **ServiceNow, BMC Helix and Jira Service Management** — are not interchangeable ticketing tools; they are three different philosophies of what support is for. Around the system of record sits the wider support toolchain: the **alerting and on-call layer** (PagerDuty/Opsgenie-class — the paging, the rotation schedules, the escalation clocks of §4.3–§4.4), the **monitoring and observability layer** (the platform stack of [openshift_workload_availability_guide.md](openshift_workload_availability_guide.md); cross-ref the SRE Workbook's *Monitoring* chapter ✅-structural), the **knowledge base** (the runbook and known-error home, §3.2), and increasingly the **AIOps layer** that correlates alerts into incidents before a human sees them (the BMC ServiceOps differentiator of §5.3 is the same idea; ⚠-secondary). The integration rule (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis): alerts must become tickets automatically, tickets must link to changes, and changes must link to code — the incident↔change↔deployment linkage that JSM does natively (§5.4) is the standard every toolchain should meet, because it is the difference between an incident that takes 20 minutes to understand and one that takes 2. The **CMDB deserves its own paragraph**, because it is the quiet critical asset of the whole tooling layer (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis; the CMDB's role is ✅-structural ITIL — service configuration management). The CMDB is the dependency map: which configuration item (CI) supports which business service, and what else depends on it. Its operational payoff is the **blast-radius query** — the SEV-1 responder asks "what does this failing component take down?" and the answer comes back in seconds instead of a 40-minute war-room archaeology session. Its failure mode is **drift**: a CMDB fed by manual updates dies within a quarter; one fed by discovery tooling stays alive. Its banking non-negotiables (⚠-secondary §8.3's criteria): relationship depth (the blast-radius query), immutability of the audit trail, and data-residency compliance. A bank's CMDB is not a database — it is the organisation's model of itself, and the incident practice (§3.1), the change practice (§3.3), the problem practice (§3.2) and the SLA practice (§3.5) all read from it. The positioning below is verified against the enterprise-ITSM comparison literature extracted live this pass (the technologymatch 2026 decision guide — ⚠-secondary but vendor-neutral and consistent with the wider market picture ✅-structural) and the banking tool-selection guidance (§8, ⚠-secondary).

### 5.2 ServiceNow — the Consolidator

**Positioning: the enterprise platform — "the standard platform that aims to run your entire company"** (⚠-secondary technologymatch; ✅-structural — ServiceNow's market-leader position in enterprise ITSM is consistently documented). The philosophy: one platform, one data model, one workflow engine for IT, HR, customer service, security and facilities — the "single system of record" thesis. Its operational strengths: the **strategic CMDB** (business-service mapping — "you can see exactly which server powers which business application"), the breadth of the workflow catalogue, the integration ecosystem, and increasingly **AI agents** that execute tasks (restart a service, check a server, confirm the fix) rather than merely chat (⚠-secondary). Its costs: the **most expensive** option, with **6–18 month implementations** and specialist developers to maintain — the platform's true price is organisational. For a tier-1 bank it is the default choice: DORA-ready templates, CMDB depth for blast-radius analysis, immutable audit trails (⚠-secondary §8.3's banking tool-selection criteria).

### 5.3 BMC Helix — the Operator

**Positioning: the infrastructure specialist — the heir of BMC Remedy, rebuilt as a modern containerised platform, focused on keeping complex estates (mainframes, multi-cloud) stable** (⚠-secondary technologymatch: "BMC Helix is the Operator. Its goal is to prevent outages"). The philosophy: the service desk should be *wired into the infrastructure*, not bolted onto it — its **ServiceOps** differentiator ingests millions of infrastructure alerts and **correlates them into a single incident ticket**, so root cause of an outage is found faster. Its strengths: **deployment flexibility** (on-premise or private cloud — decisive for government, defence and data-sovereign banks), **mainframe visibility** (cloud-native tools miss mainframes; Helix sees them), and multi-tenant complexity handling (⚠-secondary). Its weaknesses: the more industrial UX and a thinner talent pool. The banking fit: strong for **regional banks and infrastructure-heavy shops** (⚠-secondary §8's tool-selection guidance: "Regional bank (MENA/Europe) — BMC Helix: strong CMDB, multi-tenancy, cost-effective for mid-size").

### 5.4 Jira Service Management — the Accelerator

**Positioning: the dev-speed challenger — Atlassian's ITSM product, built for engineering-led organisations that want support fused with the software delivery toolchain** (⚠-secondary technologymatch: "JSM is the Accelerator. Its goal is speed"). The philosophy: heavy ITSM process is the enemy; support should live where the developers live. Its signature capability is the **dev-ops fusion**: when a bad deployment crashes a site, JSM automatically links the incident ticket to the code change in Bitbucket/GitLab, so the support team sees the probable cause instantly (⚠-secondary; the *concept* — incident↔change linkage — is standard ITSM practice ✅-structural, and JSM's native implementation is its differentiator). Its strengths: **deployment in weeks**, transparent pricing, seamless Jira Software integration. Its limits: complex non-IT workflows and asset management that needs more manual setup than ServiceNow's discovery. The banking fit: **digital-native and challenger banks**, or a Cymbal Bank estate's *internal developer-facing* support where speed beats formality (⚠-secondary §8's guidance: "Digital-native / challenger bank — Jira SM + Atlassian: fast setup, DevOps native").

### 5.5 The Tooling Table

| Platform | Positioning | Philosophy | Strengths | Weaknesses | Banking fit | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ServiceNow** | The Consolidator | One platform for the whole enterprise (IT/HR/CSM) | Strategic CMDB; workflow breadth; ecosystem; AI agents | Most expensive; 6–18-month implementations; specialist maintenance | Tier-1 global banks: DORA templates, blast-radius CMDB, audit trails | ⚠-secondary (2026 comparison literature); ✅-structural market-leader position |

| **Jira Service Management** | The Accelerator | Support fused with the dev toolchain; speed over ceremony | Weeks to deploy; transparent pricing; incident↔code linkage | Complex non-IT workflows; manual asset setup | Challenger/digital-native banks; developer-facing support | ⚠-secondary |
| **The CMDB** (across all three) | The dependency map | Know which component serves which business service | Blast-radius analysis; impact assessment; SLA targeting | Drifts stale without discovery automation | Non-negotiable in banking (impact calculation, audit) | ✅-structural (practice); ⚠-secondary (vendor depth claims) |

A compact selection decision-guide for the estate layer (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis of §5.2–§5.4): choose **ServiceNow** when the requirement is one regulated system of record across a large estate (the tier-1 bank default — the CMDB, the audit trail, the DORA templates); choose **BMC Helix** when the estate's soul is infrastructure — mainframes, multi-cloud, on-prem sovereignty — and alert correlation is the priority; choose **Jira Service Management** when the users of the tool are developers and the requirement is speed and incident↔code linkage, accepting that the deep governance workflows are not its home. The honest caveat: the platforms are converging (ServiceNow is adding developer speed, JSM is adding governance depth), so the deciding factor is increasingly **where the estate's centre of gravity is — regulated core, infrastructure depth, or developer velocity** — not the feature grid.

**The tooling lesson (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis):** the platform choice is an operating-model choice, not a features choice. A bank that buys ServiceNow and skips the practices of §3 has bought a very expensive inbox; a bank that adopts JSM for its payments switch because it is fast has bought speed in the wrong place. The rule of thumb from §8's banking guidance: **match the platform's philosophy to the estate's criticality** — the tier-1 core on the consolidator platform, the developer tooling on the accelerator, and the infrastructure depth where the mainframes are.

## 6. The SRE Overlay

### 6.1 What SRE Adds — Engineering, Not Ticket-Pushing

**SRE — Site Reliability Engineering — is Google's approach to running production services: software engineers who treat operations as an engineering problem, capped at 50% operational work so that at least half their time goes to automation and reliability engineering** (✅ sre.google — the SRE book's *Eliminating Toil* and *Being On-Call* chapters, extracted live this pass; ✅ the book's identity and the SLI/SLO/error-budget framework are cross-ref'd from [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §2.3 — the 2016 O'Reilly edition, Beyer/Jones/Petoff/Murphy). The DevOps Institute's framing: SRE is "a post-production set of practices for operating large systems at scale, with an engineering focus on operations" (⚠-secondary but consistent). For a support organisation, SRE is the **overlay** that prevents the §3–§5 machinery from decaying into ticket-pushing: it adds the toil budget (§6.2), the error budget (§6.3), the blameless postmortem (already §3.2), and the on-call engineering discipline (§4.3). It does not replace ITIL — it operates *inside* ITIL's incident and change practices with an engineering attitude (the comparison is §7). **How SRE lands on a bank (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis of the verified parts):** the overlay is applied *selectively* — you cannot run SLOs and an error budget for every internal tool, but you absolutely run them for the payments rail and the trading platform (§8.2). The SRE Workbook's engagement models (✅-structural — the Workbook's *SRE Engagement Model* chapter, from full ownership to consulting/advisory) give the bank a ladder: full SRE ownership for the critical services, SRE advisory for the channels, and no SRE at all for internal utilities — the same selectivity §4.2's multi-site decision demands. The engagement's success criterion is the toil-and-error-budget pair: the team's operational work stays under 50% (✅ §6.2), and the service's change velocity is governed by its budget (✅ §6.3) — not by the CAB's mood.

### 6.2 Toil — the Verified Definition

**Toil is the operational work tied to running a production service that is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, devoid of enduring value, and scales linearly with service growth** (✅ sre.google, *Eliminating Toil*, by Vivek Rau — extracted live this pass; the six attributes are quoted from the chapter). The chapter's sharpening details, all ✅:

- **Not all operational work is toil.** *Overhead* — meetings, HR paperwork, goal-setting — is not toil (it is not tied to running the service). Grungy work *with* enduring value (cleaning up a decade of alerting config) is not toil. "Toil is not just 'work I don't like to do.'"
- **The six attributes:** *manual* (a human's hands-on time, even running a script); *repetitive* (first-time or second-time work is not toil — it is learning); *automatable* ("if a machine could accomplish the task just as well as a human... that task is toil"); *tactical* (interrupt-driven and reactive — "handling pager alerts is toil"); *no enduring value* ("if your service remains in the same state after you have finished a task, the task was probably toil"); *O(n) with growth* (linear scaling with traffic — an ideally managed service grows an order of magnitude with zero additional work). "Not every task deemed toil has all these attributes" — the more it matches, the more likely it is toil.
- **The 50% rule:** "Our SRE organization has an advertised goal of keeping operational work (i.e., toil) below 50% of each SRE's time. At least 50% of each SRE's time should be spent on engineering project work" (✅ ch. 5; the on-call cap of 25% is the same budget refined in ch. 11, §4.3).
- **Why toil matters (✅ ch. 5):** excessive toil "creates confusion" about what an engineering organisation is, "slows progress", "sets precedent" (devs load you with more toil), "promotes attrition" (your best engineers leave), and "causes breach of faith" (new hires promised engineering work). The chapter's epigraph: **"If a human operator needs to touch your system during normal operations, you have a bug."** — Carla Geisser, Google SRE (✅).
- **The support-organisation mapping (⚠-structural, the author's application):** the §4.1 L1 scripted work is toil by design — and the toil budget is the scoreboard for how fast the L1 script becomes an L2 procedure, and the procedure becomes automation or self-service (§3.4). A support desk whose ticket volume grows linearly with the user base is a toil factory; one whose ticket volume is flat while the estate grows is an engineering organisation wearing a support hat. The mapping, made explicit (⚠-structural, the author's application of the ✅ chapter attributes):

| Toil attribute (✅ ch. 5) | Support-desk example | The fix that removes it |
|---|---|---|
| Manual | Running the same SQL to re-sync a queue every night | Automation: the script runs itself, alerts only on failure |
| Repetitive | The weekly "certificate about to expire" dance | Lifecycle tracking in the CMDB + auto-renewal (✅ NETS guide §7) |
| Automatable | Copy-paste access grants for every joiner | Self-service request + IAM integration (§3.4) |
| Tactical | Pager alerts that need a human every time | Alert tuning, runbook first, alert-on-SLO (✅ zero-downtime §2.3) |
| No enduring value | Restarting the hung batch job | Problem management: why does it hang? (§3.2) |
| O(n) with growth | One ops ticket per new client onboarding | Productise onboarding: configuration-as-code, zero-touch |


### 6.3 Error Budgets — the Verified Machinery

**The error budget is the SRE control mechanism that decides when change may proceed: error budget = 1 − SLO, and unplanned outages and planned changes spend the same budget** (✅ cross-ref the zero-downtime guide's verified framework, [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §2.3 — including "100% is the wrong target", the 99.9% ≈ 43 minutes/month arithmetic, and the 99.95% ≈ 21.6 minutes/month worked example). This section adds the *policy* machinery verified this pass from the SRE Workbook's **Example Error Budget Policy** (✅ sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy — Appendix B of the Workbook, by Steven Thurgood, 2018):

- **The policy's mechanics (✅):** "If the service is performing at or above its SLO, then releases... will proceed according to the release policy. If the service has exceeded its error budget for the preceding four-week window, we will halt all changes and releases other than P0 issues or security fixes until the service is back within its SLO." — the **launch freeze**, the exact policy the zero-downtime guide cross-refers.
- **The outage trigger (✅):** "If a single incident consumes more than 20% of error budget over four weeks, then the team must conduct a postmortem. The postmortem must contain at least one P0 action item to address the root cause." — the error budget as the *postmortem trigger*, institutionalising §3.2's learning loop.
- **The why (✅):** "Changes are a major source of instability, representing roughly 70% of our outages" — the Workbook's verified statistic, which is precisely why the change practice (§3.3), the deployment strategies (✅ [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §3) and the error budget are the same conversation.
- **The worked arithmetic (✅):** "A 99.9% SLO service has a 0.1% error budget. If our service receives 1,000,000 requests in four weeks, a 99.9% availability SLO gives us a budget of 1,000 errors over that period."
- **Escalation (✅):** disagreements about budget calculation or actions escalate to the CTO — the governance resolution channel.

**The overlay's operational meaning (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis):** the error budget turns the §3.3 change-window argument from opinion into accounting. The bank that refuses the maintenance window no longer needs a debate — the 21.6 minutes/month budget at 99.95% makes the 10-minute user-visible window a *fact* (cross-ref the zero-downtime guide's §9 "the business refuses the maintenance window" point). And the freeze policy gives the support organisation the authority it never had under pure ITIL: when the budget is spent, change stops — not because the change board said so, but because the SLO says so. Worked numbers for the payments rail (✅ arithmetic, cross-ref the zero-downtime guide's §2.3 and §2.5 worked examples): a **99.95% SLO** over a 30-day window buys **≈21.6 minutes** of allowed failure per month — and a single 10-minute user-visible maintenance window spends nearly half of it; at **99.9%** the budget is **≈43 minutes** a month, and the Workbook's framing (✅) shows the same budget as **1,000 errors per million requests over four weeks**. The support reading: every SEV-1 on the rail is not just an outage — it is a *budget withdrawal* with a receipt, and the receipts accumulate into the freeze decision. The freeze is not a punishment; per the Workbook's policy, it is "permission to focus exclusively on reliability when data indicates that reliability is more important than other product features" (✅) — the error budget reframing the oldest management argument (reliability vs features) as a number.

## 7. The Comparison

### 7.1 Four Frameworks, Four Questions

The support discipline is governed by four frameworks that practitioners endlessly pit against each other — and that a serious organisation runs **simultaneously**. Each answers a different question, with different machinery, metrics and ownership. The verified one-liners from the strategy guide set the frame: **"COBIT governs, ITIL manages services, TOGAF architects"** (✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §4.1 — TOGAF is included in the strategy guide's governance table for completeness but is not a support framework, so it stays out of this comparison). The DevOps Institute's 2022 adoption data shows how the market actually composes them: 58% DevOps, 37% ITIL, 18% SRE — with "many of the teams we surveyed using all three" (⚠ single-survey source, DevOps Institute *Upskilling IT* 2022).

- **ITIL 4** — the *service-management* framework (✅ §2): "Are we operating IT services well?" (✅ strategy guide §4.5). Machinery: the SVS, 34 practices, the service value chain. Metric: SLA attainment, service quality, consistency. Owner: the service-management function.
- **COBIT** — the *governance* framework (✅ strategy guide §4.2): "Are we governing and controlling IT properly?" Machinery: the goals cascade, 40 core governance/management objectives across five domains (EDM/APO/BAI/DSS/MEA), control objectives as audit evidence. Metric: control effectiveness, audit findings, regulatory compliance. Owner: audit, risk, the board.
- **SRE** — the *reliability-engineering* approach (✅ §6): "How do we run production as an engineering discipline, and how much risk do we accept?" Machinery: SLI/SLO/error budget, toil budgets, on-call engineering, blameless postmortems. Metric: error-budget burn, toil %, MTTR. Owner: the SRE/engineering teams.
- **DevOps** — the *culture-and-delivery* movement: "How do dev and ops collaborate to deliver value fast and safely?" Machinery: multidisciplinary teams, CI/CD pipelines, gradual change, automation-first (✅ DevOps Institute framing: "the creation of multidisciplinary teams of Dev and Ops to replace siloed Development and Operations"). Metric: deployment frequency, lead time, change-failure rate, MTTR — the DORA four (⚠-structural; the DORA metrics are the standard DevOps scorecard, cross-ref the [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) DevSecOps section for the pipeline-gate sibling). Owner: everyone — which is both its strength and why it needs the other three's structure.

### 7.3 Reading the Comparison — They Compose, They Do Not Compete

The head-to-head is a trap: the four frameworks have **different jobs, different owners and different failure modes**, and the mature organisation runs them as layers of one operating model (the strategy guide's governance-mechanisms section makes the identical argument for COBIT/ITIL/TOGAF: "complementary, not competing", ✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §4.1).

- **COBIT under everything.** The board, the auditors and the regulators read COBIT evidence: who approved the change, who had access, what was reported within the DORA clock (§8.3). Support processes that leave no audit trail fail COBIT no matter how fast they restore service.
- **ITIL in the middle.** The practices of §3 are the *shared vocabulary* the whole organisation uses — the ticket states, the change types, the SLA definitions. Without ITIL, SRE and DevOps improvise different words for the same things and the audit trail has holes.
- **SRE on the critical services.** The error budget and the toil budget are applied where reliability is a business property: the payments rail, the trading platform, the market-data feeds (§8). ITIL says *how* to run the incident practice; SRE says *whether the service is earning the right to change*.
- **DevOps everywhere as the delivery culture.** The pipelines, the multidisciplinary teams and the gradual-change discipline make the *change* that ITIL governs and SRE budgets cheap enough to do safely (cross-ref the zero-downtime guide's deployment strategies — [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §3 — and the DevSecOps pipeline gates of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6).

**The composition failure modes:** ITIL without SRE = process theatre (everything documented, nothing measured against the budget); SRE without ITIL = brilliant reliability, no audit trail; DevOps without either = speed with no governance; COBIT without the rest = a control framework floating above an unmanaged estate. The worked example of §9 shows the four layers composed on one estate.

## 8. The Banking Context

### 8.1 Why Banking Is the Hardest Support Environment

Banking combines every support-stress factor at once: **24/7 availability expectations** (the payment rails never sleep), **regulatory clocks** (incident reporting with hour-level deadlines), **concentrated blast radii** (one core system fails → millions of customers, or the whole market's settlement), **legacy-and-modern estates in coexistence** (mainframes beside cloud microservices), and **audit scrutiny of every operational action**. The strategy guide's Factory quadrant names the systems and the posture: core banking ledger, payments (SWIFT/MEPS+), market-data feeds, settlement — "ITIL discipline; change control; 'run-the-bank' budget protected" (✅ [../management/it_strategy_guide.md](../management/it_strategy_guide.md) §5). The NETS guide's FAST analysis adds the sharpest single fact: the real-time rail runs **24/7/365, and its maintenance windows are measured in minutes, not nights** (✅ [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §3.2) — a support organisation cannot schedule its way out of that; it must be *always there* (the §4 models, composed). And the stakes are quantified in the banking-ITSM literature: **a 15-minute trading-system outage can cost millions** (⚠-secondary digitalkimya). Two lessons from the regional banking record sharpen the point. First, the **2023–24 DBS outage saga** — the Singapore regulator's scrutiny of repeated digital-banking outages showed the regulatory and reputational blast radius of support failure on customer-facing channels (✅ cross-ref [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §3.2, where the FAST analysis cites it; the saga's details are out of scope here). Second, the **switch architecture itself provides the support model's fallback**: stand-in processing (STIP) and store-and-forward queues mean a payments switch can serve cached authorisations while the issuer is unreachable — support's job is to *know* the degradation mode is active and to reconcile the difference later (✅ [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §2.6). The operational reading: **in banking, availability is partly a property of the support organisation's awareness** — the stand-in that runs unattended and unreconciled is a latent incident.

### 8.2 The Critical Banking Systems

The estate that a banking support organisation protects (cross-ref the bank series and the umbrella guides — the table's systems are the verified families of [../banking/core_banking_systems_guide.md](../banking/core_banking_systems_guide.md), [../banking/capital_markets_architecture_guide.md](../banking/capital_markets_architecture_guide.md) and [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md)):

| System family | What it is | Support profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Core banking / ledger** | The accounts, balances, posting engines — the record of money | Batch windows + real-time; quarter-end stress; zero tolerance for silent corruption |
| **Payments & settlement** | SWIFT, MEPS+, FAST/PayNow, GIRO, card switches — the money movement | 24/7/365 (FAST); minutes-scale maintenance windows (✅ NETS guide §3.2); the 0800 echoes never stop (✅ NETS guide §2.6 — network-management heartbeats as the monitoring surface) |
| **Trading & markets** | Order management, e-trading, market data, risk engines | Trading-day availability; 15-minute outages costing millions (⚠-secondary); pre-open and close-of-day critical windows |
| **Market data & reference data** | Feeds, pricing, client/security master data | The upstream of everything; feed breaks cascade (cross-ref the zero-downtime guide's cascading-failure patterns) |
| **Risk & regulatory reporting** | Basel/regulatory capital, risk engines, regulatory returns | Hard deadlines (report submission); audit evidence; the COBIT control layer (✅ strategy guide §4.2) |
| **Channel systems** | e-banking, mobile, APIs, corporate portals | The customer-visible face; SLA-critical; the source of most L1 tickets |
| **Middleware & integration** | Message queues, ESB/API gateways, file transfers | The silent dependency; failure here looks like failure everywhere else |

The SLA shape per family (⚠-structural, house-typical targets in the author's design voice — the *shape* is standard banking practice, the numbers are illustrative): payments and trading at **99.95% availability** with **5-minute on-call response** (✅ §4.3's critical-service target); channels at **99.9%** with 15-minute response and first-contact-resolution targets; core banking at **deadline-attainment** (the batch lands by the published cut-off, the ledger balances at close) rather than raw availability — a core that is "up" but out of balance is a failed service; reporting at **regulatory-deadline attainment** (the return reaches the regulator on time — the availability that matters is the submission, cross-ref §8.3's regulatory calendar); market data at **feed-latency attainment** (the feed is fresh within its SLA, not merely connected). The lesson: **availability % is the wrong SLA for half the banking estate** — the right measure is the deadline, the freshness, or the settlement outcome (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis; ✅ the nines-vs-business-outcome distinction is the zero-downtime guide's §2 framing).

### 8.3 The Regulatory Overlay — DORA and the 4-Hour Clock

The newest and sharpest regulatory driver on banking support is the EU's **Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)** — in force from January 2025 for financial entities operating in the EU — which mandates, per the banking-ITSM playbook literature (⚠-secondary digitalkimya; the *regulation* is real and widely documented, the specific mechanics below are as summarised in that secondary source): **ICT incident classification by criticality, reporting of major ICT incidents to regulators within 4 hours of classification, root-cause analysis within one month, and annual ICT resilience testing.** For a support organisation this converts the incident practice of §3.1 from an internal discipline into a **regulatory reporting pipeline**: detect → classify (is this a *major* ICT incident per the DORA template?) → the 4-hour clock starts → compliance drafts the regulator notification while the bridge still works the fix → the postmortem/RCA becomes the one-month submission. The support consequences are concrete and structural:

- The **severity ladder must align with the regulatory classification** — a SEV-1 in internal terms must map to the DORA major-incident criteria or the organisation will misreport (⚠-secondary, but the alignment requirement is the playbook's central point).
- The **incident timeline is a legal document** — the §3.1 scribe's minutes and the §3.2 postmortem feed the regulator (✅-structural: regulatory incident reporting requires contemporaneous records; the NETS guide's PCI-DSS section makes the same point about audit logging that cannot be rewritten, ✅ [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §7).
- The **ITSM tooling must support the reporting workflow** — the §5 platform's templates, timers and audit immutability are the delivery mechanism (⚠-secondary §8.3's tool-selection criteria: "Native DORA/ICT incident classification templates", "Audit trail immutability (regulatory requirement)").

**The flag:** the 4-hour/1-month specifics are verified only against the digitalkimya playbook (⚠-secondary) — the author's live verification this pass confirmed the *existence* and *shape* of DORA via multiple search results but did not extract the regulation's primary text. The mechanism (classification → clock → notification → RCA) is consistent across the secondary literature and should be treated as directionally verified, with the exact hours checked against the regulation before it is cited in a formal document.

**The banking support calendar (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis of §8's verified parts):** the year has a rhythm that overrides everything else. **Year-end and quarter-end** — freeze windows on all normal changes (the ledger close, the regulatory returns, the capital calculations; ✅ strategy guide §5's Factory posture); support staffing doubles on the critical services. **Month-end** — batch-heavy, reconciliation-heavy, the problem register's recurring villains surface. **The trading calendar** — market holidays, the open/close of the Asian and European sessions, the pre-open market-data feeds (cross-ref [../banking/capital_markets_architecture_guide.md](../banking/capital_markets_architecture_guide.md) for the trading-day shape). **The regulatory calendar** — DORA's annual resilience-testing cycle, MAS-style audit cycles, the notification drills (§8.3). A support framework that has not plotted its freeze windows and staffing peaks against this calendar is a framework that will be surprised by its own year.

## 9. The Worked Example — An Ops-Support Framework for a Cymbal Bank Estate

### 9.1 The Scenario — the Familiar Context

**The house:** a Cymbal Bank–style global wholesale bank — the CIB arm of a mutualist group, headquartered in Paris with a Singapore hub serving Asia-Pacific (✅ cross-ref [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) — the mutualized-in-house core, the CIB treasury/e-trading systems largely non-public ⚠, the Singapore branch as the APAC hub). The estate in scope, in the author's familiar shape: the **payments and settlement corridor** (SWIFT connectivity, the real-time rails, the ISO 20022 migration wave — cross-ref the NETS guide's message-world analysis, ✅ [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §2), the **treasury/e-trading platform** (Murex-class, ⚠ per the CA guide §3 — the vendor link is industry-known, not confirmed), the **market-data and reference-data layer**, the **risk and regulatory reporting stack**, and the **corporate e-banking channels**. Two locations (Paris + Singapore) cover the trading day; the payments rail runs 24/7; DORA applies to the EU-facing services; the regulators watch.

**The brief:** design the operational-support framework — the tiered model, the ITIL practices, the SRE overlays, the tooling, the cadence — for this estate. The design below is a **framework proposal in the author's solution-architect voice**, not a description of any actual Cymbal Bank organisation (⚠ — all internal Cymbal Bank support specifics are non-public; every element is assembled from this guide's verified parts). The support challenges the design must answer, stated before the design: (1) **two sites, one trading day** — the follow-the-sun handoff must not drop the open incident state (§4.2); (2) **a 24/7 rail with no maintenance window** — change control must live in the error budget and the deployment strategy, not in a calendar (§6.3, §3.3); (3) **a regulatory clock on the EU-facing services** — the incident practice must produce the DORA notification without slowing the fix (§8.3); (4) **a mixed estate** — the mutualized-in-house core and the vendor platforms (SWIFT, the treasury vendor, the market-data vendors) need a single incident practice across very different support contracts (§3.1, §5); and (5) **an audit culture** — every operational action must leave COBIT-readable evidence without adding so much process that the engineers flee (§7.3). The design below is scored against these five challenges in the lessons of §9.4.

### 9.2 The Design — Tiered + ITIL + SRE

**The tiered model (§4.1), composed for a two-site bank:**

- **L1 — the service desk, follow-the-sun (Singapore day + Paris day + a third-region night bridge for the 24/7 rails).** Logs everything, triages, classifies severity, fulfils catalogue requests (§3.4), resolves the scripted 70%. First-contact-resolution targets per service family: channels 70%, payments 50% (payments tickets escalate fast by design — the cost of a wrong L1 diagnosis on a SWIFT path outweighs the cost of escalation, ⚠-structural house-rule). **This is the toil floor (§6.2)** — the budget that automation must attack first: password resets and access requests are already self-service; the next automation targets are the recurring L1 scripts.
- **L2 — application and infrastructure specialists per domain, in both sites:** payments, treasury/trading, market data, risk/reporting, channels, plus the platform squads (middleware, network, databases, the OpenShift/cloud platform — cross-ref [openshift_workload_availability_guide.md](openshift_workload_availability_guide.md)). L2 owns incident investigation, vendor interface (SWIFT, the treasury vendor, the market-data vendors), and problem *diagnosis*.
- **L3 — the engineering teams on rotation:** the developers of each system take support shifts (the SRE/DevOps fusion, §6–§7). L3 owns code-level root cause, permanent fixes (as changes, §3.3), and the runbooks that make their fixes L2 procedures.

**The ITIL practices (§3), wired to the estate:**

- **Incident management:** the severity ladder is aligned to business impact *and* the DORA classification (§8.3): **SEV-1** = payments/trading service down or a DORA-major ICT incident — bridge within 15 minutes (⚠-structural target), incident commander + scribe + comms lead, the 4-hour regulatory clock running in parallel, executives and compliance on the bridge; **SEV-2** = major degradation or non-critical service down — same-day; **SEV-3/4** = normal queue. Runbooks first, investigation second; the runbook library is a first-class artefact maintained by L3 (cross-ref the zero-downtime guide's GameDay requirement — "no GameDay without a runbook", ✅ [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §7.3).
- **Problem management:** every SEV-1 and every third recurrence opens a problem record; RCA with 5-Whys + timeline reconstruction (§3.2); blameless postmortem within five business days; the error-budget policy's trigger is adopted as the standing rule — **>20% of a service's monthly error budget consumed by one incident ⇒ postmortem with at least one P0 action** (✅ §6.3). The known-error register feeds L1 scripts — the knowledge-flow loop of §4.1.
- **Change enablement:** standard/normal/emergency types; the **CAB meets weekly** with real risk data (CMDB blast-radius from the §5 platform, not opinions); **freeze windows** at quarter-end, year-end and regulatory-reporting dates; **change windows** for the batch estate on the batch schedule, and for the 24/7 rails — none: the rails' changes go through the **error-budget gate instead** (the SRE overlay, below), deployed via the zero-downtime strategies (canary → ramp → blue-green retreat, ✅ [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) §3) rather than in a "quiet window" that does not exist.
- **Service level management:** the SLA set per service family — payments and trading at 99.95% (≈21.6 minutes/month of allowed failure, ✅ zero-downtime §2.3 arithmetic), channels at 99.9%, reporting at deadline-attainment rather than availability (the batch must land by 06:00, not be "up" all night). MTTR is the support scoreboard; the review cadence is monthly per service, quarterly with the business.

**The SRE overlays (§6):**

- **Error budgets on the critical services** (payments, trading, market data): the budget is computed from the SLO; **launch freeze when spent** (except P0/security); the freeze has real teeth because the change practice §3.3 recognises it. The 70%-of-outages-from-changes statistic (✅ §6.3) is quoted at every CAB.
- **Toil budget on every support team:** ≤50% operational work, ≥50% engineering/automation; on-call ≤25% (✅ §4.3); the L1 automation backlog is a first-class engineering programme with its own SLO (⚠-structural house-rule): *every scripted L1 action is either automated, self-serviced, or justified in writing within a quarter*.
- **On-call:** primary + secondary rotations per critical service; the Singapore–Paris pair gives a natural follow-the-sun day (✅ §4.2 — no night shifts for the trading-day services); the 24/7 rails keep a night bridge in a third region with the two sites as each other's secondary fall-through (✅ §4.3).

**The tooling (§5):** the tier-1 core on **ServiceNow** — the CMDB is the blast-radius engine, the incident templates carry the DORA clock, the audit trail is immutable (⚠-secondary §8.3 criteria; the *choice* is the author's design decision, not a Cymbal Bank fact). **Jira Service Management** alongside it for the engineering-facing flows (incident↔code linkage for the L3 developers). **BMC Helix**-class monitoring correlation on the infrastructure estate if the mainframe exposure demands it (⚠-structural, house-decision). The rule: one system of record for the regulators and the SLA (ServiceNow), one system of truth for the engineers (JSM), and the monitoring layer feeding both. The design at a glance (⚠ by design — the author's framework proposal):

| Layer | Decision | Verified basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Tiers** | L0 self-service + L1 desk (follow-the-sun) + L2 domain specialists (both sites) + L3 engineers on rotation + L4 vendors governed | §4.1, §4.2, §3.4 |
| **Incident** | SEV-1..4 ladder aligned to DORA; bridge with commander/scribe/comms; runbooks first | §3.1, §8.3 |
| **Problem** | SEV-1 and third-recurrence → problem record; blameless postmortem ≤5 days; >20% budget trigger | §3.2, §6.3 |
| **Change** | Weekly CAB with CMDB risk data; freeze windows; error-budget gate on the 24/7 rails | §3.3, §6.3 |
| **SLA** | 99.95% payments/trading; 99.9% channels; deadline-attainment for reporting | §3.5, ✅ zero-downtime §2.3 |
| **SRE** | Error budgets + toil budget (≤50%) + on-call (≤25%) on critical services | §6.2, §6.3, §4.3 |
| **Tooling** | ServiceNow (record + DORA clock) + JSM (engineering truth) + monitoring layer feeding both | §5.2, §5.4, §5.1 |


### 9.3 The Operating Rhythm

- **The day:** Singapore opens the book; the trading-day services are covered by the follow-the-sun handoff to Paris (the overlap is the handover ritual — open incidents, pending changes, overnight alerts, §4.2); the rails' night bridge takes over the 24/7 services; the rotation rolls.
- **The week:** CAB on Wednesday with the freeze-window calendar and the error-budget burn report; problem review on Thursday (open postmortem actions with owners); Friday is *not* a change day on the trading corridor (⚠-structural, the classic bank rule — weekend changes on the rails only via the emergency path or the canary gate).
- **The quarter:** error-budget review per critical service; GameDay on one critical system (§7.3 — the escalation matrix and the runbooks rehearsed); DORA resilience-testing cycle on the EU-facing services (§8.3); the toil budget reviewed per team.
- **The incident:** the SEV-1 drill is the composition test — L1 detects (or the monitoring does), L2 diagnoses under the runbook, L3 fixes and rolls back under the deployment strategy, the incident commander runs the bridge, compliance runs the 4-hour clock, the scribe builds the legal timeline, and the postmortem that follows spends the error budget's attention: **the whole framework exists to make the 3 a.m. page boring** (⚠-structural, the author's synthesis). The implementation ramp — how the design lands without a big-bang (⚠ by design, the author's proposal): **days 1–30 — the baseline:** stand up the severity ladder, the escalation matrix and the bridge roles (§3.1, §4.4); instrument the critical services to the SLO standard and publish the first error-budget numbers (✅ zero-downtime §2.3 — "nothing migrates until 'normal' is measurable"); run the first postmortem drill. **days 31–60 — the practices:** wire the incident→problem→change flows in the ITSM platform (§5); stand up the weekly CAB with CMDB risk data (§3.3); publish the service catalogue and the request flows (§3.4); the toil register starts (every manual action logged against §6.2's attributes). **days 61–90 — the overlays:** the first error-budget review with teeth (a freeze, if the data says so); the first GameDay on a non-critical service (§7.3 — the escalation matrix rehearsed); the first follow-the-sun handover with the written checklist (§4.2). The ramp's principle: **the framework is adopted in the order it protects** — roles and ladders first, practices second, overlays third, because a toil budget means nothing before the incident practice exists to spend it.

### 9.4 The Lessons

1. **The tiered model is the load-bearing wall; the practices are the mortar; the SRE overlay is the engineering budget that keeps the wall from cracking.** L1/L2/L3 without incident and problem practices is a queue; with them, it is a system. (✅-structural synthesis of §3–§6.)
2. **The error budget outranks the CAB on the 24/7 rails.** Where no change window exists, the budget and the deployment strategy are the only change controls that respect the system — the CAB approves intent, the budget approves *permission* (✅ §6.3; ✅ zero-downtime §3).
3. **Regulatory clocks turn process into product.** Under DORA, the incident timeline, the classification and the RCA are deliverables, not paperwork — the support organisation's process quality is directly auditable (⚠-secondary §8.3, directionally verified).
4. **Follow-the-sun is a health policy as much as a coverage model** — day-shift quality at all hours, no night shifts where avoidable (✅ §4.2), and it happens to be the only model that fits a two-continent bank.
5. **Toil is the tax on every skipped automation** — and the 50/25 caps are the only way a support team stays an engineering team (✅ §6.2–§6.3).
6. **One platform philosophy per estate layer** — consolidator for the regulated core, accelerator for the engineers, operator for the infrastructure depth (§5.5's lesson, applied in §9.2).

## 10. The One-Page Summary — The Support That Never Sleeps

**Operational support is the human-and-process machinery that makes availability a property of the organisation — and it is built from four verified layers.** The **practices** (ITIL 4, 2019 — the SVS, the four dimensions, 34 practices ✅) define the work: incident management restores service fast, problem management kills the recurrence, change enablement lets change happen safely, request management makes the routine frictionless, service-level management keeps score, and the service desk is the front door. The **models** organise the people: tiered L1/L2/L3 routes work to the right expertise, follow-the-sun gives 24-hour day-shift quality without night shifts, on-call rotations put a named engineer on every page with 5/30-minute response targets, and the escalation matrix makes authority arrive with the severity. The **tooling** records it all: ServiceNow the consolidator for the regulated core, BMC Helix the operator for the infrastructure depth, Jira Service Management the accelerator for the engineers — one system of record, one system of truth, one monitoring layer feeding both. The **overlays** keep it alive: SRE's toil budget (manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, no enduring value, O(n) — the verified definition) caps the drudgery at 50% so the team stays an engineering team, and the error budget (1 − SLO, spent by outages *and* changes, freeze when exhausted) turns the oldest argument in IT — "can we ship?" — into an accounting fact. The **frameworks compose, they do not compete**: COBIT governs, ITIL manages services, TOGAF architects — and SRE and DevOps add the engineering attitude and the delivery speed that keep the process honest. The **banking context** is why it all matters: 24/7 rails with minutes-scale windows, the switch that never sleeps, quarter-end freezes, and a regulatory clock that turns the incident timeline into a legal document.

**The discipline in five numbers (all ✅ verified in this guide):** **34** — the ITIL 4 practices, the process backbone (14 general + 17 service + 3 technical, ✅ §2.4); **5 and 30** — the on-call response targets in minutes for critical and standard services (✅ §4.3); **50 / 25** — the percent caps on toil and on-call time that keep a support team an engineering team (✅ §6.2–§6.3); **43** — the minutes of allowed failure per month behind a 99.9% SLO, the budget every change and every outage must share (✅ zero-downtime §2.3); **4** — the regulatory hours to report a major ICT incident under DORA (⚠-secondary §8.3).

And the final word — **the support that never sleeps.** Here is the honest truth the whole discipline rests on: **no system is ever "up" by itself. The 99.95% in the SLA is not a property of the software — it is a property of the organisation: the L1 analyst who recognises the pattern at 2 a.m., the L2 specialist who follows the runbook while the bridge fills up, the L3 engineer who rolls back the canary in ninety seconds, the problem manager who turns the third recurrence into a permanent fix, the change board that said no this week because the budget was spent, the scribe who wrote down the timeline because the regulator would ask.** The switch that never sleeps is really the *support* that never sleeps — the rotation, the handover, the escalation matrix, the postmortem — the human machinery underneath the availability math, doing its work so invisibly that the only evidence it ever existed is that nothing happened. **That is the whole discipline: making the failure imperceptible by making the support relentless — and the support that never sleeps is the quiet engine behind every system that never goes down.**
