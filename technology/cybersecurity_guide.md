## 1. The Overview

### 1.1 The Short Answer

Cybersecurity is the discipline of **protecting information and the systems that process it from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction** — the practical, operational, organisational realisation of the information-security goals of **confidentiality, integrity and availability** (the CIA triad, ✅-canon) applied to *connected* systems. It is not one activity: it is a **program** with five permanent parts — a **threat landscape** you must know (who attacks, with what, and how the numbers move), a **security operation** you must run (the SOC, the SIEM, the SOAR, hunting, incident response), an **offensive function** you must rehearse (pentesting, red teaming, bug bounties), a **security organisation** you must govern (the CISO, the three lines of defence, awareness), and a **framework map** you must navigate (the NIST CSF, ISO 27001, CIS Controls, MITRE ATT&CK) to place the **domains** (network, endpoint, identity, data, application, cloud, OT) where the controls actually live. The rest of this guide maps all five parts and cross-refs the cluster's design-time deep-dives instead of re-deriving them.

### 1.2 The Definition — the Discipline

- **The discipline (verify).** The NIST Computer Security Resource Center's glossary definition of cybersecurity is *"the ability to protect or defend the use of cyberspace from cyber attacks"* ⚠-structural for the exact wording (the CSRC glossary was not extracted live this pass; the definition is uniformly quoted in the literature and consistent with the CNSSI 4009 family of definitions — flagged honestly). The information-security goal-set underneath it — **confidentiality, integrity, availability** (plus authenticity and non-repudiation in the extended formulations) — is ✅-canon (Saltzer & Schroeder's 1975 design-principles literature and every framework since; see the sibling [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §3 for the principles that operationalise them).
- **Cybersecurity vs information security vs IT security (⚠-structural).** The industry treats *information security* as the umbrella (the CIA triad over all information, physical and digital), *cybersecurity* as the connected-systems subset (the same goals over systems reachable through networks), and *IT security* as the operational subset (the controls run by the IT organisation). The boundaries are fuzzy and vendors blur them; the architect's reading is that they are the same discipline at different scopes, and this guide uses "cybersecurity" for the whole connected-systems program.
- **The CIA triad in the bank (⚠-structural framing; the goals are ✅-canon):** the triad is not abstract in a Cymbal Bank estate — **confidentiality** is the client master data and the market positions (a leak is a PDPA/GDPR/regulatory event), **integrity** is the payment instructions and the trade bookings (a tampered SWIFT message is the STRIDE-T nightmare), **availability** is the trading floor and the payment rails (a DORA-scale outage is the ENISA #1 threat realised). Every control in the domains map (§7) serves one of the three; the threat-modelling pass ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §2.2) is where the goals meet the design.
- **The discipline as a program.** The single most useful definition for a practitioner is operational rather than etymological: **cybersecurity is the program that (1) knows its threat landscape, (2) runs a detection-and-response operation, (3) rehearses the adversary via offence, (4) governs itself as an organisation, and (5) places its controls across the domains using the frameworks as the map.** Every section of this guide is one part of that sentence.

### 1.5 The Canon — Where the Discipline Comes From

- **The 1970s — the origin (✅-canon via the sibling's ledger):** the discipline's founding documents are the 1970s computer-security studies (the Willis Ware panel report on security in time-sharing systems, 1970; James P. Anderson's *Computer Security Technology Planning Study*, 1972) and Saltzer & Schroeder's 1975 design principles. - **The 2000s — the process era (⚠-structural):** ISO/IEC 27001 (first edition 2005), the Microsoft SDL, and the Gartner SIEM coinage (2005 — ✅ verified, §3.2) turned security into a managed process with an evidence trail.
- **The 2010s — the automation and the adversary era (⚠-structural):** the ATT&CK knowledge base (2013, ✅ via [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3), the APT1 disclosure (2013, §2.4), the Zero Trust coinage (Kindervag 2010, ✅ via [zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md](zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md)), and the bug-bounty platforms (2012, §4.4).

## 2. The Threat Landscape

### 2.1 The Landscape — What the Annual Reports Say

The threat-landscape canon is the annual report cycle of four organisations, and the architect's habit should be to read the *shifts* across editions, not the numbers of any single year. The current verified snapshot (this pass, August 2026):

- **ENISA Threat Landscape 2025** (1 October 2025, ✅ verified live): analyses **4,875 incidents** over 1 July 2024–30 June 2025; the headline is that **threat groups are reusing tools and techniques, introducing new attack models, exploiting vulnerabilities, and collaborating** against EU digital infrastructure. The prior edition (ETL 2024, 19 September 2024, ✅ verified live) identified **seven prime threats** — threats against **availability** topping the chart, followed by **ransomware** and **threats against data**, then malware, social engineering, supply-chain compromise and information manipulation/interference.
- **Verizon DBIR 2026** (the current edition, ✅ verified live on the landing page — top takeaways): **31% of breaches now start with software vulnerabilities**, beating stolen credentials as the top initial vector ("hackers shifting from tricking people to exploiting systems"); **48% of all breaches involve ransomware**, with payouts shrinking and businesses increasingly choosing not to pay; **15% of attack techniques are now bolstered by generative AI** (from spotting gaps to writing malware); and **mobile click rates are 40% higher**, making mobile the new favourite target as email phishing detection improves.
- **Mandiant M-Trends 2026** (✅ verified live): grounded in **500k+ hours of incident investigations in 2025**; the notable themes are **SaaS-integration abuse** for massive data theft and **cross-cloud lateral movement**. (Specific dwell-time figures ⚠ not verified this pass — see the ledger.)
- **CrowdStrike Global Threat Report 2026** (existence ✅ verified live; specific statistics ⚠ not verified this pass).

- **How to read the canon (this guide's synthesis):** the four reports are *complementary instruments*, not rivals — ENISA is the EU threat-centric view (threats × actors × mitigations), the DBIR is the breach-data view (what actually happened, drawn from thousands of real incidents), M-Trends is the IR-firm view (what the responders see inside the intrusions they are called to), and the CrowdStrike GTR is the telemetry view (what the sensors on millions of endpoints see). The architect's habit: read ENISA for the *threat menu*, the DBIR for the *initial-access and human-element reality*, M-Trends for the *dwell-time and detection truth*, and the GTR for the *intrusion-speed trend*. ⚠-structural for the characterisations (this guide's synthesis over the verified report facts above).

**The landscape reading for a bank:** the vectors are converging on the *initial-access* problem (vulnerable software, phishing, stolen credentials) and the *post-access* monetisation (ransomware, data exfiltration), with GenAI lowering the cost of both sides. The implications for the program appear in §3 (detection must assume a foothold) and §9.

### 2.2 The Threat Actors — Nation-States, Cybercriminals, Hacktivists, Insiders

The actor taxonomy used by the canon (ENISA, Verizon, Mandiant all classify along the same axes — motive, capability, persistence — ⚠-structural for the exact taxonomy wording, standard across the sources verified) has four families:

- **Nation-states and state-sponsored groups (⚠-structural):** the highest-capability actors — espionage, sabotage and (increasingly) hybrid warfare; motivated by geopolitics; resourced by governments; the APT class (§2.4) is their hallmark. For a bank the relevant subset is economic espionage and the targeting of financial-market infrastructure; the group naming conventions are Mandiant's APT series and Microsoft's weather-based taxonomy (see §2.4).
- **Cybercriminals (⚠-structural):** the financially motivated majority — ransomware operators and their RaaS affiliates, BEC scammers, carding rings, credential thieves. The DBIR's long-running finding is that the majority of breaches are financially motivated ⚠ (the precise annual percentage shifts; the 2026 edition's takeaways above show ransomware in 48% of breaches ✅).
- **Hacktivists (⚠-structural):** ideology-motivated groups whose tool of choice is disruption — DDoS and defacement — rather than theft; ENISA's sectorial reporting has repeatedly highlighted hacktivist DDoS against public administration ✅ (the ETL-adjacent press record, verified live). For a bank, hacktivism is primarily an *availability* threat (the ETL 2024 #1 prime threat).
- **Insiders (⚠-structural):** employees, contractors and third-party users acting negligently, maliciously, or under coercion. The insider is the actor the controls struggle with most, because the insider is already past the perimeter; the SG-personnel angle (clearance, vetting) is cross-ref'd in [singapore_security_clearance_guide.md](singapore_security_clearance_guide.md), and the *detection* problem it creates is the threat-hunting rationale of §3.4.

| Actor family | Motive | Capability / persistence | Typical TTPs | Bank exposure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nation-states** ⚠-structural | Geopolitics, espionage, economic advantage | Highest; persistent (the APT class) | Custom malware, living-off-the-land, long lateral movement | Market positions, payment rails, client data, market infrastructure |
| **Cybercriminals** ⚠-structural | Financial | High volume; fast | Ransomware/RaaS, BEC, credential stuffing, carding | Ransomware (48% of breaches ✅), payment fraud, extortion |
| **Hacktivists** ⚠-structural | Ideology | Low-moderate; episodic | DDoS, defacement, leaks | Availability of client-facing channels (the ETL #1 threat ✅) |
| **Insiders** ⚠-structural | Grudge, greed, error, coercion | Inside the perimeter by construction | Data exfiltration, sabotage, credential misuse, accidents | The clearance/vetting layer ([singapore_security_clearance_guide.md](singapore_security_clearance_guide.md)) and the maker-checker controls |

**The actor-axis lesson:** motive determines behaviour. Nation-states persist; criminals monetise fast; hacktivists disrupt; insiders betray or err. Detection content, response playbooks and tabletop scenarios should each be built per actor family — the same event (a credential misuse) means different things if the actor is a criminal affiliate (fast cash-out) or a nation-state (patient lateral movement).

### 2.3 The Malware and the Ransomware

- **The malware baseline (⚠-structural for the taxonomy; the categories are universal):** malware is the umbrella for the malicious-code families — viruses, worms, trojans, spyware, botnets, loaders, stealers, wipers. What changed in the 2020s is the *industrialisation*: commodity malware is sold as a service, and the interesting malware is bespoke (see the APT angle, §2.4).
- **Ransomware — the flagged figure (⚠ per the task framing — but with verified anchors):** the DBIR 2026 landing page (✅ verified live this pass) states **48% of all breaches involve ransomware**, with payouts shrinking as victims increasingly refuse to pay; ENISA's ETL 2024 (✅ verified live) ranked **ransomware the #2 prime threat** after availability attacks. The ransomware *economics* — double extortion (encryption + data-leak publication), Ransomware-as-a-Service affiliates, the shift to data-theft-only extortion ⚠-structural (the mechanics are widely documented but no single primary source was extracted this pass) — explain why the IR playbook (§3.5) and the offline-backup strategy are the non-negotiable core of any program.
- | Malware family | What it does | The control (cross-ref) |
|---|---|---|
| **Loaders / droppers** ⚠-structural | Deliver and install the next stage; the initial-access payload behind phishing | Email/web filtering, EDR, the phishing controls of §2.5 |
| **Stealers / infostealers** ⚠-structural | Harvest credentials and session tokens — the fuel for the 31%-vulnerability / credential-vector reality ✅ | Identity controls ([distributed_auth_guide.md](distributed_auth_guide.md)), MFA, session hygiene |
| **Ransomware / wipers** ⚠-structural | Encrypt (or destroy) data for extortion; 48% of breaches ✅ | Immutable backups, segmented recovery, the IR playbook of §3.5 |
| **Botnets** ⚠-structural | Remote-control fleets for DDoS and spam — the availability threat ✅ | Network filtering, the availability/resilience frame of [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) |
| **Bespoke APT malware** ⚠-structural | Custom, persistent implants — the nation-state class of §2.4 | Threat hunting (§3.4) and the purple loop (§3.6) |

**The bank reading:** a bank's ransomware exposure is not just encrypted disks — it is *regulatory* (DORA's operational-resilience obligations; cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1) and *systemic* (a payments outage is an availability event with contagion). The mitigation stack — immutable backups, the offline restore test, the segmented recovery environment — belongs to the resilience discipline of [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md) as much as to security.

### 2.4 The APTs — the Advanced Persistent Threats

- **The definition (✅ verified):** an **advanced persistent threat** is "a stealthy cybersecurity threat, typically manipulated by a state or state-sponsored group, which gains unauthorized access to a computer network and remains undetected for an extended period" — with the modern extension to non-state groups running large-scale targeted intrusions. Motivations are typically political or economic; the targets span every major sector including financial services.
- **The naming record (✅ verified at the structural level):** Mandiant's 2013 report **APT1: Exposing One of China's Cyber Espionage Units** is the canonical disclosure that made the APT class public and gave the industry its APT-numbering convention ⚠-structural for the exact report title/date being verified this pass — it is uniformly cited and the Mandiant APT-groups list is referenced in the verified record; Microsoft's 2023 shift to a **weather-based threat-actor naming taxonomy** (✅ verified via the documented record) retired the joke-prone naming style. The MITRE ATT&CK **groups** view (attack.mitre.org/groups, ✅ via [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3) is the working index of tracked groups and their techniques.
- **Dwell time (⚠ for current figures):** the M-Trends definition of dwell time — "the number of days an attacker is present in a victim environment before they are detected" — is ✅ verified (the threat-hunting record); the *values* are edition-specific and the current M-Trends 2026 figures were ⚠ not verified this pass (the verified FireEye-era record shows Americas 71 days, EMEA 177 days, APAC higher, for 2018 — historical, do not cite as current). The concept, not the number, is the point: **dwell time is the metric the SOC exists to compress** (§3).
- **The bank reading:** APTs targeting banks are not hypothetical — financial services is a named target sector in the verified record. The defense is the threat-modelled estate ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §9) plus the hunting operation (§3.4): the APT's defining property is *persistence*, and persistence is only defeated by *proactive search*.

### 2.5 The Phishing and the Social Engineering

- **The mechanism (⚠-structural for the taxonomy; the mechanics are universal):** phishing is the social-engineering family — deceptive messages (email, SMS/smishing, voice/vishing) that induce the target to reveal credentials, install malware, or authorise a fraudulent transfer. It is consistently the most common *human* initial-access vector in the DBIR record ⚠ (the precise annual share shifts; the 2026 verified takeaways show the attack moving to mobile — **40% higher click rates on mobile** — as email detection improves ✅).
- **Why it works (✅-canon psychology, ⚠-structural for the framing):** the attacker exploits trust, urgency and authority rather than a technical vulnerability; the control is therefore behavioural (awareness, §5.5) plus technical (email authentication — DMARC/SPF/DKIM, URL filtering, and the bank's favourite: **transaction confirmation on a separate channel**, which is why the Cymbal Bank digital layer's biometric login and push-based confirmations exist — cross-ref [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) §7.1).
- **The BEC variant (⚠-structural):** business-email compromise — the impersonation of executives or vendors to redirect payments — is the fraud-adjacent phishing family that hits the treasury function directly; the control is the maker-checker separation of duties of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §3.6 applied to payment instructions, not just to system changes.

### 2.6 The Supply-Chain Attacks

- **The class (✅-canon via the sibling's ledger):** supply-chain attacks compromise the software or the third party *between* the attacker and the victim — a poisoned dependency, a trojanised update, a breached vendor. The canonical modern case is SolarWinds (2020) ⚠-canon (universally documented; the sibling references the era in its §1.5 timeline), and the class is why ENISA's ETL 2024 lists supply-chain compromise among the seven prime threats ✅.
- **The machinery — cross-ref the SBOM (✅ via [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6.4–6.5):** the response to the class is the **software bill of materials** (the SBOM — the NTIA minimum elements; EO 14028's 2021 SBOM mandate, ✅-canon) plus **SLSA** (supply-chain levels for software artifacts — provenance and signed artifacts). The sibling owns the machinery; this guide's point is the *operational* consequence: a supply-chain event is an **incident-response event** (§3.5) that starts with "which of our systems consumed the poisoned artifact?" — a question only answerable if the SBOMs exist and are searchable.
- **The canonical cases (⚠-canon — universally documented, not re-verified this pass):** **SolarWinds (2020)** — the trojanised Orion platform updates, the supply chain as a *platform-scale* compromise; **log4j (2021)** — the single vulnerable dependency (Log4Shell) found in millions of applications, the supply chain as a *dependency-scale* compromise. The two are the paired lessons of the class: the first says *verify the vendor artifact* (provenance, SLSA), the second says *know every dependency you ship* (the SBOM — and SCA, cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §5.5).
- **The bank reading:** for a Cymbal Bank the third-party estate *is* the attack surface — core-banking vendors, the payments switch, the SaaS layer, the cloud providers, the AI platforms (cross-ref [beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md](beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md) and [enterprise_ai_gateway_guide.md](enterprise_ai_gateway_guide.md)); DORA regulates the Register of Information accordingly (cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1 and [../banking/regtech_guide.md](../banking/regtech_guide.md)).

## 3. The Security Operations

### 3.1 The SOC — the Security Operations Centre

- **The definition (✅ verified):** a **security operations centre (SOC)** is the dedicated function where the enterprise's information systems — websites, applications, databases, data centres and servers, networks, desktops and other endpoints — are **monitored, assessed and defended**. SOC analysts monitor the network, investigate potential incidents, and take the steps necessary to remediate attacks. A SOC is **people, processes and technology, under a governance and compliance framework**; it can be in-house or outsourced to a managed security service provider (MSSP) — the common shape for smaller organisations.
- **The SOC's job in one sentence:** turn the raw noise of the estate (logs, alerts, telemetry) into a *decided* state — "this is benign", "this is a real incident", "this is contained" — fast enough to matter. The SOC's quality metrics are the ones that compress dwell time: **time to detect (TTD) and time to respond (TTR)** ⚠-structural (the metric names are universal practice).
- **The SOC team shape (⚠-structural):** tiered analysts — Tier 1 triage (alert review, initial classification), Tier 2 investigation (deep-dive, containment actions), Tier 3 hunting/forensics (the proactive layer of §3.4), plus the shift leads and the IR coordinator who runs the lifecycle of §3.5. In a bank, the SOC also carries the *reporting* obligation — the incident timeline that will be shown to the regulator (DORA's evidence chain, cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1).
- **The SOC's tool spine (✅-structural):** the SIEM (§3.2) as the aggregation and correlation layer, the SOAR (§3.3) as the automation and case-management layer, EDR on the endpoints (the endpoint domain, §7.3), and the threat-intelligence feed as the external context. The "next-gen" market direction — SIEM/SOAR consolidation, agentic automation (visible in the verified CrowdStrike 2026 platform line-up: "Next-Gen SIEM", "Agentic SOAR") — changes the tooling, not the function.
- **The maturity ladder (⚠-structural):** SOC maturity is commonly described in five steps — *Level 1* (reactive, ad-hoc alerting), *Level 2* (standardised processes, defined triage), *Level 3* (measurable, monitored metrics — TTD/TTR trended), *Level 4* (proactive — hunting and purple engagements scheduled), *Level 5* (predictive — intel-led, automation-assisted decisioning). The named maturity frameworks (the SOC-CMM family ⚠) differ in labels, not in direction; the architect's reading is that maturity = **the shift from reactive alert consumption to proactive, metric-driven operation** — the same direction as the threat-hunting argument of §3.4.

### 3.2 The SIEM — the Detection Engine

- **The definition (✅ verified):** **security information and event management (SIEM)** combines security information management (SIM — the long-term log collection, storage and reporting) with security event management (SEM — the real-time alerting) to enable **real-time analysis of security alerts generated by applications and network hardware**. The term was **coined by Gartner analysts Mark Nicolett and Amrit Williams in 2005** ✅; NIST's definition is an application that gathers security data from information-system components and presents it as actionable information through a single interface ✅. SIEMs are **central to SOCs**, used to detect, investigate and respond to incidents.
- **The mechanics (⚠-structural, standard practice):** the pipeline is collect (logs from endpoints, network devices, cloud, identity), normalise (a common schema), correlate (rules, analytics, UEBA-style behavioural baselines), alert, and retain (the compliance record — PCI-DSS and HIPAA are named in the verified record as SIEM-reporting drivers). The failure mode every SOC knows is the **alert flood** — correlation rules that produce noise; the control is tuning against the false-positive rate.
- **The neighbours (⚠-structural):** the SIEM must be distinguished from its cousins — **log management** (collection and retention without correlation; the SIEM's raw-material layer), **XDR** (extended detection and response — endpoint/network/cloud telemetry correlated at the vendor platform, increasingly the SIEM's *feeder* rather than its rival), and **UEBA** (user-and-entity behavioural analytics — the anomaly layer that some SIEMs embed). The architect's reading: the SIEM is the *enterprise* correlation plane; XDR is the *vendor-ecosystem* correlation plane; a mature estate runs both with the XDR alerts feeding the SIEM's case management.
- **The bank reading:** in a Cymbal Bank estate the SIEM's hardest integration is the *legacy core* — AS/400-era platforms and the mutualized group systems (cross-ref [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) §2) that lack modern telemetry; the SIEM programme is as much a *log-source onboarding* project as a tooling project. Cross-ref the audit-as-code angle where the evidence trail is generated ([audit_as_code_guide.md](audit_as_code_guide.md)).

### 3.3 The SOAR — the Response Engine

- **The definition (⚠-structural — flagged):** **security orchestration, automation and response (SOAR)** is the Gartner-coined category (2017 ⚠-structural for the exact coinage record — Gartner's own pages were paywalled/blocked this pass; the category's existence and meaning are uniformly documented) for the layer that **orchestrates** playbooks across tools (ticketing, EDR, firewall, identity), **automates** the repeatable response steps (enrichment, blocking, containment), and **manages** the incident case from detection to closure.
- **What it actually buys (⚠-structural):** the SOAR's value is not magic automation — it is *consistency and throughput*: the playbook encodes the senior analyst's decision logic so the Tier-1 analyst executes it identically at 3 a.m. The automation targets the low-judgement, high-volume steps (IP blocking, hash quarantine, account disable) while keeping the high-judgement steps (containment strategy, forensic preservation, regulator notification) human.
- **The convergence (⚠-structural):** the 2024–2026 vendor record (verified in the CrowdStrike 2026 platform line-up) shows SIEM and SOAR consolidating into "next-gen SIEM / agentic SOAR" platforms — the architectural point stands: detection and response are one workflow, and the tools should be bought as one workflow, not as separate silos.

### 3.4 The Threat Hunting — the Proactive Layer

- **The definition (✅ verified):** **threat hunting is the process of proactively searching for threats against computer systems** — in contrast to the traditional reactive tools (firewalls, IDS, SIEM) that investigate evidence *after* a warning. Hunting assumes the detection rules missed something and goes looking: hypothesis-driven, TTP-based (the tactics, techniques and procedures of ATT&CK — cross-ref [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3), and IoC-curious (indicators of compromise).
- **The dwell-time connection (✅ verified):** the M-Trends definition of dwell time — days an attacker is present before detection — is the hunting metric: **hunting is the activity that finds the attacker the rules missed**, and the dwell-time trend is the scoreboard. The maturity framing — the Team Cymru **Threat Hunting Maturity Model** (✅ via the verified record) — runs from initial (adhoc, IoC-driven) through to operational (hypothesis-driven, TTP-based, integrated with the intel cycle). The SANS threat-hunting survey series ⚠ (sans.org blocked extraction this pass) is the standard practitioner reference but could not be verified.
- **The bank reading:** for a bank, hunting is where the APT defense (§2.4) actually lives. The hunt priorities should be drawn from the bank's own threat model ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §9) — the legacy-core trust zone, the payments flow, the privileged-identity layer — and from the ATT&CK techniques the bank's sector is actually being hit with (per the intel feed).

### 3.5 The Incident-Response Lifecycle — the NIST SP 800-61

- **The standard (✅ verified live this pass):** **NIST SP 800-61, *Computer Security Incident Handling Guide*** — Rev. 2, published August 2012, defines the canonical **four-phase incident-response lifecycle**:
  1. **Preparation** — the IR plan, the team, the tools, the playbooks, the training — built *before* the incident.
  2. **Detection and Analysis** — event triage, alert validation, scope assessment (what systems, what data, what impact); the phase where most incidents are lost (misclassified or unanalysed).
  3. **Containment, Eradication and Recovery** — stop the spread (containment strategy chosen for the incident class — the disconnect-and-preserve trade-off), remove the adversary (eradication), and restore business operation (recovery, with the evidence preserved throughout).
  4. **Post-Incident Activity** — the lessons-learned review, the metrics, the control improvements that close the loop back into Preparation.
- **The 2025 update (✅ verified live this pass — the important freshness point):** SP 800-61 Rev. 2 was **withdrawn on 3 April 2025 and superseded by SP 800-61 Rev. 3** (April 2025), *Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management: A CSF 2.0 Community Profile* — which reframes IR as an activity embedded in the **NIST CSF 2.0** risk-management cycle rather than a standalone handling procedure. The four-phase lifecycle remains the industry's mental model (Rev. 3's authors include Scarfone, who co-authored Rev. 2); a program should run the lifecycle while reporting through the CSF 2.0 lens (§6.2).
- **The playbook anatomy (⚠-structural):** the IR *playbook* is the executable form of the lifecycle — one playbook per incident class: **phishing/credential theft** (triage the report, reset the credentials, check for reuse, review the mailbox rules), **ransomware** (contain the spread, preserve the evidence, invoke the backup-restore path, assess the data-theft exposure, decide the regulator notification), **BEC/payment fraud** (stop the payment, maker-checker escalation, counterparty notification), **supply-chain compromise** (identify the poisoned artifact, find every consumer via the SBOM, cross-ref §2.6), **insider data exfiltration** (preserve, isolate, HR/Legal join). Each playbook names the trigger, the steps, the owners, the decision points and the evidence to preserve — and is exercised in tabletop form before it is needed in anger.
- **The IR metrics (⚠-structural, the standard operational names):** the lifecycle is only manageable if it is measured — **time to detect (TTD)**, **time to contain (TTC)**, **time to eradicate**, **time to recover (TTR)**, the **false-positive rate** of the detection layer, and the **post-incident action closure rate** (the metric that proves the lessons-learned loop actually lands). The SOC's scoreboard (§3.1) and the dwell-time trend (§3.4) are the same numbers from two directions: detection latency is dwell time viewed from inside.
- **The bank reading:** a bank's IR programme is regulated: DORA's TLPT (threat-led penetration testing, Article 26) and the operational-resilience obligations mean the IR playbook is a *supervisory artefact* — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1. The incident timeline (detection timestamp, containment timestamp, regulator notification timestamp) is the evidence the examiners will ask for; the SOC of §3.1 should therefore log the lifecycle phases as first-class data, not as narrative.

### 3.6 The Purple Teaming — the Feedback Loop

- **The definition (⚠-structural — flagged):** **purple teaming** is the practice of running the red team (offence, §4) and the blue team (defence — the SOC, detection and response) *together* — the red team's attacks and the blue team's detections are exercised in the same engagement so that gaps in *detection coverage* are found and fixed, not just gaps in *security controls*. The term's precise origin is not attributable to a single verified primary source this pass ⚠; the practice is documented across the industry record (the MITRE ATT&CK Evaluations, which publish emulation-driven detection results for vendor products, are the public institutionalisation of the idea ⚠-structural).
- **Why purple beats red-only (⚠-structural):** a classic red-team report says "we got in and you didn't see us" — valuable, but it does not say *which detection would have caught us, and why it didn't fire*. A purple engagement produces exactly that: the detection gap list, mapped to the ATT&CK techniques used, which feeds the SIEM correlation rules and the hunting hypotheses (§3.4). The purple team is the mechanism that turns the offence budget into detection improvements.
- **The cluster tie-in:** the ML-security variant of the same loop is the red-teaming machinery of [adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md](adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md) §11.3 (the attack-type/success-criteria table) and §12.3 (the tooling: Garak, PyRIT, Counterfit); the AI-era extension is the continuous-validation paradigm of [beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md](beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md).

**The ops decision map — build, buy or outsource (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the operations question is never "SIEM or not" but *who runs the operation*. The maturity path is usually: MSSP-only → hybrid (in-house core + MSSP nights/weekends) → in-house-plus (in-house core + MSSP for overflow and niche skills). The bank design of §9.3 picks the hybrid.

## 4. The Offensive Security

### 4.1 Why Attack Yourself — the Offence-Defence Loop

Offensive security is the discipline's deliberate asymmetry: the defender must be right every time, the attacker only once — so the defence must *rehearse the attacker*. The three practices form a spectrum of scope, depth and permission: **pentesting** (scope-limited, scheduled, system-level), **red teaming** (objective-based, adversarial, organisation-level), and **bug bounties** (continuous, crowd-sourced, surface-level). All three produce the same thing — evidence of what an adversary could do — and all three feed the same consumers: the threat register ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §2.8), the detection backlog (§3.6), and the remediation backlog ([security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §5).

### 4.2 The Pentesting — the OWASP WSTG and the PTES

- **The practice (✅-canon via the sibling, cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §5.6):** penetration testing is the human, adversarial, end-to-end assessment — trained testers attempt to compromise the application as real attackers would (recon, exploitation, privilege escalation, chaining findings). The sibling owns the *placement* (automated scanning continuously; human pentesting on a schedule tied to risk — annually for regulated bank applications and before major client-facing releases); this guide owns the *method canon*.
- **The OWASP WSTG (✅ verified live):** the **Web Security Testing Guide** is the OWASP flagship testing resource — a framework of best practices for testing web applications and web services. **v4.2 is the current stable release (December 2020); v5.0 is in development.** Its working unit is the test case, identified as `WSTG-<category>-<number>` (e.g., `WSTG-INFO-02`), organised by phase (information gathering, configuration, identity, authN/authZ, session, input validation, error handling, crypto, business logic, client-side, API testing) ⚠-structural for the full category list (the format and versioning are verified; the category enumeration is the guide's own structure).
- **The PTES (✅ verified live):** the **Penetration Testing Execution Standard** organises the *engagement* itself into **seven phases**: (1) **Pre-engagement Interactions** (scope, rules of engagement, legal authorisation), (2) **Intelligence Gathering**, (3) **Threat Modeling**, (4) **Vulnerability Analysis**, (5) **Exploitation**, (6) **Post Exploitation**, (7) **Reporting** — plus the PTES Technical Guidelines. It is at v1.0 ("road tested" per the standard's own description) with a v2.0 in the works ✅.
- **The regulatory floor (✅ via the sibling):** for banks the pentest is not optional — DORA's **TLPT** (threat-led penetration testing, Article 26) and the MAS TRM regime (now **FSM-N05** ⚠, per the sibling's §8.4 flag) both require adversary-style testing for the highest-risk entities; cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1 and §5.6.
- **WSTG + PTES together:** WSTG says *what to test* (the web-app test catalogue); PTES says *how to run the engagement* (the process). A mature program uses both: PTES for the engagement governance, WSTG for the web-app test content — and adds the OWASP ASVS for the verification levels (cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §4.3).
- **The engagement colours (⚠-structural):** pentests are classed by the knowledge given to the tester — **black-box** (nothing but the target's public face — the closest to an external attacker), **grey-box** (authenticated access, architecture context — the highest-value-per-dollar for most applications, because it tests the *authorised-user* attack surface that black-box misses), **white-box** (full source and architecture access — the deepest, slowest, most expensive). The bank's regulated applications should run grey-box as the standard and white-box for the crown jewels; the colour is chosen at PTES phase 1 (pre-engagement), never after.
- **The WSTG phase map (⚠-structural for the full enumeration, the phase structure per the WSTG):** the guide's test cases are organised as a journey — information gathering, configuration and deployment management testing, identity management, authentication, authorisation, session management, input validation, error handling, cryptography, business logic, client-side testing, and API testing — each phase a checklist of `WSTG-<cat>-<n>` cases that the tester executes, evidences and reports.

### 4.3 The Red Teaming — the Objective-Based Adversary

- **The definition (⚠-structural — flagged):** red teaming is the *objective-based* adversarial exercise — a team (internal or external) given a realistic goal ("obtain the SWIFT credentials", "exfiltrate the client master data") and the attacker's freedom of method (social engineering, physical, technical), operating against the live organisation with a defined timebox and rules of engagement. Where a pentest answers "is system X vulnerable?", a red team answers "could a real adversary achieve objective Y against us?" — it tests *people, process and physical* as much as technology, and it tests the *detection* (§3) by design.
- **The cluster tie-in (✅ via the sibling):** the ML/GenAI variant of red teaming is fully owned by [adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md](adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md) §11.3 (the attack-type × success-criteria table — evasion, extraction, poisoning, prompt injection) and §12.3 (the tooling); [llm_development_risks_security_guide.md](llm_development_risks_security_guide.md) owns the LLM-specific threat surface. The security-by-design sibling frames the *frequency* (annual or per risk rating, as a GameDay) in its §5.6.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** for a Cymbal Bank the red-team objectives should be drawn from the crown jewels — the payments estate, the client-confidential data, the market-position systems — and the after-action report feeds the purple-team loop (§3.6): the red team's techniques map to the detection gaps, which map to the SIEM rules and the hunting hypotheses.
- **Choosing the engagement (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the three practices of §4 are chosen by *question*, not by fashion — **pentest** when the question is "is this system/application exploitable?" (system-scoped, scheduled, regulated — §4.2); **red team** when the question is "could an adversary achieve this objective against us as we actually operate?" (organisation-scoped, episodic, secret-keeping part of the design — §4.3); **bug bounty** when the question is "what does a continuous crowd find on our exposed surfaces?" (surface-scoped, continuous, permission-managed — §4.4). The mature program runs all three on different cadences — and the purple-team loop (§3.6) is what makes each of them improve the others.

### 4.4 The Bug Bounties — the Crowd as the Testing Layer

- **The model (✅ verified):** a **bug bounty program** is a deal by which individuals receive recognition and compensation for reporting bugs, especially security vulnerabilities; without a financial reward it is a **vulnerability disclosure program**. It is a form of **crowdsourced penetration testing** that grants permission for unaffiliated researchers (white hats) to find and report vulnerabilities. The scale increased dramatically in the late 2010s; large organisations run their own programs (Microsoft, Google, Facebook, Mozilla, the EU, the US federal government) or buy them through platforms.
- **The platforms (✅ verified):** **HackerOne** — one of the first to embrace crowdsourced security; pioneering bug bounty *and coordinated vulnerability disclosure*; its network had paid **$230M+ in bounties by December 2022**, with customers including the US Department of Defense and Department of State, General Motors, GitHub, Goldman Sachs, Google and Microsoft. **Bugcrowd** — founded **2012** (Sydney; HQ San Francisco); a crowdsourced security platform running bug-bounty programs plus **Penetration Testing as a Service (PTaaS)** and **attack-surface management**; it acquired the ASM firm Informer (2024) and the AI-driven security firm Mayhem Security (2025). The positioning: HackerOne leans on the hacker-community/coordination heritage; Bugcrowd leans on the managed-service/PTaaS breadth ⚠-structural for the contrast phrasing (the verified record supports the facts, the one-line positioning is this guide's synthesis).
- **The program tiers (⚠-structural):** the model scales by permission and reward — the **disclosure program** (no reward; a channel and a promise to fix — the entry tier that every organisation can run), the **private bounty** (invitation-only researchers; controlled scope; the workhorse tier for banks), the **public bounty** (the whole crowd; the highest volume and the highest noise), and the **managed program** (platform-operated, with triage and validation as a service — Bugcrowd's PTaaS shape). The tier chosen is a function of the asset's risk and the organisation's triage capacity, not of ambition.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** banks have historically been cautious with bug bounties because the *attack surface* (client data, payments) is regulated and the *permission boundary* is legally sensitive — scope definition is the whole game. The modern shape is a **restricted-scope program** (the API layer, the mobile apps, the public web estate — never the core) run on a platform with a disclosed rules of engagement, feeding the same remediation backlog as the pentests. The Cymbal Bank digital-layer surfaces (cross-ref [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) §7) are the natural candidates.

## 5. The Security Organization

### 5.1 The Organisation Problem — Security Is a Staff Function

Security is a **staff function**: it protects the business's assets but does not itself produce revenue, which means its budget, its authority and its culture are permanently contested. The organisation design has to solve three problems: **authority** (who can say no to a risky release?), **accountability** (who owns a risk when it materialises?), and **independence** (who audits the people who run the controls?). The three answers are the CISO (§5.2), the governance structure (§5.3), and the three lines of defence (§5.4). ⚠-structural for the framing; each element is verified below.

**The operating model (⚠-structural):** beyond the CISO's office, the security *function* must choose how it sits relative to delivery — **centralised** (one security organisation serving all of IT; consistent standards, but slow and distant from the teams), **embedded** (security engineers inside each delivery team; fast and context-rich, but inconsistent and easily captured by the team's agenda), or **hybrid** (a central core of policy, architecture and the SOC, with embedded security engineers in the high-risk delivery teams — the shape that most large banks converge on). The hybrid is the §9 design's choice: the SOC and the GRC are central; the markets-estate delivery teams carry embedded security engineers; the group platforms are served by the central core. The failure mode of the centralised-only shape is the security *gate* that slows everything; the failure mode of the embedded-only shape is the standards that quietly diverge.

### 5.2 The CISO — the Role

- **The definition (✅ verified):** the **chief information security officer (CISO)** is the senior-level executive responsible for **establishing and maintaining the enterprise vision, strategy and program** to ensure information assets and technologies are adequately protected — directing staff in identifying, developing, implementing and maintaining the processes that reduce information and IT risk; managing information-security technologies; implementing policies; and **ensuring compliance with regulatory frameworks** (GDPR, PCI DSS, FISMA, ISO/IEC 27001 certification). The CISO may report to the CIO or directly to the CEO.
- **The history (⚠-structural for exact dates):** the role is conventionally traced to **Steve Katz at Citibank** (1994–1995) — the first named corporate CISO — per the CSO Online 30-years retrospective ✅-referenced in the verified record. The role's centre of gravity has since moved from technical guardian (2000s) through compliance officer (2010s) to **board-facing risk executive** (2020s, where the DORA/NIS2 era makes the CISO's regulatory reporting a board-level matter) — ⚠-structural for the periodisation.
- **The reporting structure (⚠-structural):** the CISO's reporting line is the organisation's most consequential security decision — reporting to the **CIO** (the common shape; security is close to the technology it protects but can be subordinated to delivery agendas), reporting to the **CEO** or the board directly (the independence-maximising shape; security gets a seat but loses the IT-adjacent operating leverage), or the **dual/reporting-matrix** shapes in between (security reporting to risk or COO, with a dotted line to the board). The trend in the DORA/NIS2 era is toward the board-facing shape ⚠-structural — the regulator wants the security function to be *able to say no in public*. The CISO's own span typically covers security operations (§3), the security architecture and engineering function, the GRC (governance/risk/compliance) function, and the awareness program (§5.5) — ⚠-structural for the span, which varies by organisation.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** in a Cymbal Bank group the CISO role is *layered* — the group IT subsidiary runs the mutualized security platforms (cross-ref [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) §2.2) while each entity (retail regional banks, LCL, Cymbal Bank, Amundi, CA Assurances) carries its own accountable security function reporting into the group's governance. The architect's takeaway: **security authority follows the data and the regulation, not the org chart** — the CISO for the markets estate owns the Cymbal Bank surfaces; the group CISO owns the shared platforms.

### 5.3 The Security Governance — Policy, Risk Appetite, Committees

- **The shape (⚠-structural — the governance mechanics are universal practice):** security governance is the decision machinery around the security program: the **policy hierarchy** (board-level security policy → standards → procedures → baselines), the **risk appetite statement** (what residual risk the organisation will accept — expressed per asset class and per threat family), the **risk-acceptance process** (who may accept a risk and with what sign-off), and the **committees** (a security steering committee under the board/risk committee, with the CISO as the reporting line).
- **The working artifacts (⚠-structural, this guide's enumeration):** the governance machinery lives in five documents — the **security policy** (the board-level intent, one page, signed), the **standards** (the mandatory rules — cryptography, access, logging, vendor), the **procedures** (the how-to — onboarding, incident response, access request), the **baselines** (the technical minimums — hardening guides, image templates), and the **risk-acceptance record** (the signed decisions that deviate from the above). The CISO's monthly report to the steering committee is the sixth artifact: the risk register digest, the metrics (TTD/TTR, dwell-time trend, awareness click-rate), the open acceptances, and the incidents of the period.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** in a bank the governance machinery is *supervised*: the risk taxonomy, the incident-reporting thresholds and the outsourcing register are regulated (cross-ref [../banking/regtech_guide.md](../banking/regtech_guide.md) for the compliance machinery, and the risk-management framing of the bank-series guides). The practical artefact is the **risk register**: every finding from the threat register ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §2.8), the pentests (§4.2), the bug bounties (§4.4) and the audits (§5.4) lands in it, with an owner, a due date and an acceptance decision. The program-investment framing (cost of the program vs cost of the breach) is owned by [../management/business_case_development_guide.md](../management/business_case_development_guide.md).

### 5.4 The Three Lines of Defence

- **The model (✅-structural via the IIA record):** the **three lines of defence** is the Institute of Internal Auditors' model for how assurance should be organised (the 2013 IIA position paper *The Three Lines of Defense in Effective Risk Management and Control*, updated in 2020 as the **Three Lines Model** ⚠-structural for the exact update naming — the IIA's role as the internal-audit standard setter is ✅ verified):
  - **First line — operational management:** the business and IT functions that *own* and *manage* risk day-to-day — including the security controls embedded in delivery (the DevSecOps machinery of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6).
  - **Second line — oversight functions:** risk management, compliance and the security function itself — the CISO's organisation *sets* the security policy and *monitors* the first line's execution. (The security function sits in the second line — a fact that matters: the SOC's operators are second-line monitors, not first-line owners, of the business processes they watch.)
  - **Third line — independent assurance:** internal audit, which evaluates the effectiveness of both lines above, independent of management.
- **The value (⚠-structural):** the model prevents the two classic failures — the first line claiming "security's job" while shipping unhardened systems, and the second line *owning* risk it should only be monitoring. The bank's supervisory expectations (maker-checker, segregation, audit independence) map directly onto the lines.

| Line | Who | Security role | Owns |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First** | Operational management — delivery, IT operations, business units | Run the embedded controls (DevSecOps, access reviews, maker-checker, the §5.5 behaviours) | Risk ownership and management |
| **Second** | Risk, compliance, the security function | Set the policy, monitor the first line, run the SOC, operate the risk register | The oversight framework — not the risks themselves |
| **Third** | Internal audit | Independent evaluation of both lines; the assurance opinion to the board | Independence and the audit opinion |

### 5.5 The Security Awareness — the Human Layer

- **The problem (⚠-structural — flagged):** the human is the attack surface that the phishing section (§2.5) describes; the DBIR's long-running finding is that a large share of breaches involve a **non-malicious human element** (error, credential mishandling, falling for pretext) ⚠ — the precise annual figure was not verified this pass; the *direction* is consistent across the DBIR editions and is the reason awareness is a control, not a poster campaign.
- **The program (⚠-structural):** security awareness is the systematic reduction of human risk: **baseline training** (the policy hierarchy translated into behaviour), **phishing simulations** (measured, remediated, repeat — the metric is click-rate reduction over time), **role-based depth** (the payments team learns BEC and the maker-checker rule; developers learn secure coding — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §4), and **culture** (the reporting channel that makes "I clicked" a safe thing to say — which is also the SOC's best detection feed).
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** for a bank, awareness is supervised: regulators expect evidence of an awareness program and its metrics; the SG personnel angle (clearance, vetting, the insider risk framing) is cross-ref'd in [singapore_security_clearance_guide.md](singapore_security_clearance_guide.md).

## 6. The Frameworks Map

### 6.1 Why Frameworks — the Map, Not the Territory

Frameworks are the discipline's shared language: they let an organisation *say* its posture, *compare* it year over year, and *evidence* it to auditors and regulators. The map of this section covers the four that matter most — the **NIST CSF 2.0** (the strategic map), **ISO/IEC 27001:2022** (the certifiable management system), **CIS Controls v8** (the tactical control list), and **MITRE ATT&CK** (the adversary-behaviour language). The *details* of the first two are verified in the sibling's ledger and are cross-ref'd here, **not re-derived**; the CIS Controls and ATT&CK get their verified specifics inline.

**How the four fit together (this guide's synthesis):** the frameworks are not alternatives — they are *different axes of the same cube*. **CSF 2.0** is the outcome axis: it names the results (Govern/Identify/Protect/Detect/Respond/Recover) without prescribing the means, which makes it the board-level map and the program-level structure. **ISO 27001:2022** is the *management-system* axis: it wraps any control set in the certifiable machinery (policy, risk assessment, internal audit, management review) and contributes the 93-control Annex A as a checklist. **CIS Controls v8** is the *prioritisation* axis: it orders the controls by attack prevalence (offense informs defense) and phases them via the implementation groups, which makes it the engineer's roadmap. **ATT&CK** is the *adversary* axis: it names the behaviours the other three are defending against, which makes it the common language for detection and testing. The mature program runs all four simultaneously — the failure mode to avoid is treating any one of them as *the* framework.

### 6.2 The NIST CSF 2.0

- **The framework (✅ via the sibling's verified ledger — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.6):** the **NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0**, released **26 February 2024**, organises cybersecurity risk management into **six functions — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover** — with **Govern** (governance, strategy, risk appetite, supply-chain oversight) the new first-class function that the 1.1 version lacked. It is outcome-based, not control-prescriptive: it says *what* outcomes to achieve, not *which* products to buy.
- **Use it for (⚠-structural framing, the framework's own design intent):** the executive conversation and the program map — "where are we weak?" answered in six words (Govern/Identify/Protect/Detect/Respond/Recover), plus the CSF 2.0 community profiles (like the SP 800-61 Rev. 3 IR profile of §3.5) that specialise it per domain. Cross-ref the sibling's §8.6 for the verified detail.

### 6.3 The ISO/IEC 27001:2022

- **The framework (✅ via the sibling's verified ledger — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.6):** **ISO/IEC 27001:2022** is the certifiable **information security management system (ISMS)** standard — the management-system machinery (context, leadership, planning, operation, performance evaluation) around a control set: **93 Annex A controls in four themes — organisational, people, physical and technological**. Certification is the third-party-verified evidence that the management system exists and operates.
- **Use it for (⚠-structural framing):** the *certifiable backbone* of the program — the ISMS is the container into which the CSF 2.0 outcomes, the CIS Controls and the operational practices all pour; a bank's SOC/ISO 27001 certificates and the PCI-DSS validation scope are the audit evidence the counterparties ask for (cross-ref the NETS security estate, §8).

### 6.4 The CIS Controls v8

- **The framework (✅ verified live this pass):** the **CIS Critical Security Controls v8** are "a prioritized set of CIS Safeguards to defend against the most prevalent cyber attacks" — **18 controls** (inventory of assets, data protection, secure configuration, access control, continuous vulnerability management, audit-log management, email/web protection, malware defences, etc. ⚠-structural for the full enumeration), organised into **three implementation groups (IG1/IG2/IG3)** so an organisation can start with the essential hygiene (IG1 — the "cyber hygiene" set) and mature toward IG3. The design principles are verified: **offense informs defense** (selected from attacker-behaviour data), **focus** (only the critical actions), **feasible**, **measurable**, and **align** (mapped to NIST, CSA, SAFECode, MITRE ATT&CK, OWASP). The current release is **v8.1** ✅ — with explicit emphasis on hybrid-cloud estates and supply-chain security.
- **The 18 controls at a glance (⚠-structural for the full enumeration — the verified record confirms 18; the titles below are the standard naming):** 1 inventory of enterprise assets, 2 inventory of software assets, 3 data protection, 4 secure configuration, 5 account management, 6 access control management, 7 continuous vulnerability management, 8 audit log management, 9 email and web browser protections, 10 malware defences, 11 data recovery, 12 network infrastructure management, 13 network monitoring and defence, 14 security awareness skills training, 15 service-provider management, 16 application software security, 17 incident response management, 18 penetration testing. The grouping is notable: the list runs from the *hygiene* (inventory, config, access, logs) through the *defence* (malware, recovery, monitoring) to the *programme* (awareness, IR, pentesting) — which is why IG1 (the first six in practice) is the honest starting point.
- **Use it for:** the *tactical* gap analysis and the IG-based roadmap — "have we done IG1?" is the fastest honest posture question an organisation can ask itself.

### 6.5 The MITRE ATT&CK

- **The framework (✅ via the sibling's verified ledger — cross-ref [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3):** **MITRE ATT&CK** is the adversary-behaviour knowledge base — **tactics** (the *why*: initial access, execution, persistence, privilege escalation, defence evasion, credential access, discovery, lateral movement, collection, exfiltration, command-and-control, impact), **techniques and sub-techniques** (the *how*), mapped to **groups** (the *who* — attack.mitre.org/groups) and **software** (the *what*). The sibling owns the library mechanics (CAPEC/ATT&CK/CWE comparison, the enterprise/ICS/mobile matrices); this guide's point is its *role in the program*: ATT&CK is the shared vocabulary that connects the threat model (§2), the detection content (§3 — SIEM rules and hunting hypotheses are written in ATT&CK technique IDs), and the offence (§4 — red-team emulation plans are ATT&CK-sourced).
- **ATT&CK in one walkthrough (⚠-structural for the technique IDs — the example chain is illustrative, built from the verified framework structure):** a realistic bank intrusion as ATT&CK would name it — **Initial Access** T1566 (phishing) → **Execution** T1059 (scripting) → **Persistence** T1136 (create account) → **Credential Access** T1078 (valid accounts) → **Defence Evasion** T1070 (indicator removal) → **Lateral Movement** T1021 (remote services) → **Collection** T1005 (data from local system) → **Exfiltration** T1048 (over alternative protocol). The power of the language: the purple-team report (§3.6) can say "we covered T1566 and T1078; we had no detection for T1021" — and the hunting calendar (§3.4) can be scheduled against the *gaps*, not against guesswork.
- **Use it for:** detection engineering, hunting hypotheses, red-team emulation, and the purple-team gap reports (§3.6) — the program's *adversary language*.

## 7. The Domains Map

### 7.1 The Seven Domains — Where the Controls Live

The frameworks say *what outcomes*; the **domains** are *where the controls live*. The map below covers the seven domains of a modern estate — network, endpoint, identity, data, application, cloud, OT — each with its focus and its cluster cross-ref. The dedup contract applies here harder than anywhere: the cluster owns the deep material for most domains, so this section maps and cross-refs rather than re-derives.

**Reading the domain map (this guide's synthesis):** three readings matter. *First*, the domains are **not silos — they are layers of the same attack path**: the phishing email (§2.5) lands on the endpoint (§7.3), steals the identity (§7.4), moves over the network (§7.2) to the application (§7.6) in the cloud (§7.7), to exfiltrate the data (§7.5) — and the OT estate (§7.8) is where a bank's physical-world value (the ATM, the payment hardware) lives. A control placed in one domain compresses the path at that layer. *Second*, the domains map onto the zero-trust pillars (identity, device, network, application, data — cross-ref [zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md](zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md) §4): the pillars are the *architecture's* names for the same places. *Third*, the cluster's ownership is uneven — identity, application and cloud are deeply owned; endpoint and OT are not — and that unevenness is the map of where the next commissions should go.

### 7.2 The Network Domain

- **The focus (⚠-structural framing):** segmentation, filtering, encryption in transit, and the boundary itself — the domain that the Zero Trust paradigm deliberately de-emphasises as a *trust* boundary while keeping it as a *defence-in-depth* layer (the sibling's §3.3 defence-in-depth tie-in).
- **The cross-ref (✅ via the sibling):** the network domain is **fully owned by [zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md](zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md)** — NIST SP 800-207 (August 2020, the seven tenets), the CISA pillars (identity/device/network/application/data), the SDP/microsegmentation architecture patterns, and the banking remote-access transformation. The resilience angle (the network as an availability dependency) is [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md).
- **The key controls (⚠-structural):** network segmentation and microsegmentation (the blast-radius control), firewalls and the default-deny at the trust boundaries, TLS/encryption in transit everywhere, DNS and egress filtering (the C2 choke point — the ATT&CK command-and-control tactic of §6.5), and network detection (the NDR layer feeding the SIEM of §3.2).

### 7.3 The Endpoint Domain

- **The focus (⚠-structural):** the laptops, servers, mobile devices and workstations where the user meets the data — endpoint protection (EPP), endpoint detection and response (EDR), device compliance, and the device pillar of zero trust. The domain is where the 2020s remote/hybrid estate moved the perimeter, and where the agent-era paradigm ([beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md](beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md)) is rewriting the rules — the device is becoming the policy-enforcement point.
- **The cross-ref:** the device pillar is in [zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md](zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md) §4 (the CISA model); the EDR-to-SOC feed is §3 of this guide; the mobile surface is the DBIR 2026 finding of §2.1. No sibling deep-dives EDR tooling specifically ⚠ — flagged as a possible future commission.
- **The key controls (⚠-structural):** EDR on every endpoint (the SOC's richest telemetry source), centralised patch and configuration management (the 31%-vulnerability vector of §2.1 is an unpatched-endpoint problem), disk encryption and device lockout (the laptop-loss reality), application allow-listing for the high-risk tier, and the device-compliance check at every access decision (the zero-trust device pillar).

### 7.4 The Identity Domain

- **The focus (✅ via the sibling):** authentication (who you are) and authorisation (what you may do) — the domain the Zero Trust paradigm made the *new perimeter* (the identity pillar), and the one where the modern bank's control effort concentrates (privileged access, service identity, the human-insider risk of §2.2).
- **The cross-ref:** **fully owned by [distributed_auth_guide.md](distributed_auth_guide.md)** — passwords → SAML → OAuth2/OIDC → JWT → mTLS → WebAuthn, RBAC/ABAC/ReBAC, policy engines, SPIFFE/SPIRE service identity, SSO, PSD2/SCA. The SG personnel angle (who gets access, at what clearance) is [singapore_security_clearance_guide.md](singapore_security_clearance_guide.md).

### 7.5 The Data Domain

- **The focus (⚠-structural):** classification, encryption at rest, data-loss prevention, retention, and the privacy/regulatory frame (PDPA/GDPR) — the domain where the *value* the attackers want actually lives (the "threats against data" prime threat of §2.1).
- **The cross-ref:** the data-governance machinery (classification, lineage, quality, retention) is [data_governance_guide.md](data_governance_guide.md); the cloud data-plane controls are [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §7; the AI-data angle is [beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md](beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md) and [llm_development_risks_security_guide.md](llm_development_risks_security_guide.md).
- **The key controls (⚠-structural):** data classification as the prerequisite for everything else (unclassified data cannot be protected proportionately), encryption at rest with the key hierarchy in HSMs (the §8.1 lesson), DLP on the egress channels (the exfiltration tactic of §6.5), tokenisation/pseudonymisation for the sensitive fields in the non-production environments (cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §3), and retention-and-deletion discipline (the GDPR/PDPA right-to-be-forgotten as an operational schedule, cross-ref [data_governance_guide.md](data_governance_guide.md)).

### 7.6 The Application Domain

- **The focus (✅ via the siblings):** the software itself — secure coding, testing, the API surface, the LLM/ML surfaces — the domain where the bank's own build-vs-buy choices create or remove risk.
- **The cross-ref:** **fully owned by [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md)** (the secure SDLC — OWASP Top 10, ASVS, SAST/DAST/IAST/SCA, DevSecOps, SBOM/SLSA) plus [api_governance_guide.md](api_governance_guide.md) (the API security layer) and [llm_development_risks_security_guide.md](llm_development_risks_security_guide.md) / [adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md](adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md) (the GenAI/ML surfaces). The *testing* of this domain is §4 of this guide.
- **The key controls (⚠-structural):** the pipeline gates of the DevSecOps machinery (SAST/DAST/SCA in CI — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6), the SBOM with the dependency-verification loop (§2.6), the API gateway as the enforcement point (cross-ref [api_governance_guide.md](api_governance_guide.md)), and the LLM/ML controls of the GenAI surfaces (prompt-injection defence, model red-teaming — cross-ref [llm_development_risks_security_guide.md](llm_development_risks_security_guide.md) and [adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md](adversarial_ml_attacks_guide.md)).

### 7.7 The Cloud Domain

- **The cross-ref:** the shared-responsibility model, posture management (CSPM), workload protection (CWPP/CNAPP), and the identity-permission layer (the misconfiguration and over-permission failures the DBIR-era breach record keeps surfacing).
- **The key controls (⚠-structural):** the shared-responsibility inventory (who owns what per workload — the sibling's §7.1), CSPM for the misconfiguration drift (the top cloud vector), workload protection for the runtime layer, **cloud IAM hygiene** (the over-permissioned service account is the cloud's version of the privileged identity — cross-ref [distributed_auth_guide.md](distributed_auth_guide.md) for the authorisation models), and the cloud-egress DLP of the data domain.
- **The cross-ref:** **fully owned by [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §7** (shared responsibility, CSPM, CWPP/CNAPP) and the cloud-data-plane angle of [zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md](zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md); the agent-era cloud angle is [beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md](beyond_zero_enterprise_security_guide.md).

### 7.8 The OT Domain

- **The focus (⚠-structural — flagged):** operational technology — the industrial control systems, ATMs, payment hardware, market-data feeds and physical-infrastructure controllers that a bank (unlike most software companies) actually owns. OT security is ICS/SCADA-style: availability-first (the ETL #1 prime threat of §2.1), air-gapped-or-segmented, patched conservatively, and increasingly attacked as the IT/OT boundary converges.
- **The cross-ref (✅-adjacent):** the closest cluster material is the payments-hardware security estate of [../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/nets_software_systems_guide.md) §6 (HSM-protected PIN/crypto, PCI-DSS scope) and the market-infrastructure angle of the bank series. OT as a full discipline is **not deep-dived anywhere in the cluster** ⚠ — flagged as the most significant future commission.
- **The key controls (⚠-structural):** the network segmentation that keeps OT off the IT domain (the air-gap-or-segment rule), the HSM key hierarchy as the physical root of trust (the §8.1 lesson), vendor-signed firmware and change control (the OT supply chain), availability-first patching (tested in a replica before the production controller), and the physical access control that the logical controls cannot replace.

## 9. The Worked Example — a Security Program for a Cymbal Bank

### 9.1 The Scenario — the Familiar Context

The scenario is the house itself, reduced to its security-relevant shape: a **Cymbal Bank in Singapore** — the CIB markets estate (e-trading, treasury, connectivity) sitting beside the group's mutualized platforms (shared identity, payments, the digital layer), regulated by MAS, DORA-relevant for the EU parent, with a payments envelope in the HSM/PCI-DSS class and a compliance machinery consuming security evidence. The brief: **design the security program** — not a product purchase, not a single framework adoption, but the whole program: the org (§9.2), the SOC (§9.3), and the frameworks (§9.4). The cross-refs are the cluster guides; the verified facts are the §1–§8 material.

**The crown jewels (⚠-structural, the scenario's own synthesis):** every design decision below follows from naming what the program exists to protect — (1) **the payment rails** (SWIFT-class messaging, the settlement flows — tampering here is the STRIDE T/R worst case, cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §2.2); (2) **the client-confidential data** (the master data of the markets estate — disclosure is a PDPA/GDPR event, not a bug); (3) **the market positions and the trading systems** (confidentiality and integrity priced in money); (4) **the privileged identities** (the trading-floor and operations accounts — the key to everything above, cross-ref [distributed_auth_guide.md](distributed_auth_guide.md)); and (5) **the key estate itself** (the HSMs and the PKI of the payments envelope — the NETS model of §8.1: offline capability bought with key-based trust). The red-team objectives of §9.3 and the CIS IG3 scope of §9.4 are drawn from this list.

### 9.2 The Design — the Org

- **The CISO structure (§5.2):** a **federated CISO model** matching the estates — a group-level CISO owning the shared platforms and the group standards, and an entity-level CISO (or head of security) for the CIB markets estate with authority over the trading-floor surfaces, reporting to the group CISO's governance. Authority follows data and regulation: the payments envelope's security reports into the group; the markets estate's security reports into the CIB line with a dotted line to the group.
- **The governance (§5.3):** one group security policy hierarchy (policy → standards → procedures → baselines), one risk-appetite statement expressed per estate (the markets estate accepts low-latency-constrained controls; the payments envelope accepts none), one security steering committee (group CISO + entity CISOs + risk + audit + a board-level security champion — the DORA-era board reporting line), and **one risk register** as the spine: every finding from threat modelling ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §2.8), pentests (§4.2), bug bounties (§4.4) and audits lands there with an owner and a date.
- **The three lines (§5.4):** first line — the delivery and operations teams run the controls embedded in their processes (the DevSecOps machinery of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6); second line — the security function sets policy and monitors (the SOC is here); third line — internal audit assesses the whole, independent. The SG-personnel layer (clearance, vetting) overlays the first line via [singapore_security_clearance_guide.md](singapore_security_clearance_guide.md).
- **The awareness (§5.5):** a measured program — baseline training for all staff, phishing simulations with click-rate trending, role-based depth (payments teams learn BEC and maker-checker; developers learn secure coding), and the safe-reporting culture that feeds the SOC.

### 9.3 The Design — the SOC

- **The operating model (§3.1):** an in-house core (the CIB markets estate justifies it — the crown jewels are local) with MSSP augmentation for the follow-the-sun overnight shift; tiered analysts (T1 triage, T2 investigation, T3 hunt/forensics); the IR coordinator running the §3.5 lifecycle; and the regulator-facing incident timeline as a first-class data product.
- **The tool spine (§3.2–3.3):** one SIEM platform ingesting the estate — with the *log-source onboarding* project as the real work (the AS/400-era legacy core, the mutualized group platforms, the cloud data plane, the identity layer); one SOAR layer encoding the playbooks (phishing → credential reset; ransomware → containment + the offline-backup restore path; BEC → payment-stop + maker-checker escalation); EDR across the endpoints; the threat-intel feed tagging events with ATT&CK technique IDs (§6.5).
- **The hunting (§3.4):** a hunting calendar drawn from the bank's threat model — the legacy-core trust zone, the payments flow, the privileged-identity layer — written as ATT&CK hypotheses ("who in our estate could be running technique T1078-valid-accounts right now?"), with the dwell-time trend as the scoreboard.
- **The purple loop (§3.6):** quarterly purple engagements — the red team (§4.3) attacks the crown jewels (obtain the SWIFT credentials; exfiltrate client master data), and the output is the *detection-gap list* feeding the SIEM rules and the hunting backlog, not just the vulnerability list.
- **The DORA overlay (§3.5):** the IR programme is exercised as a supervised artefact — TLPT-scale testing per [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1, tabletop exercises with the regulator-reporting timeline, and the evidence chain preserved from detection to notification.

### 9.4 The Design — the Frameworks

- **The NIST CSF 2.0 as the map (§6.2):** the program's annual posture statement is written in the six functions — Govern (the steering committee, risk appetite, supply-chain oversight), Identify (the asset inventory and the threat model), Protect (the domains of §7), Detect (the SOC of §9.3), Respond (the IR lifecycle), Recover (the resilience machinery of [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md)). The board conversation is six words; the detail is the profiles.
- **ISO/IEC 27001:2022 as the certifiable backbone (§6.3):** one ISMS across the group entities, 93 Annex A controls in four themes, certification as the third-party evidence the counterparties and the group audit ask for; the payments envelope additionally holds the PCI-DSS scope and the HSM key-management certification record (the NETS model, §8.1).
- **The CIS Controls v8 as the tactical roadmap (§6.4):** the estate runs the IG1 hygiene set first (inventory, secure configuration, access control, audit logs, malware defences — the honest "have we done IG1?" question), maturing toward IG3 per estate risk; the v8.1 emphasis on hybrid-cloud and supply-chain maps onto the Cymbal Bank reality (the mutualized platforms + cloud + the vendor estate).
- **MITRE ATT&CK as the adversary language (§6.5):** every SIEM rule, hunting hypothesis, red-team emulation and purple-team gap report is tagged with technique IDs — so the whole program speaks one adversary language and the framework map of §6 becomes the program's operating system: **CSF 2.0 says where we are; ISO 27001 proves the management system; CIS v8 orders the work; ATT&CK names the enemy.**

| **The SOC** | In-house core + MSSP follow-the-sun; tiered analysts; SIEM + SOAR + EDR + intel; hunting calendar; quarterly purple engagements | TTD/TTR trends, the dwell-time trend, the detection-gap list, the regulator-ready incident timeline |
| **The frameworks** | CSF 2.0 as the map; ISO 27001:2022 as the ISMS backbone; CIS v8 (IG1 first) as the roadmap; ATT&CK as the language | The annual six-function posture, the certification, the IG1/IG2/IG3 gap scores, the ATT&CK-tagged detection content |
| **The estates** | Group platforms / markets / payments / compliance each threat-modelled and weighted (§7 domain map) | Per-estate threat registers (cross-ref [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §2.8) |

### 9.4c The Build Sequence — Years 1 to 3

- **Year 1 — the foundation (⚠-structural sequencing, this guide's synthesis):** stand up the org (§9.2) — the federated CISO structure, the steering committee, the risk register; run the **CIS IG1 hygiene set** across the estate (inventory, secure configuration, access control, audit logs); onboard the *priority log sources* into the SIEM (the identity layer, the payments flow, the cloud data plane — the legacy core last, because it is the hardest); write and tabletop the top-five IR playbooks (§3.5); launch the awareness baseline and the first phishing simulation. The year's test: the first purple engagement, against one crown jewel.
- **Year 2 — the operation (⚠-structural):** the SOC reaches Level 3–4 maturity (metric-driven, hunting scheduled — §3.1); the SOAR playbooks go live for the high-volume classes (phishing, credential theft); the ISO 27001 ISMS is scoped and certified for the highest-priority entity; the bug-bounty program opens on the restricted scope (the API/mobile/web surfaces — §4.4); the DORA/TLPT-scale testing cycle starts for the regulated applications.
- **Year 3 — the program (⚠-structural):** the hunting calendar and the purple engagements run quarterly as routine; the posture statement is reported annually in the six CSF functions with year-over-year deltas; the CIS maturity reaches IG2 per estate, IG3 for the crown jewels; the awareness metrics (click-rate trend, report-rate trend) are reviewed by the steering committee; the lessons of §9.5 are re-read against the dwell-time trend. The program is no longer a project — it is the bank's security operating system.

### 9.5 The Lessons

1. **The program is the unit, not the product.** The bank's security cannot be bought — it is the org (§9.2), the SOC (§9.3) and the frameworks (§9.4) operating as one loop: threat model → detection → purple-test → remediation → re-model.
2. **The estates are the reality.** The federation (group platforms / markets / payments / compliance) means one governance, many threat models — the failure mode is a group standard that fits no estate and an estate control that ignores the group.
3. **Detection is where the money goes.** The APT threat (§2.4) and the 48%-ransomware reality (§2.3) both say the same thing: the dwell-time compressor (SOC + hunting + purple) is the highest-ROI investment; the §3 machinery is the heart of the program.
4. **The frameworks are the language, not the work.** CSF 2.0, ISO 27001, CIS v8 and ATT&CK exist so the program can say itself, evidence itself and order itself — none of them is the program.
5. **The human is a control.** The awareness program (§5.5) and the maker-checker discipline ([security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §3.6) are not soft skills — they are the controls that stop BEC and the insider (the DBIR human-element record, §2.5). The investment case for the whole program is owned by [../management/business_case_development_guide.md](../management/business_case_development_guide.md): the cost of the program vs the cost of the breach.

## 10. The One-Page Summary — the Final Word: the Security Program

**Cybersecurity is a program, not a product.** It has five permanent parts. **First, the threat landscape** — the actors (nation-states who persist, criminals who monetise, hacktivists who disrupt, insiders who err or betray), the malware/ransomware economics (48% of breaches now involve ransomware — Verizon DBIR 2026 ✅), the APTs (stealthy, state-backed, defined by dwell time), the phishing (moving to mobile — 40% higher click rates ✅), and the supply chain (the SBOM/SLSA answer to the poisoned-artifact class — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6.4–6.5). **Second, the security operation** — the SOC (people, process, technology), the SIEM (the 2005 Gartner-coined detection engine), the SOAR (the response engine), threat hunting (the proactive layer that compresses dwell time), the NIST SP 800-61 incident-response lifecycle (four phases — and now Rev. 3, the 2025 CSF 2.0 profile ✅), and purple teaming (the loop that turns offence into detection). **Third, the offensive function** — pentesting (WSTG v4.2 + the PTES seven phases), red teaming (objective-based, feeding the purple loop), bug bounties (HackerOne's $230M+ paid, Bugcrowd's PTaaS — the crowd as a testing layer). **Fourth, the security organisation** — the CISO (the role, traced to Citibank's Steve Katz), governance (policy hierarchy, risk appetite, one risk register), the three lines of defence (operate, oversee, assure), and awareness (the human as a measured control). **Fifth, the framework map and the domains** — NIST CSF 2.0 (Govern/Identify/Protect/Detect/Respond/Recover) as the strategic map, ISO 27001:2022 as the certifiable ISMS, CIS Controls v8 as the tactical roadmap, MITRE ATT&CK as the adversary language, all placed across the seven domains (network, endpoint, identity, data, application, cloud, OT — each cross-ref'd to the cluster's deep-dives). In a Cymbal Bank the program is a federation of estates — the mutualized group platforms, the markets estate, the payments envelope, the compliance machinery — joined by one governance and one framework map, with the SOC as its heart and the risk register as its spine. The design-time material — the secure SDLC, the threat-modelling methodology, the zero-trust architecture, the agent-era paradigm — is not re-derived here; it is the cluster's, and the program stands on it. The final word: **the security program** — known landscape, running operation, rehearsed offence, governed organisation, mapped frameworks — is the discipline itself.

## 11. The Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Cybersecurity** | The ability to protect or defend the use of cyberspace from cyber attacks (NIST glossary ⚠-structural for the exact wording) — the connected-systems realisation of the confidentiality/integrity/availability goals |
| **Threat landscape** | The current state of who attacks, how, and with what frequency — as measured by the annual canon (ENISA ETL, Verizon DBIR, Mandiant M-Trends, CrowdStrike GTR) |
| **Threat actor** | An entity that poses a threat — nation-states, cybercriminals, hacktivists, insiders — classified by motive, capability and persistence |
| **APT** | Advanced persistent threat — a stealthy, typically state-sponsored actor that gains access and remains undetected for an extended period ✅ |
| **Ransomware** | Malware that encrypts (and/or exfiltrates) the victim's data for extortion; in 48% of breaches per the DBIR 2026 ✅ |
| **Malware** | Malicious software — viruses, worms, trojans, spyware, botnets, loaders, stealers — the commodity and bespoke attack payload families |
| **Phishing** | Social engineering via deceptive messages to induce credential theft, malware installation or fraudulent authorisation |
| **Supply chain** | Attacks that compromise software or a third party between attacker and victim; the SBOM/SLSA machinery is the defence (cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6.4–6.5) |
| **SOC** | Security operations centre — the function that monitors, assesses and defends the enterprise's systems; people, process and technology ✅ |
| **Security operations centre** | See SOC |
| **SIEM** | Security information and event management — SIM+SEM; real-time analysis of security alerts; the term coined by Gartner's Nicolett and Williams in 2005 ✅ |
| **SOAR** | Security orchestration, automation and response — the playbook/automation/case-management layer ⚠-structural (Gartner 2017 category) |
| **Threat hunting** | Proactively searching for threats — hypothesis-driven, TTP-based, versus reactive alert-driven detection ✅ |
| **Incident response** | The handling of a security incident through the NIST SP 800-61 lifecycle: preparation, detection & analysis, containment/eradication/recovery, post-incident activity ✅ |
| **NIST 800-61** | The NIST incident-handling guide — Rev. 2 (Aug 2012), withdrawn April 2025, superseded by Rev. 3 (Apr 2025, the CSF 2.0 community profile) ✅ |
| **Purple team** | The joint practice of red (offence) and blue (defence) teams to find detection gaps, not just vulnerabilities ⚠-structural |
| **Pentesting** | Penetration testing — the human, adversarial, scope-limited assessment of systems ✅ |
| **WSTG** | The OWASP Web Security Testing Guide — v4.2 stable (Dec 2020), v5.0 in development; the web-app test catalogue ✅ |
| **PTES** | The Penetration Testing Execution Standard — seven phases from pre-engagement to reporting ✅ |
| **Red team** | The objective-based adversarial exercise testing people, process, physical and detection against realistic goals ⚠-structural |
| **Bug bounty** | Crowdsourced vulnerability reporting for compensation — platform-facilitated (HackerOne, Bugcrowd) or in-house ✅ |
| **CISO** | Chief information security officer — the senior executive owning the enterprise security vision, strategy and program ✅ |
| **Governance** | The decision machinery — policy hierarchy, risk appetite, acceptances, committees ⚠-structural |
| **Three lines of defence** | The IIA assurance model — operations own risk, oversight functions monitor, internal audit assures ✅-structural |
| **Awareness** | The systematic reduction of human risk — training, phishing simulation, role-based depth, reporting culture ⚠-structural |
| **NIST CSF** | The NIST Cybersecurity Framework — 2.0 (26 Feb 2024), six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover ✅ via sibling |
| **ISO 27001** | ISO/IEC 27001:2022 — the certifiable ISMS standard, 93 Annex A controls in four themes ✅ via sibling |
| **CIS Controls** | The CIS Critical Security Controls v8/v8.1 — 18 prioritized controls, three implementation groups ✅ |
| **MITRE ATT&CK** | The adversary-behaviour knowledge base — tactics, techniques, groups, software ✅ via sibling |
| **Network security** | The domain of segmentation, filtering, encryption in transit — cross-ref [zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md](zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md) |
| **Endpoint** | The device domain — EPP/EDR, device compliance, the zero-trust device pillar |
| **Identity** | The authentication/authorisation domain — the new perimeter — cross-ref [distributed_auth_guide.md](distributed_auth_guide.md) |
| **Data security** | The domain of classification, encryption at rest, DLP and privacy — cross-ref [data_governance_guide.md](data_governance_guide.md) |
| **Application security** | The software domain — secure coding, testing, APIs, LLM/ML — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) |
| **Cloud security** | The shared-responsibility domain — CSPM, CWPP/CNAPP — cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §7 |
| **OT** | Operational technology — ICS/SCADA-style availability-first security; the cluster's biggest un-owned domain ⚠ |
| **Security program** | The whole — known landscape, running operation, rehearsed offence, governed organisation, mapped frameworks — the final word of this guide |
