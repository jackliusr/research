# The Art and Science of Ice Breaking

**A comprehensive guide to initiating conversations, building rapport, and creating meaningful connections in professional and social settings**

*Part of the Career Development Series — for architects, engineers, and banking professionals*

---

## 1. What Ice Breaking Is (And Isn't)

### Definition

Ice breaking is the act of initiating a conversation with someone you do not know or do not know well, with the goal of establishing rapport, finding common ground, and creating a foundation for further interaction. It is the opening move — the first few exchanges that determine whether a conversation takes off or fizzles out. It is not about being the most entertaining person in the room; it is about making the other person feel comfortable enough to engage.

### What It Is

- **A bridge** — connecting two strangers by finding the smallest point of shared reality
- **A safety signal** — communicating "I am approachable, I mean no harm, I am interested in you"
- **An invitation** — offering the other person an easy way to engage without social risk
- **A diagnostic** — testing the waters to see if the other person is open to conversation
- **A foundation** — the first layer of what could become a professional relationship or collaboration

### What It Is Not

- **Not a performance** — you do not need to be the funniest or most charismatic person. The goal is to connect, not impress.
- **Not an interrogation** — rapid-fire questions without sharing anything yourself makes people feel interviewed, not welcomed.
- **Not a transaction** — walking up to someone only to hand them a business card and leave is not ice breaking; it is prospecting.
- **Not a pitch** — your product or agenda can wait. The ice breaker is about them, not you.

### Ice Breaking vs. Small Talk vs. Deep Connection

| Stage | What It Is | Goal | Duration |
|-------|-----------|------|----------|
| **Ice Breaking** | The opening move | Establish safety and willingness to engage | 30s – 2min |
| **Small Talk** | Surface-level polite conversation | Find common ground and build rhythm | 2 – 10min |
| **Deep Connection** | Meaningful exchange of ideas or experiences | Create trust and lasting relationship | Ongoing |

Ice breaking is the bridge *into* small talk. Small talk is the scaffolding for deeper conversation. Skip to depth and you feel invasive; stay in the shallows and you feel hollow. Move through these stages deliberately.

## 2. Why Ice Breaking Matters

### Professional Impact

Opportunities — jobs, deals, partnerships, mentorship — often come from people you have just met. A good ice breaker creates possibility space for those opportunities to emerge. A bad one closes it before it opens. First impressions set the tone for every future interaction. People remember how you made them feel far longer than what you said. Networks are built one conversation at a time, and the cost of a missed connection is invisible but real.

### Career Advancement

Networking effectiveness is one of the strongest predictors of career mobility. Meetings start more productively when the first few minutes establish rapport rather than awkward silence. Leadership presence includes approachability — the best ideas are worthless if no one feels comfortable sharing them. Visibility matters: people cannot advocate for you if they do not know you, and they cannot know you if you never speak to them.

### The Banking / Architect Context

As a solution architect at a major bank, ice-breaking situations arise constantly: meeting new stakeholders on each project, cross-team collaboration across business and technology, client-facing interactions for requirements and presentations, industry events and conferences, interviewing (both sides), and vendor/partner meetings. Every one of these starts with an ice breaker.

### The Stakes

| Scenario | Bad Ice Breaker | Good Ice Breaker |
|----------|----------------|------------------|
| New stakeholder meeting | Awkwardness colours whole engagement | Immediate rapport accelerates decisions |
| Networking event | Leave having spoken to no one meaningful | Leave with 2-3 genuine connections + follow-ups |
| Interview | Never recover from a flustered start | Establish conversational rhythm that showcases your best self |
| Cross-team kickoff | Teams stay siloed, communication stays transactional | Teams collaborate openly, problems surface early |

A bad ice breaker creates awkwardness that lasts the entire interaction. A good one can turn a stranger into a valuable connection within minutes. The skill is learnable, and the return on investment is enormous.

## 3. The Psychology of First Impressions

### The Primacy Effect

People form lasting impressions within seconds of meeting you. The first 7 seconds are critical — trustworthiness, competence, and warmth are assessed almost instantly. Once formed, first impressions are remarkably sticky: confirming evidence is welcomed, disconfirming evidence is resisted. You do this too, whether you realise it or not.

**Implication:** Prepare for the first 30 seconds of any important interaction more carefully than the next 30 minutes. Those seconds set the impression; everything after is refinement.

### The Halo Effect

A positive first impression colours all subsequent perceptions. If someone likes you, they assume you are competent and worth listening to. Warmth is judged before competence — people need to know if you are friend or threat before they care how capable you are. A smile, eye contact, and open posture trigger positive halos. Nervousness or closed body language creates a negative halo that discounts everything good you do afterwards. **Lead with warmth; competence will be inferred.**

### Mirror Neurons

Humans are biologically wired to sync behaviour with conversation partners. Mirror neurons fire both when you perform an action and observe someone else doing it. Subtle matching of energy, pace, posture, and gestures builds rapport on a neurological level.

**How to use it:** If they speak slowly and quietly, lower your volume. If they are animated, bring yours up. If they lean in, you can lean in. The key word is subtle — forced mirroring is detectable and feels like manipulation.

### The Approach-Avoidance System

Every interaction triggers an ancient neurological assessment: friend or threat? Your job is to signal warmth and safety.

| Approach Signals (Safety) | Avoidance Signals (Threat) |
|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Smile | Crossed arms |
| Open body posture | Darting eyes |
| Slow deliberate movements | Fidgeting |
| Relaxed eye contact | Closed posture |
| Palms visible | Hands in pockets |

### The Trust Equation

**Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-Orientation**

- **Credibility** — do you know your stuff? (Demonstrated over time, not in the first 30 seconds)
- **Reliability** — do you do what you say? (Proven through follow-through)
- **Intimacy** — do I feel safe sharing with you? (This is what ice breakers build)
- **Self-Orientation** — are you focused on yourself or on me? (Ice breakers signal low self-orientation through genuine curiosity)

A great ice breaker raises Intimacy and lowers perceived Self-Orientation simultaneously.

### Emotional Safety

People need to feel safe before they engage. Safety comes from your demeanour (open posture, genuine smile, relaxed eye contact), your first words (low-risk, easy-to-answer, well-intentioned), the environment (quieter spots enable better conversation), and exit availability — people engage more freely when they know they can leave without offence. Signal early that they are not trapped.

## 4. Classic Ice Breaker Techniques

### Observation-Based

Comment on something in the shared environment. Low-risk because the topic is already mutually visible — you are not inventing a topic, just noticing what is there.

- "This is a great venue — have you been here before?"
- "That's an interesting point you made in the meeting. I'd love to hear more."
- "I love your company's logo — what's the story behind it?"
- "I noticed you're wearing a [brand/team logo] — are you a fan?"

**Why it works:** Shows awareness and attentiveness. Gives the other person an obvious, low-effort entry point.

### Context-Based

Reference the event or situation that brought you both together. The safest possible starting point because context is the one thing you are guaranteed to share.

- "What brings you to this conference?"
- "How did you get involved with this group?"
- "What's your take on the keynote so far?"
- "Is this your first time at this event, or have you been before?"

### Compliment-Based

Genuine, specific compliments that show you paid attention. Vague flattery is detectable; specific recognition lands.

- "I really enjoyed your presentation on X, especially the part about Y."
- "That's a great question you asked — I've been wondering the same thing."
- "I admire how you handled that tough question from the audience."

**Rules:** Be specific. Be genuine. Do not compliment appearance unless the context clearly invites it. Never compliment to manipulate.

### Question-Based

Open-ended questions invite conversation and give the other person control over the direction of their answer.

- "What's the most interesting thing you're working on right now?"
- "What's your favourite part of your job?"
- "If you could solve one problem in your industry, what would it be?"
- "What's a trend in our space that is overhyped — or underhyped?"

**Why it works:** Open-ended questions cannot be answered with a single word. They invite elaboration, and the other person chooses how deep to go.

### Common-Ground-Based

Find shared experience, background, or mutual connections. The fastest route to trust — shared identity creates an immediate sense of "us."

- "We both work in fintech — what drew you to this space?"
- "I see you're also from Singapore — which part?"
- "I believe we both know [mutual acquaintance] — how do you know them?"
- "I work in architecture at a bank — sounds like we face similar challenges."

### The FORD Framework

A memory aid for conversation topics after the ice breaker:

| Letter | Topic | Example |
|--------|-------|---------|
| **F** | Family | "Do you have kids?" (Use carefully — not everyone wants to discuss family) |
| **O** | Occupation | "What's the most interesting project you're working on?" |
| **R** | Recreation | "What do you do when you're not working?" |
| **D** | Dreams | "If you could work on any problem in the world, what would it be?" |

Move from F to D gradually — dreams are more personal than occupation. FORD is for the small talk phase, not the opening seconds.

## 5. Situational Ice Breaking Strategies

### Conference / Networking Event

**Preparation.** Set a specific goal: meet 3 people, learn 2 things. Review the attendee list. Prepare 2-3 event-related topics. Have a 30-second introduction ready: who you are, what you do, what you are curious about.

**At the event.** Approach someone standing alone — they will likely be grateful for the rescue. Alternatively, approach the edge of a small group (2-3 people) — easier to join than a tight cluster. Use event context: "What sessions have you attended so far?" / "What brought you to this conference?"

**Timing.** The best networking happens during coffee breaks, lunch queues, and moments after talks end. Be present in those windows rather than checking your phone.

**Closing.** After a meaningful exchange: "It was great meeting you — I'd love to continue this conversation. May I follow up?"

### Business Meeting

**Before (arrive early).** Chat with early arrivals. Use observation: "I like your approach to [topic] — how long has your team been working on this?" Find a point of connection before the formal agenda starts.

**During.** Acknowledge contributions by name. Ask follow-up questions. Find points of agreement and build on them. If the agenda is tight, save extended conversation for after.

**After.** Follow up with anyone you connected with. Reference something specific they said. Suggest continuing the conversation.

### One-on-One Meeting / Coffee Chat

**Opening.** Express genuine gratitude: "Thanks for making time — I really appreciate it." Open with curiosity: "I'd love to hear about your journey — how did you get into [field]?" Ask about their current reality: "What's the biggest challenge in your role right now?" / "What's something exciting happening in your team?"

**During.** Let them set the pace. Share relevant experience but keep focus on them. Listen for cues about what they care about and follow those threads.

**Closing.** Summarise what you learned. Suggest a specific next step. End with genuine appreciation.

### Industry Event with Unfamiliar Domain

Honesty is a strategy — people enjoy being the expert and explaining their world to an interested newcomer.

- "I'm new to this space — I'd love to understand your perspective on [topic]."
- "Can you explain X like I'm five? I'm still learning the landscape."
- "What's the one thing everyone in this industry should know?"
- "What's a misconception outsiders often have about your field?"

Your vulnerability signals trust and gives them a comfortable role — teacher or guide — that most people enjoy playing.

## 6. Ice Breakers for Specific Personas

### For Introverts

**Your strengths:** observation, listening, noticing what others miss.

**Strategies:** Prepare questions in advance — arrive with 3-4 conversation starters already in mind. Use the listener role — ask a good question and then listen. People will remember you as a great conversationalist even if you barely spoke. Find one person, not a crowd — look for someone standing alone or on the edge of a group. One good conversation beats ten superficial ones. Use observation-based openers — they play to your strengths. Give yourself an exit plan.

**Mindset:** You do not need to be the life of the party. One genuine connection is enough.

### For Extroverts

**Your strengths:** energy, engagement, ability to talk to anyone. **Your risk:** dominating without meaning to, mistaking talking for connecting.

**Strategies:** Ask more questions than you answer — aim for a 70/30 ratio of them talking to you talking. Practice active listening — paraphrase what they said before responding. Match energy to the other person — if they are quiet and reserved, dial yours down. Resist filling silence — comfortable pauses are okay. Share the spotlight when others join.

### For Remote Workers

Schedule virtual coffee chats — 15-minute no-agenda video calls with colleagues. Use tools like Donut (Slack) for random connections across the organisation. Create conversation hooks — share interesting articles, post questions of the day. Be visible: keep your status updated, participate in team channels, join optional social calls. Use async tools (Loom, voice notes) to create conversation starters.

### For Senior Leaders

Acknowledge their time: "I appreciate this opportunity to connect." Show you have done your homework — reference their work, their team, their recent achievements. Ask for their perspective, not their approval — "I'd love to hear how you think about [challenge]" is better than "Do you think my idea is good?" Be concise — senior leaders value brevity above almost everything else. Offer value — share an insight, connection, or resource.

### For Junior Colleagues

Show genuine interest in them as people, not just as junior staff. Ask about their experience: "How are you finding the team? What's been surprising?" Offer specific, actionable help — not "Let me know if you need anything" but "I went through a similar challenge with X — would you like me to walk you through my approach?" Reduce power distance: sit beside them rather than across a desk, use first names, share your own early-career struggles. Mentor without condescension.

## 7. Cross-Cultural and Singapore/Asian Context

### Universal Principles

Warmth and genuine interest are recognised in every culture. Respect is the baseline — you can never go wrong with politeness. Observation and context are safe in any setting. Listening is universally valued.

### Hierarchy and Address

Address by title and family name initially unless invited otherwise — "Mr. Tan" or "Dr. Lim" is safer than first names in formal settings. In banking, titles carry particular weight. When in doubt, err on formality — it is easier to dial formality down than up. Pay attention to how others address someone and follow their lead.

### The Concept of "Face"

Avoid public confrontation or criticism. Praise in public, critique in private. Never make someone lose face by highlighting their mistake in a group. If you need to disagree, do so privately and diplomatically. "Face" is about preserving dignity and social harmony — it is not about ego, but about respect for the relationship.

### Relationship Before Business

Singapore business culture values trust before transaction. Invest time in relationship building before diving into business. Meals, coffee, and informal conversations are part of the business process, not separate from it. Rushing to business too quickly can feel rude or transactional. Patience signals you value the person, not just the deal.

### Shared Context

Singlish terms used appropriately can build rapport (but do not force it — insincere attempts feel mocking). Food is a universally safe topic — Singaporeans love talking about food. "Have you eaten?" is a genuine expression of care, not just a question about meals. Hawker centres, food recommendations, and restaurant comparisons are excellent conversation material that can fill entire conversations.

### Multicultural Awareness

Singapore is multicultural — Chinese, Malay, Indian, Eurasian, and expatriate communities coexist. Be aware of different religious and cultural sensitivities. Avoid scheduling important meetings during major holidays (Chinese New Year, Hari Raya, Deepavali, Christmas) without checking first. Learn a few phrases in Mandarin, Malay, or Tamil if you regularly work with diverse teams — the effort signals respect. During Ramadan, be mindful of colleagues who are fasting — avoid lunch meetings and be sensitive about energy levels in the afternoon.

### Networking Events Involving Meals

Meals are prime networking opportunities. Use food as a conversation starter: "Have you tried the [dish]? It's excellent here." / "I'm always looking for good [cuisine] recommendations — any suggestions?" Always wait for the host or most senior person at the table to start eating before you begin.

## 8. Conversation Flow After Ice Breaking

### The ARMS Framework

| Step | Action | Example |
|------|--------|---------|
| **A** | **Ask** — open-ended questions about them | "What's the most interesting thing you're working on?" |
| **R** | **Respond** — show you are listening and relate | "That's fascinating — we faced a similar challenge. How did you handle it?" |
| **M** | **Mirror** — match their energy, pace, and topics | If they are analytical, be analytical. If enthusiastic, match that. |
| **S** | **Share** — offer something relevant and brief about yourself | "I switched from backend to architecture for similar reasons." |

Cycle through ARMS repeatedly. Each cycle deepens the connection.

### Natural Progression

Ice Breaker → Find Common Ground → Explore Topic → Deeper Connection → Exchange Value → Next Steps

Within the first few exchanges, identify something shared — industry, challenge, interest, experience. Once you find common ground, ask follow-up questions that go deeper. As the conversation deepens, share something real but not intimate — a challenge you faced, a lesson you learned. Offer something useful — an insight, connection, or resource — not transactionally, but generously. Finally, suggest a specific, low-pressure follow-up.

### Questions That Deepen Conversation

- "That's fascinating — what made you get into that?"
- "What's the most rewarding part of what you do?"
- "What's a lesson you learned the hard way?"
- "If you could go back 5 years, what advice would you give yourself?"
- "What's something you're trying to get better at?"
- "What's the best piece of professional advice you ever received?"

Use one or two per conversation — more than that feels like an interview. Each one signals genuine interest in a way that surface questions cannot.

### Listening Techniques

- **Paraphrase:** "So if I understand correctly, you're saying that..." / "Let me make sure I got that..."
- **Ask follow-ups** that reference specific details they mentioned earlier
- **Avoid:** interrupting, finishing their sentences, planning your response while they talk, one-upping ("That's nothing, I...")
- **Use minimal encouragers:** nodding, "mm-hmm," "right," "interesting," "tell me more"

### Transitioning to Business

"This has been great context — shall we talk about how we might work together?" / "I'd love to continue this conversation — can I follow up with an email?" / "Based on what you've shared, I think there might be a way we can collaborate on [specific idea]." / "I don't want to take up too much of your time — let me share why I wanted to connect." Transition when you have built enough rapport that the business topic feels like a natural extension, not a non-sequitur.

## 9. Ice Breakers to Avoid

### The Bad Ones

**Overly personal questions (too soon).** Salary, relationship status, and divisive politics are off-limits in professional ice breaking. Asking "Are you married?" or "Do you have kids?" before establishing rapport can feel invasive.

**Negative comments.** "This conference is boring, right?" puts them in an awkward position — agreeing is rude to organisers, disagreeing creates conflict with you. Complaining can bond people, but it bonds around negativity. Aim for positivity or shared curiosity.

**Overly aggressive compliments.** "You're the most interesting person here" comes across as insincere. "I've heard so much about you" can sound like gossip. Keep compliments specific and grounded.

**One-upping.** Anything starting with "That's nothing, I..." diminishes their experience. "You faced that challenge? We had it ten times worse" makes the conversation about you. "You liked that book? The author's earlier work is much better" is condescending.

**Jargon bombing.** Drowning them in acronyms and technical terms they may not know. Check in: "Does that terminology make sense?" rather than assuming shared context.

### The Boring Ones

**"So, what do you do?"** Inevitable but lazy. Add context: "What's the most interesting part of your work?" **"Nice weather, isn't it?"** Too generic — shows no effort or situational awareness. **Business card fishing.** Asking for contact info before any rapport is built — people feel used when conversation is clearly lead generation. **Interview mode.** Rapid-fire questions without sharing anything about yourself — "What do you do?" → "Where do you work?" → "How long have you been there?" The person feels processed, not connected with.

### Reading the Room

**Signals to exit:** short closed-ended answers, looking away or checking watch, body turned away, no questions back to you.

**Graceful exits:** "It was great meeting you — I won't keep you any longer. Enjoy the rest of the event." / "Thanks for the chat — I know you probably want to network, so I'll let you mingle." / "This has been really valuable. Is there a good way to follow up?"

**The rule:** Never force conversation with someone who wants to be alone. Save face for both of you by exiting gracefully.

## 10. Ice Breaking for Remote and Virtual Settings

### The Challenges

No physical context to comment on — no venue, no shared environment, no observable details to anchor a conversation. Harder to read body language — you see only a face and shoulders. Awkward silences feel longer and heavier on video. Higher cognitive load — video calls are mentally tiring, and small talk can feel like extra work. Technical barriers — lag, audio issues, connectivity problems interrupt natural flow. No natural adjacency — you cannot bump into someone in a virtual hallway.

### The Strategies

- **Use the first 2-3 minutes** for personal connection before diving into the agenda. Set a team norm: "Let's take a few minutes to catch up first."
- **Comment on their background** (if visible and appropriate): bookshelf, plant, artwork. Avoid private or personal items.
- **Acknowledge time zones:** "I really appreciate you joining early/late for this — thank you." / "How's your day going so far?"
- **Use the chat** for sharing links, resources, and emoji reactions to show engagement without interrupting.
- **Leverage virtual coffee tools** — Donut (Slack) for random pairings, Coffee Club for structured rotations, recurring random 1:1s within your team.

### Virtual Meeting Openers

"How's your week going?" / "What's something good that happened today — work or personal?" / "Before we dive in, any interesting discoveries this week?" / "I saw you're working on [project] — how's that progressing?" / "Any good weekend plans?" (Friday) / "How was your weekend?" (Monday). For recurring meetings, use rotating questions: "What's one thing you learned this week?" / "What's a win since our last meeting?" / "What's something you're looking forward to?"

### Remote Team Ice Breaking

Create dedicated Slack channels for non-work conversation: #random, #food, #pets, #books. Establish social rituals: virtual Friday drinks/coffee (optional, low-pressure), show-and-tell sessions, weekly questions, online games or quizzes. Celebrate birthdays and work anniversaries in team channels. Use a shared document or virtual whiteboard for ice breaker questions that everyone answers async before the meeting.

### Graceful Virtual Exits

"I know we have a full agenda, so let's dive in." / "Great catching up — let's continue over Slack." / "Thanks for sharing — that gives me great context for our discussion." / "I'll drop that resource in the chat."

## 11. Following Up After Ice Breaking

### The Golden Rule

Follow up within 24 hours while the interaction is still fresh. Every additional hour reduces recall. Reference something specific from the conversation. Suggest a specific next step. Offer value — share an article, introduction, or insight.

### Templates

**After an event:** "Great meeting you at [event]. I really enjoyed our conversation about [topic]. Would love to continue — are you free for a coffee or Zoom next week?"

**After a meeting:** "Thanks for the insightful chat at [meeting]. Your point about [specific topic] really resonated. I'd love to hear more about [related topic] if you have time."

**After a coffee chat:** "Thank you again for your time. The advice you shared about [specific topic] was incredibly helpful. I'd love to stay in touch — let me know if there's ever anything I can help with."

### Maintaining the Connection

Connect on LinkedIn with a personalised note referencing your conversation — never use the default message. Share relevant resources when you come across them tied to your conversation. Check in every 3-6 months tied to something specific — a post they shared, a promotion, an industry event. Offer value before asking for anything. Attend events where they will be present.

### From Ice Breaker to Relationship

First Contact (Ice Breaker) → Follow-Up (Recap + Value) → Second Interaction (Deeper Conversation) → Ongoing Exchange (Share Value, Check In) → Collaboration / Work Together

Most connections never move past the first two stages because follow-up is neglected. The people who invest in follow-up build the strongest, most valuable professional networks.

## 12. Practicing Ice Breaking

### Daily Practice

Every day has low-stakes opportunities with no real downside: chat with the barista ("How's your day going?"), greet colleagues in the elevator ("Morning! How's your week starting?"), start a conversation at the gym ("I've been trying to figure out this machine — any tips?"), talk to someone in a queue ("This line is always long — is the food worth the wait?"). If it goes well, you had a pleasant human moment. If it is awkward, you will never see that person again.

### Weekly Practice

Attend one networking event or industry meetup (even 30 minutes). Start a conversation with someone new at work — from a different team or department. Volunteer for a cross-functional project. Say yes to the social event — team lunch, after-work drinks. Join a professional community — Slack groups, LinkedIn groups, industry associations.

### Deliberate Practice

Set specific goals: "This week I will start 3 conversations with people I do not know." Keep a simple log: technique used, how it went, what you would change. Ask for feedback from trusted colleagues: "How did I come across when we first met? What could I have done better?" Try new techniques — if you always use observation openers, try question-based. Review your log monthly. Identify patterns. Double down on what works.

### Building Confidence

Start with low-stakes interactions to build the muscle before the big event. Focus on the other person, not yourself — nervousness comes from self-focus. Remember that most people are also nervous — many at networking events are hoping someone will talk to them. Every interaction is practice — there are no failures, only data. You do not need to be perfect — just genuine. A slightly awkward authentic opening beats a smooth fake one every time.

## Appendix: Quick Reference Card

**Five go-to ice breakers.** 1) "What brings you to [event/meeting]?" — works everywhere, always. 2) "What's the most interesting thing you're working on right now?" — shows genuine interest. 3) "I really enjoyed [specific thing] — how did you approach that?" — specific and flattering. 4) "I see you're also from [shared context] — how do you find working in [field]?" — builds common ground. 5) "I'm new to [topic/space] — what's the one thing I should know?" — honest and inviting.

**Pre-interaction checklist.** Do I know who I am meeting? (Quick LinkedIn review?) What is my goal? What shared context can I reference? What genuine question am I curious about? Do I have a graceful exit planned?

**Signs it's working.** They ask you a question in return. Their answers get longer and more detailed. They smile, lean in, or open their posture. They introduce you to someone else. They suggest continuing the conversation.

**Signs to exit.** One-word answers. Eyes scanning the room. Body turned away. Checking phone or watch. No questions back to you.

**The golden rules.**
1. Be genuinely interested, not interesting — curiosity beats charisma
2. Focus on the other person — the best conversationalists are the best listeners
3. Follow up within 24 hours — the conversation is only the beginning
4. Practice daily — low-stakes interactions build the muscle for high-stakes ones
5. You do not need to be perfect — just genuine. Authenticity is the ultimate ice breaker
