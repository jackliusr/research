# MOSIP: Deciding Once Who Exists

**MOSIP — the Modular Open Source Identity Platform — is the open-source, modular software stack that a state installs when it decides to answer one question once, for a whole population: *is this person unique?* Everything MOSIP does follows from that question. It captures a person's demographics and biometrics at a registration centre, inserts those biometrics into an automated biometric identification system (ABIS), runs a one-to-many (1:N) search against every record the system has ever stored, routes the borderline hits to human adjudicators, and — only then — mints a unique identity number. It then keeps that identity alive: authentication, update, deactivation. This guide is the deep-dive on that **foundational layer** — how an identity comes to exist, is deduplicated and is maintained at national scale. It deliberately stops at the point where `technology/verifiable_credentials_guide.md` begins, because the credential, wallet, format and trust-fabric layers are already owned by that sibling guide.**

## 1. The Overview

### 1.1 The Short Answer

**MOSIP is not an identity system. It is a kit for building one.** The project does not issue credentials to citizens, does not run a country's enrolment campaign, and does not own a single resident record anywhere. What it ships is modular, open-source, microservice-based software that a government (or an organisation standing in for one) installs, configures and operates in its own jurisdiction, so that the resulting national identity system is *the country's* system — its law, its registration centres, its biometric devices, its system integrator, its data, physically inside its own borders. The project describes itself on its home page as "A Digital Public Good for Identity," and that framing is accurate in the narrow structural sense: it is public infrastructure input, not the service built on it ✅ (mosip.io home page, retrieved 16 October 2026).

The platform was **established in 2018**, incubated at the **International Institute of Information Technology Bangalore (IIIT-B)** ✅ (mosip.io home page, retrieved 16 October 2026; iiitb.ac.in project page). The licence that matters for adoption is the **Mozilla Public License 2.0 (MPL 2.0)**, a file-level weak-copyleft licence, used across the core repositories, with the **MIT** licence for sample apps, SDKs and helper utilities and **CC-BY-SA 4.0** for non-code assets such as documentation ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/license, page last updated ~4 months before 16 October 2026). The licence page states the ownership position plainly: "All MOSIP code is owned and maintained by International Institute of Information Technology, Bangalore, on behalf of MOSIP" ✅ — and that single sentence is the load-bearing fact for anyone deciding whether to bet national infrastructure on the project.

What MOSIP actually *does*, mechanically, is captured in one pipeline. A resident's demographic data and biometrics (fingerprints, iris, face) are captured at a registration centre by an offline-first thick client. The packet flows through a staged, event-driven backend. The biometrics are inserted into an **Automated Biometric Identification System (ABIS)** and a **1:N identify** request is fired against every record the ABIS holds. Potential matches go to a **Manual Adjudication System** for a human decision. If the person is judged unique, the system issues a **Unique Identity Number (UIN)**; if the person is judged a duplicate of an existing record, the packet is **rejected** ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication"). That pipeline — not the credential, not the wallet, not the QR code — is the part of MOSIP this guide is about, and it is the part that makes the platform hard.

The nuance that keeps this guide honest is that MOSIP is both narrower and more consequential than its marketing implies. It is *narrower* because it is code, not a campaign: the inclusion outcomes attributable to a MOSIP-based system depend on buses, staffing, consent forms, power backup and grievance desks — which the project's own field-operations guidance describes at length ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/setup/cookbook/field-operations). It is *more consequential* because the pipeline above decides something nothing downstream can undo: **whether you exist, once, in the official record.** A bank that cannot complete KYC can refuse an account. A foundational identity system that decides you are a duplicate of someone else, or that cannot capture your biometrics, decides something with a much longer tail.

### 1.3 The Decoder

One crisp line each, so the rest of the guide reads cleanly.

- **Foundational identity** — a unique, government-recognised identifier issued to a person that is *not tied to a single service*, providing the base layer that functional (sector) IDs can lean on ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/overview).
- **Functional identity** — a sector-specific identifier issued for a specific use case (health, finance, social protection, education, voting) that may *leverage* the foundational identity rather than replace it ✅ (same page).
- **Civil registration / CRVS** — the Civil Registration and Vital Statistics system that records life events (birth, death, marriage, divorce) and is the natural upstream partner of a foundational identity system ✅ (docs.mosip.io, MOSIP–CRVS integration).
- **Enrolment** — the physical, procedural act of capturing a person's demographics and biometrics at a registration centre (or in-home via the Android Registration Client) and submitting a packet ✅ (docs.mosip.io, field operations; identity-issuance).
- **UIN (Unique Identity Number)** — the permanent identifier a resident receives once their packet passes validation and deduplication; what MOSIP calls a "foundational" number ✅ (docs.mosip.io, identity-issuance).
- **Deduplication** — the process of comparing a new individual's demographic and biometric data against everything already stored, to establish that the person is unique within the system ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication").
- **ABIS (Automated Biometric Identification System)** — the external system that performs the 1:N biometric search; MOSIP's architecture deliberately keeps it as a pluggable, replaceable component that never learns residents' identities ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page).
- **IDA (ID Authentication)** — the *independent* authentication service that answers "is this the person who holds this UIN/VID?" — with one or more factors — and can be seeded by any system, "including MOSIP" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ID Authentication services).
- **Partner programme and sandbox** — the PMS-governed onboarding of every external actor (banks, telecoms, government bodies, device and ABIS providers) plus two hosted environments, Collab (development) and Synergy (stable release), as the entry point for integration ✅ (docs.mosip.io, sandbox details; partners).
- **Identity lifecycle** — the end-to-end state machine: issuance, verification, management and support systems — and the spine of this guide (Section 5) ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/id-lifecycle-management).

Two more the reader will meet, worth defining once:

- **VID (Virtual ID)** — a revocable, regenerable alias for a UIN, so the permanent identifier is never handed to a relying party ✅ (docs.mosip.io, privacy-and-security; id-repository VID service).
- **MAS (Manual Adjudication System)** — the human decision layer that receives borderline biometric hits from the backend; built by the country's system integrator, not by MOSIP ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication").

### 1.4 Why a Bank Should Care: The Cymbal Bank Lens

A bank does not buy an identity platform. A bank *inherits* the identity world its market lives in, and that inheritance shows up at one specific place: the onboarding counter. Everything the bank will later do — CDD, sanctions screening, account opening, credit assessment, KYC refresh, fraud detection — assumes a prior answer to a prior question the bank did not get to ask: *is this person who they say they are, and does the state agree?*

Where a MOSIP-based system exists, that question has a state-grade answer for the first time. The bank's counterpart is the documented **Authentication Partner / Relying Party** role — "Entities that use MOSIP for authentication like banks, telecom, Govt. institutes etc." ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners) — and the project's own partner-policy model means the bank gets *attribute-level* access, not the whole record: one partner may receive only a name, another a name and a photograph; where eKYC is permitted, the partner may receive additional personally identifiable information ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners). That is the shape of the integration, and it is a genuinely different shape from "upload a PDF of your passport."

So the Cymbal Bank lens is not "should we use MOSIP" — a bank never does; it is not the adopter. The lens is: **what does the presence of this platform change about our onboarding, our cost-to-onboard, our inclusion obligations, and our model risk?** Three things, at minimum. First, the *verification* half of onboarding becomes a call, not a document collection — cheaper, faster, and harder to fake. Second, the *decision* half is untouched: MOSIP does not tell a bank whether to onboard someone, and it does not carry the bank's regulatory obligations. Third, a new dependency enters the model — the bank now depends on the continued operation, accuracy and legal standing of a piece of national infrastructure it does not control and cannot replace. Sections 9 and 14 work that through, and the honest conclusion is the one the guide's closing line states: the platform's hard problem is not storage, it is the decision, and a bank inherits the consequences of that decision without inheriting the authority over it.

### 1.5 The Boundary Declared by Name

This guide and `technology/verifiable_credentials_guide.md` are neighbours with a hard fence between them, and the fence runs along a simple question: **does the claim already exist?** The verifiable-credentials guide owns everything that happens *after* an identity and its attributes exist. It owns the W3C VC data model and its version history; the DID layer; the formats, including SD-JWT; the signature, privacy and zero-knowledge machinery; revocation and status mechanics; presentation and issuance protocols and flows; ecosystem roles and the trust fabric; and the use cases built on top — KYC-as-a-use-case, national digital identity including Singpass and the EUDI wallet, the eIDAS 2.0 regulatory layer, and the repo's Cymbal Bank VC KYC pilot. Every one of those topics is out of scope here, and none of them is re-derived here. **This guide owns the layer beneath them: how an identity comes to exist at all, how it is deduplicated against an entire population without collapsing into a single record, how it is issued, authenticated, updated and deactivated, and what it costs a country — in institutions, logistics and law — to keep that running.** Where the two guides touch — the platform's own Inji and eSignet modules, and the documented loan-application use case — this guide names them once and hands the reader to the sibling (Section 10). One further boundary, stated just as plainly: `technology/non_human_identity_guide.md` is about **machine identity** — workloads, services, devices — and it is **not adjacent** to this guide. A SPIFFE ID and a national UIN are both "identities" in the loose sense and in no other sense; they have different subjects, different threat models, different governance and different failure modes. Do not let a taxonomy word do the work of an argument.
## 2. What MOSIP Is

### 2.1 The project, stated without adjectives

Strip away the framing and MOSIP is a body of software plus a small institution. The software is a set of modular, microservice-based components — registration, processing, identity storage, biometric interfacing, authentication, partner management, reporting — released as JARs to Maven Central, images to Docker Hub, and Helm charts from `mosip.github.io/mosip-helm`, with Kubernetes cluster scripts in `mosip-infra` and `k8s-infra` ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/deploymentnew/getting-started; docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/technology/architecture). The institution is a not-for-profit incubated at IIIT-B, whose own project page states that MOSIP "is a IIIT-Bangalore project, established in 2018 as a core component of global Digital Public Infrastructure" ✅ (iiitb.ac.in/projects/mosip-2, retrieved 16 October 2026; the page carries no date of its own).

The project's own framing is that it "offers countries **modular** and **open-source** technology to **build** and **own** their national identity systems" ✅ (mosip.io home page, retrieved 16 October 2026). Every one of those three verbs is doing work. *Build* — the platform is not a service; there is no MOSIP cloud to sign up for. *Own* — the intended end state is that the country's institutions hold the deployment, the data and the operational burden. *Modular* — the components are designed to be separable, and Section 4 examines how far that actually goes.

**What MOSIP is not, stated once.** It is not a credential issuer. It is not a registration authority — it does not employ the operators who capture the biometrics. It is not a biometric algorithm vendor: the ABIS is external and pluggable, and the project explicitly does not recommend using a ABIS for 1:1 authentication, deferring that to a Biometric SDK ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page). It is not a legal framework: the project's own principles document says the legal and governance framework is a country matter, and that governments are "the ultimate decision-maker on these aspects of an identity system" ✅ (MOSIP Country Engagement Principles PDF, mosip.io/program_partner/country-engagement-principles.pdf, retrieved 16 October 2026; cover and footer credit Pratiksha Trust).

### 2.2 The origin, dated

The establishment date is **2018**, and it is asserted consistently across primary sources: the project's home page ("established in 2018 at the International Institute for Information Technology Bangalore"), the IIIT-B project page ("established in 2018"), and IIIT-B's research-centres listing, which carries MOSIP as one of the institute's centres ✅ (all retrieved 16 October 2026). Note the two spellings of the institution in circulation — the site's own body text says "International Institute for Information Technology Bangalore" while the docs say "International Institute of Information Technology, Bangalore (IIIT-B)" — and treat the docs/footer form as canonical; the difference is a typo on a web page, not an entity distinction worth building a theory on ✅/⚠.

The *reason* the project was started, in the project's own words, is a reaction to a specific failure pattern: the home page says MOSIP was established "as a response to the challenges faced by nations adopting digital ID systems," and the Country Engagement Principles document is more explicit — the project "was envisaged to provide an open-source solution to tackle problems related to digital identity systems, particularly that of closed, proprietary technology and consequent vendor lock-in" ✅ (both retrieved 16 October 2026). That is the origin story and it is coherent with the licence choice examined next.

### 2.3 The licence, precisely

The licence situation is layered, and the layers matter for anyone reasoning about a fork, a vendor offering, or an exit.

- **Core repositories: MPL 2.0.** The docs state that "MOSIP's primary licenses across its repositories are the Mozilla Public License 2.0 (MPL 2.0) and the MIT License," with MPL 2.0 "the primary license used across all core MOSIP repositories" ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/license, page last updated ~4 months before 16 October 2026). MPL 2.0 is a *file-level* weak-copyleft licence: only the files containing MPL-licensed code must remain under MPL; other files in the same repository may carry different terms. The docs give the rationale directly — it permits independently evolving components, guarantees that improvements to MPL files are published, is compatible with permissive licences such as MIT, BSD and Apache 2.0, and allows proprietary or differently-licensed modules to coexist in one repository ✅.
- **MIT for reusable modules.** The MIT licence is used "for modules intended for broad reuse and easy integration such as sample applications, helper utilities, SDKs, and educational/reference implementations" ✅ (same page). The docs also record a specific migration: "Used when MOSIP moved ARC license from MPL → MIT," with a Corporate Contributor License Agreement recorded for the contributing organisations ✅.
- **CC-BY-SA 4.0 for non-code.** Documentation, training content, diagrams and sample datasets carry Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 ✅.
- **Per-repo hygiene.** "Each repository includes" a `LICENSE.txt` and a `/third-party-licenses/` folder; a NOTICE file is maintained where attribution is required; and an SBOM is generated per release using CycloneDX ✅.
- **Contribution compliance.** Every contributor signs a Developer Certificate of Origin, "enforced automatically via GitHub checks," with every commit signed off ✅.
- **The simplification to be aware of.** The GitHub organisation profile states flatly that "All modules are licensed under the Mozilla Public License 2.0 and hosted on GitHub" ⚠ (github.com/mosip org README, retrieved 16 October 2026). That is a summary, not a contradiction — but the docs are the finer-grained and more accurate source, and the guide flags the org README's simplification rather than repeating it as fact.

Why MPL 2.0 rather than Apache 2.0 or the GPL is the interesting design question, and the docs answer it: MPL is weak copyleft at the *file* level, so a country, a system integrator or a vendor can extend MOSIP with proprietary components in the same repository while any improvements to MPL-licensed files stay open ✅. That is exactly the trade a platform hoping to attract a commercial partner ecosystem — device makers, ABIS vendors, system integrators — needs: enough copyleft to prevent a proprietary fork from swallowing the commons, little enough to let vendors build billable layers on top.

### 2.4 The trademark, and the separation from the licence

The code licence and the name are two different instruments, and conflating them is a real error. IIIT-B asserts and has registered the **MOSIP®** trademark in India and other countries, managed through a separate IP policy page ✅ (mosip.io/ip_policy, retrieved 16 October 2026). The practical consequence: MPL 2.0 gives you the right to the *code*; it gives you no right to call your deployment or your product "MOSIP" in a way the trademark policy forbids. For a vendor considering a commercial offering, that is the clause to read first.

### 2.5 The funding question, handled honestly

One additional corroborating trace, not a funding claim in itself: the *MOSIP Country Engagement Principles* PDF is published with "Pratiksha Trust" on its cover and footer ✅ (mosip.io/program_partner/country-engagement-principles.pdf, retrieved 16 October 2026), which is consistent with that funder's role without proving the scale or the term of any grant.

This guide records that as a limitation of what could be verified, and it does **not** guess which organisations the four logos represent, does **not** add any funder name not present in the source above, and does **not** print a funding amount, a grant period or a donor share that no source in this pass supports. The reason to be this strict is not pedantry. A funder set is how a reader infers whose priorities a global public good is likely to serve, and inferring that from a logo wall would be inventing evidence. What *can* be stated as verified is the ownership line — IIIT-B owns and maintains the code on behalf of MOSIP ✅ — and the trademark registration ✅. Everything about money is flagged, undated, and single-sourced.

### 2.6 Who governs MOSIP — the honest gap

A reader would reasonably want the next paragraph to describe the board, the steering committee, the technical decision rights, and the process by which a country's bug report becomes a roadmap item. **This pass could not establish any of that.** The licence trail points to IIIT-B as the owner of record ✅; the principles document credits Pratiksha Trust ✅; the footer says "Incubated by IIITB" ✅. But no primary source retrieved this pass names a governing board, a steering committee, a technical advisory council with a charter, or a published decision-rights document — and this guide says so rather than inferring governance from the copyright line. Section 16 carries this as an explicit unverified item. For the reader deciding whether to depend on the project, the honest formulation is: the *licence* answers "may I fork and run this forever?" with an unusually clean yes; the *governance* question answers "who decides what happens to the roadmap?" with something closer to silence.

## 3. The Problem It Solves

### 3.1 Foundational identity versus functional identity

The project's own overview page draws the distinction that everything else depends on, and it is worth quoting in its own terms. A **Foundational Identity System** "provides individuals with a unique identifier issued by the government, enabling identity assertion and verification across multiple services. Unlike functional IDs, which are designed for specific use cases such as healthcare, finance, and social services, foundational IDs serve as a universal framework that various sectors can leverage" ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/overview).

The consequence of that architecture is not cosmetic. A functional ID — a health number, a tax number, a bank customer number — is issued *by the sector, for the sector*, and it is normally the case that no sector trusts another sector's identifier. The result is the familiar fragmentation: the same person is five different records in five institutions, none reconcilable, and every new service re-enrols them from scratch. A foundational ID is the attempt to issue one identifier that all sectors can *reference* rather than duplicate, with the foundational system doing the deduplication work once ✅. The project's own framing of the downstream relationship is that functional IDs "can leverage the foundational ID system to ensure accuracy, efficiency, and seamless service access" ✅. Read that carefully, because it is where the design leverage sits: the foundational layer is not intended to *replace* sectoral systems. It is intended to become the thing they all point at.

A second consequence, less often stated and more important for this guide, is that a foundational identifier is *the thing that has to be right*. A duplicated health number produces a billing reconciliation problem. A duplicated foundational identifier produces two records that both claim to be the same person — or one record that has swallowed two people — and every system downstream of it inherits the error permanently. Section 6 is about the machinery that is supposed to prevent exactly that.

### 3.2 The population without an ID

The scale claim in circulation is that roughly **850 million people** lack legal and verifiable identity. It appears on the project's home page as "Nearly 850 million people across the world lack legal and verifiable ID," immediately followed by the project's own interpretation — "An identity gap of this scale impedes access to rights and services, disproportionately affecting the most vulnerable sections of society" ✅ (mosip.io home, retrieved 16 October 2026) — and it appears again in the Country Engagement Principles, which says "around 850 million people globally lack formal identity" and ties the figure to UN Sustainable Development Goal **Target 16.9** — "By 2030, provide legal identity for all, including birth registration" ✅ (principles PDF, retrieved 16 October 2026). The same target and phrasing also appear on the project's About page ✅ (mosip.io/mosip_project).

Here is the discipline this guide applies: the *attribution* is verified — this is what the project publishes, and it is the project's stated motivation — but the *underlying figure* originates outside MOSIP and was **not** verified at its original source this pass ⚠. It is printed here as project-stated and marked accordingly, not as a demographic fact. That distinction matters because "850 million" is doing rhetorical work: it is the number that makes the case for building the platform at all. A reader who wants to lean on it should go to the originating statistic, and this guide says so rather than laundering the project's citation into an independent one.

What the verified material does establish, without needing the headline number, is that the *problem class* is real and that the project's design responds to it: the inclusion mechanisms documented by the project (the introducer route for people with no documents, biometric exception handling, in-home registration, multi-language support) are all answers to specific, named ways people fall outside an enrolment process ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/principles/inclusion). You do not need a global statistic to see what those mechanisms are for.

### 3.3 The inclusion argument

The project's inclusion case, in its own documented form, has four load-bearing parts, and each is worth stating precisely because each has a corresponding failure mode in Section 13.

First, **the introducer route**. "The Introducer concept in MOSIP allows individuals without formal identity documents to be enrolled into the digital identity system. A trusted and verified Introducer, such as a community leader or an authorized individual, vouches for the person's identity," with the documented intent of covering "refugees, nomadic groups, and those lacking paperwork" ✅ (docs.mosip.io inclusion page). Read structurally, this is a deliberate softening of the evidence standard at the enrolment gate — a trust-based verification standing in for documentary verification — and the page frames it as such: "inclusion through trust-based verification" ✅.

Second, **biometric exception handling**. "When biometrics cannot be collected, MOSIP provides alternative methods, such as marking the biometric modality as an exception and capturing a photograph as evidence, thereby allowing inclusive identity registration for all," with the documented population being "elderly individuals, manual laborers, and persons with disabilities" and the named causes being "worn fingerprints or missing biometrics like finger or eyes" ✅ (same page). This matters more than it looks: a biometric-first identity system is, by construction, hostile to people whose biometrics will not capture, and the exception path is the corrective.

Third, **physical reach**. The Android Registration Client is documented as enabling "in-home registration for individuals who are unable to visit physical registration centers," explicitly to expand coverage for people in remote or underserved areas ✅ (same page).

Fourth, **channel plurality**. Verification is documented as multi-modal through Inji and eSignet across "online/offline, self-service/assisted modes," and usable "even with smartphones, feature phones, or no phones" ✅ (same page). That last clause is the one that admits how thin the frontier is: designing for people with *no phone* is designing for the population a digital-by-default system would otherwise define as unreachable.

There is a fifth element — the project's collaboration with **GenderMag** to integrate gender-sensitive design principles into the user interface ✅ (same page) — which is a design-process claim rather than a system capability, and is marked accordingly.

The honest reading of all five is that MOSIP provides *the mechanisms* for inclusion and *not* the outcome. An exception path that must be switched on, staffed and supervised is a very different thing from an exception path that happens by default. Section 13(b) works that failure mode through.

### 3.4 Scale, and what "scale" means here

Two separate scale claims should not be mixed up. The first is the *design* claim: the project's documentation states the platform serves "populations from a few thousand to several hundred million" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, project documentation). That is an engineering statement about the architecture's intended operating envelope, and it is the reason the components are event-driven, queue-based and offline-first rather than a single database with an application in front of it. The second is the *deployment* claim — how many people are actually registered on MOSIP-based systems today — and that is the contested, undated, project-published material handled in Section 15 and Section 16 ✅/⚠ (mosip.io home, retrieved 16 October 2026: "203,044,617+ Residents Registered on MOSIP"; iiitb.ac.in: "26 countries"; the project's country page: "32 country engagements till date").

What is worth extracting from the scale discussion, before the adoption numbers get their honest treatment, is why scale is the entire technical problem. Deduplication is a 1:N search, so its cost grows with the population it searches against; enrolment is a physical process, so its throughput is bounded by registration kits, operators, power and roads; and issuance is a state transition that must be exactly right, because a wrongly issued UIN is far harder to unwind than a wrongly issued bank account. The three constraints pull in different directions, and the architecture in Section 4 is best understood as the project's settlement among them.
## 4. The Architecture and What "Modular" Actually Means

### 4.1 The principle list, and why it is the right place to start

The project states its architectural principles as a list, and the list is the most useful single artefact for judging the design, because every subsequent choice can be traced back to one of its entries: **Data Privacy, No Vendor Lock-in, Open Standards, Async/Offline First, Commodity Computing, Fault Tolerant, Manageable, Secure By Default** ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/technology/architecture). The docs describe MOSIP as "built on a modular, microservices-based architecture" whose modularity "enables seamless adoption even in complex scenarios," with most modules "designed as robust foundational infrastructure components, making them suitable for integration into various projects" ✅.

Read the eight principles as constraints rather than virtues and the architecture explains itself. *Async/offline-first* is why the registration client is a thick desktop application that can run disconnected and sync later, and why the backend is a staged event pipeline driven by message queues rather than a synchronous API chain. *Commodity computing* is why the stack runs on ordinary x86 servers and Kubernetes rather than special hardware. *No vendor lock-in* and *Open standards* are why the ABIS, the biometric devices and the system integrator are all pluggable roles rather than baked-in dependencies. *Fault tolerant* and *Manageable* are why the deployment guidance pushes a service mesh and high availability for production (§8). *Data Privacy* and *Secure By Default* are why keys are centrally managed, why every record carries an integrity check, and why data leaving the platform is signed and encrypted (§12).

### 4.2 The modules, named

The platform decomposes into the following, drawn from the project's organisation README, the docs overview and the identity-issuance and support-systems pages ✅ (github.com/mosip org README; docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/overview; docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/id-lifecycle-management):

- **Pre-Registration** — online demographic entry and appointment booking, so the centre visit is shorter and the data is pre-validated ✅.
- **Registration Client** — the offline-first thick desktop client that captures demographics and biometrics; and the **Android Registration Client**, a portable/tablet variant for in-home and field registration ✅.
- **Registration Processor** — the backend that validates and processes packets and drives UIN generation ✅.
- **ID Repository** — the identity store: Identity service, VID service, Credential service, Credential Request Generator, Credential Feeder, Salt generator ✅.
- **ID Authentication (IDA)** — the independent authentication service, with Authentication Service, KYC Authentication Service, OTP Request Service, Internal Authentication/OTP, and Authentication Transaction History ✅.
- **Resident Services / Portal** — the resident-facing surface for updating data and monitoring ID usage ✅.
- **Partner Management** — onboarding, policy and certificate management for external partners ✅.
- **Administration**, **Reporting** — configuration, roles, permissions and reporting on lifecycle activity ✅.

Supporting components named across the docs include **Key Manager**, **Datashare**, **WebSub**, **Object Store**, **Masterdata Service**, **Audit Manager**, **Biometric SDK**, **Quality Manager**, **Salt generator**, **Credential Request Generator**, **Credential Feeder**, **Identity service** and **VID service** ✅ (docs.mosip.io, supporting-components and id-repository pages). The presence of an audit manager, a salt generator and a dedicated quality manager is the tell that this is not a CRUD application: each exists to serve a specific obligation — non-repudiable audit, irreversible hashing, and biometric quality gating respectively.

### 4.3 The Registration Processor, in detail

The Registration Processor is the pipeline, and the docs describe it as internally following **SEDA — staged event-driven architecture — "where data flows via multiple stages till the UIN is issued"** ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/id-lifecycle-management/identity-issuance/registration-processor/overview). The organisation README characterises it as an Apache Camel workflow with Vert.x-based stages ✅ (github.com/mosip org README). The documented stage-and-service relationships are:

- Packets are uploaded by the Registration Client to the **Packet Receiver** ✅.
- Validation runs, then pre-registration is notified via the datasync service ✅.
- Biometric quality is checked in the **Quality Classifier stage** using an *external biometric SDK* ✅ — that is, quality gating is a pluggable dependency, not an internal algorithm.
- The processor shares biometric data with **ABIS**, a **Manual Adjudication System** and a **Verification System**, with the sharing policy fetched from **PMS** ✅.
- Shared data is passed as a **Datashare** URL rather than embedded in the message, and the URL's access is governed by partner policy ✅ (docs.mosip.io, Datashare and partner-policy pages).
- The **ABIS Middleware stage** communicates with ABIS over **ActiveMQ** queues ✅.
- The processor stores and updates demographic and biometric information in the **ID Repository**, and activates or deactivates the UIN ✅.
- It calls IDA's **Internal Authentication Service** to authenticate the applicant (in the update flow), the introducer, the operator and the supervisor, when biometric-auth mode is used to create the packet ✅.
- After UIN processing, the **Printing (Credential Requestor) stage** calls the **Credential Service** to create credentials for print, which are pushed to WebSub for printing systems to consume ✅.
- A **Notification Service** sends email/SMS to the applicant ✅.
- The processor connects to the external **Manual Adjudication System** over a queue; what is sent is policy-driven and its specification is similar to an ABIS identify request ✅.
- It calls **Key Manager** to decrypt packets and encrypt information, uses **Masterdata Service** to validate the centre, machine and user, connects to a **virus scanner** at packet intake, and every stage calls **Packet Manager** to read packet contents ✅.

Two design decisions in that list deserve to be read as decisions. The first is that **quality gating happens before deduplication and uses a vendor SDK**, which means a country's choice of quality-scoring SDK affects its false-positive and false-negative profile before the ABIS ever sees a template. The second is that **the Manual Adjudication System is built by the system integrator, not by MOSIP** ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication") — the platform defines the interface and the queue, and hands the actual human-decision application to the country. That is modularity in its most consequential form: the platform takes responsibility for *routing* the difficult case and explicitly does not take responsibility for *judging* it.

### 4.4 The ID Repository

The ID Repository is the identity store, and its documented services are **Identity service** (stores, updates and retrieves identity information; retrieves and updates UIN status), **VID service** (creates and updates Virtual IDs mapped against a UIN, and their status), **Credential service** (creates credential data for partners), **Credential Request Generator** (creates credential-issuance requests), **Credential Feeder** (feeds existing UIN/VID identity information to a newly deployed IDA instance) and **Salt generator** (a one-time job populating the salts used to hash and encrypt data, which must be run before deploying the Identity and VID services) ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/id-lifecycle-management/identity-issuance/id-repository).

Three details in that list carry weight. The Identity service uses the server-side **Biometric SDK to extract templates** from provided biometric data ✅ — so template extraction happens inside the repository's trust boundary, not only at enrolment. The **Salt generator** is a one-time, must-run-first job whose output is stored in `uin_hash_salt` and `uin_encrypt_salt` ✅ — meaning the hashing of the identifier is keyed, and losing or rotating that material is an operationally serious event. And the **Credential Feeder** exists specifically to seed a *newly deployed IDA instance* with past identity data ✅, which is the documented acknowledgement that IDA instances are multiple and mutable — a fact Section 4.5 develops.

The repository page also states the sovereignty property directly: the ID Repository supports **data sovereignty, with data remaining within the country's borders** ✅ (docs.mosip.io, id-repository). That is a design claim about the intended deployment topology, and its honesty depends entirely on where the deployment actually runs — which is Section 8's subject.

### 4.5 ID Authentication: the deliberately separable half

ID Authentication is described in the docs in one sentence that is easy to skim past and hard to overstate: "ID Authentication is built as an independent service that can be seeded with data for authentication by any system, **including MOSIP**," and "we can have multiple IDA modules running from a single issuer" ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/id-lifecycle-management/identity-verification/id-authentication-services). Several consequences follow.

The authentication layer is *separable from the issuance layer*. A national system could run MOSIP's issuance pipeline and a different authentication service; or run IDA over an identity system that is not MOSIP's. The docs consistently describe the IDA module itself as an **Online Verification Partner** in the ecosystem ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners) — that is, the project treats its own authentication component as one instance of a role, not as the system. Multiple IDA instances from one issuer are supported ✅, and the Credential Feeder exists precisely to seed them ✅.

Mechanically, IDA's documented services are the Authentication Service (UIN/VID, one or more auth types), the KYC Authentication Service (eKYC over UIN/VID with one or more auth types), the OTP Request Service, Internal Authentication and Internal OTP (used by Resident Services, Registration Processor and Registration Client), and the Authentication Transaction History Service ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ID Authentication services). Credential data reaches IDA by a documented callback: the ID Repository requests a credential on UIN insertion/update or VID creation; the Credential Service creates it and uploads it to Datashare; the Datashare URL is pushed to IDA over WebSub; and IDA's credential-issuance callback downloads and stores it in the IDA database ✅ (same page). The docs also note the specific operational failure that callback creates — a person with a valid UIN may fail authentication because the credential has not yet reached the IDA database during high registration volume — and the platform's answer is an optional **Authentication Error Eventing** feature (plugin, Kafka-topic based, IDA 1.2.1.0) that lets a subscriber detect these errors and trigger on-demand template extraction ✅ (same page). That is a good example of the platform documenting its own eventual-consistency seam rather than pretending it away.

One design boundary worth stating because Section 11 leans on it: the docs warn that "ABIS is used for 1:N deduplication. For 1:1 authentication, Biometric SDK is used. MOSIP does not recommend using an ABIS for 1:1 authentication" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page). Deduplication and authentication are therefore *different technical problems with different components* — even though both involve matching a biometric. Conflating them is treated in Section 13(e) as a failure mode in its own right.

### 4.6 Where "modular" is a claim rather than a guarantee

Modularity is the project's central adjective, and the honest analysis separates three things it could mean.

**Where modularity is verifiably real.** The pluggable roles are interfaces, not marketing. ABIS is external, connects only over message queues, and is exchanged as a role with its own API specification, its own testing kit and its own partner type ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS and partners pages; github.com/mosip/abis-testing-kit). The Manual Adjudication System is built outside MOSIP ✅. Biometric devices are a partner category (Device Provider, FTM Provider) ✅. The technology-stack page states outright that "in deployments, many components can be swapped for equivalent open-source or commercial alternatives, based on your architecture and operational needs" ✅ (docs.mosip.io technology-stack page). The registration client can be the thick desktop client *or* the Android client ✅. The authentication layer can be MOSIP's or anyone's ✅. Those are four independent, demonstrated seams, and they are the substance of the claim.

**Where modularity is real but narrow.** The credentials and the wallet are modular in the sense that components are separable — but they are still MOSIP's components (Inji, eSignet) unless a country replaces them, and the data model downstream of them is where interoperability actually gets decided (Section 10 hands that question off). Similarly, "open standards" is listed as a principle ✅, and the platform does build on OIDC for eSignet ✅ and on ISO biometric formats for capture ✅ — but the principle is a direction of travel, not a certification, and the guide does not upgrade it into one.

**Where "modular" is a claim rather than a guarantee.** The strongest form of the claim is that a country can take *only what it needs* — and the docs' own deployment guidance softens this: "Apart from these installation models, countries can adopt a model of their choice" ✅ (docs.mosip.io/deploymentnew/getting-started) is permissive, but the platform's own installer path is a bundled V2/V3 installation (§8), the shared components (Key Manager, Masterdata, WebSub, Datashare, Audit Manager, the databases) are *common infrastructure* that most modules assume, and the partner-policy model means the *data-access* decisions live in one central policy engine ✅. In other words: the *code* is modular; the *assembly* is a system. A country adopting MOSIP gets a set of separable components bound together by shared infrastructure and a single policy authority — which is a different proposition from "pick three modules and go."

None of that is dishonest of the project, which does not claim otherwise. It is the honest reading of what "modular" buys: the ability to change vendors, swap the ABIS, replace the wallet, and exit a bad system-integrator relationship without re-writing national infrastructure. That is a real and rare property. It is not the same as saying the system is a loose bag of interchangeable parts, and a reader who assumes it is will be surprised at the first integration.

## 5. The Identity Lifecycle

### 5.1 The framing the docs use

The project groups the lifecycle into four documented categories: **Identity Issuance**, **Identity Verification**, **Identity Management** and **Support Systems** ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/id-lifecycle-management). Identity Issuance "encompasses the set of modules responsible for the creation and assignment of unique identities," orchestrating the end-to-end process "from the collection of demographic and biometric data, through validation and processing, to the final generation of a unique identity number (UIN)"; its sub-modules are Pre-registration, Registration Client, Android Registration Client, Registration Processor and id-repository ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/id-lifecycle-management/identity-issuance). Identity Verification covers ID Authentication; Identity Management covers Resident Services, the Resident Portal, ID Schema and Identifiers; Support Systems covers Administration, the Partner Management Portal and Reporting ✅ (docs.mosip.io, identity-management and support-systems pages).

That taxonomy is clean, but it is organised by *module*, and this guide's job is to show the lifecycle as a *state machine over one person's identity*. So the sections below walk the six states in order of a resident's life: enrolment, deduplication, issuance, authentication, update, deactivation.

### 5.2 Enrolment

Enrolment begins, optionally, online. Pre-registration lets a resident enter basic demographic data and book an appointment ✅ (docs.mosip.io, overview). Then comes the part that makes this a national-scale programme rather than a software rollout: the registration centre.

The project's field-operations guidance is the single best piece of evidence in the entire corpus that enrolment is a physical, logistical, human process, and it is worth describing in the project's own detail ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/setup/cookbook/field-operations). A registration centre is documented as comprising, scaled to expected throughput: one or more registration stations — "typically three registration kits — with one extra laptop available as a hot standby"; biometric devices and a printer/scanner attached to each registration workstation; "a trained registration operator, and a registration supervisor, present at all times"; a dedicated print station for ID cards with its own colour printer, not shared with the registration stations; sufficient seating in a waiting area; "a tokenisation mechanism for batching walk-in residents into registration slots"; handouts explaining the flow; posters at the entrance about what may and may not be carried in; **pre-printed consent forms**; power backup sized to keep all kits running through any expected outage window; security measures agreed with the country authority; a large screen in reception playing an explainer video; a helpdesk with one or two dedicated staff; and staff identification through a uniform or badge ✅.

The documented staffing table, role by role, is: registration operator (one per workstation), standby registration operator, supervisor (present whenever the centre is open), standby supervisor (particularly in the first weeks), helpdesk officer (one or two), and print-station operator ✅. Per-kit preparation includes matching the laptop to specification, applying Windows updates and *then disabling updates* until the pilot closes "to avoid mid-day reboots," installing and verifying antivirus without conflicting with the Registration Client, removing unrelated applications, connecting and testing an external monitor for the resident, connecting *powered* USB hubs, registering each biometric device with its vendor so the registration does not expire mid-pilot, installing SBI L0 (registration) and SBI L1 (authentication) software with drivers, testing print and scan, stocking paper and a spare toner cartridge, and provisioning a photo booth with a white background, adjustable focus light and a marked, camera-aligned seating position ✅.

On modes, the guidance documents both **online** and **offline** registration. Online: the Registration Client talks to the central environment in real time over the country network or VPN; residents may pre-register and complete biometric capture at the centre; used where connectivity is reliable for the whole registration window. Offline: the client captures locally and packets sync when connectivity returns; used where connectivity is intermittent or absent, or as a fallback when the online path degrades — and "operators must understand sync windows, packet status and the helpdesk steps for stuck packets" ✅. The guidance then makes a recommendation that is really an admission about how much of this problem is human: "**Exercise both modes deliberately.** Schedule at least one full day of offline registration at one Center, even if connectivity is good. The point is to build muscle memory for the offline run-book before scale-up exposes the country to it for real" ✅.

The documented best-practice section closes the loop on quality and states the causal chain this guide will come back to in Section 6. Operators are told that "biometric quality is the single largest determinant of authentication success later in the resident's life with the credential"; to take multiple captures and keep the best; to watch the on-screen score and aim above **40** and redo anything below threshold; to watch for smudged or light fingerprints and half-closed eyes. And the guidance states the consequence outright: "It is always better to spend an extra two minutes at the kit than to send a low-quality packet downstream. **Low-quality biometrics translate directly into ABIS false positives, manual adjudication backlogs and authentication failures after issuance**" ✅ (emphasis added). That sentence, from the project's own field guidance, is the bridge from this section to Section 6 — the pipeline's accuracy problem begins at the kit, with a person, a device and a minute of care.

Exception capture is documented as a supervised act: show the resident which biometric is being marked an exception, in their language; make sure the physical reason is visibly captured in the exception photograph; and "get a supervisor to co-sign exception captures so the audit trail is clean" ✅.

### 5.3 Deduplication

The packet, once submitted, is validated and processed by the Registration Processor, and the step that defines the platform is deduplication — the comparison of the individual's details against everything already stored, in two stages, with a human decision at the ambiguous edge ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication"). Section 6 treats this as its own subject because it is the thesis-critical machinery; for lifecycle purposes the three facts that matter are these. Deduplication is a **gate**, not a check: a confirmed match rejects the packet. It **always includes biometric 1:N search** regardless of what the earlier stages concluded. And it can **end in a human decision** rather than an automatic one, because the borderline case is routed to a Manual Adjudication System ✅.

### 5.4 Issuance

If the packet survives — validation, quality gating, deduplication and any adjudication — the Registration Processor drives UIN generation and the ID Repository writes the record. The documented pieces of issuance are the Identity service storing the demographic record and the references to biometric and document files held in the Object Store, the kernel ID generator minting the UIN, WebSub publishing events about UIN updates, and the Credential Request Generator producing the credential-issuance request ✅ (docs.mosip.io, id-repository; registration-processor overview). Then the credential path: the Credential Service produces credential data for print and for partners, and the Printing (Credential Requestor) stage pushes it to WebSub for printing systems ✅ (docs.mosip.io, registration-processor overview; id-repository). Finally notification — email or SMS to the applicant ✅.

The physical artifact still matters. The documented field flow ends with the operator printing an acknowledgment slip, explaining it, asking the resident to note the registration ID (and to photograph the slip if they have a smartphone), recording the RID, name and phone number manually in an operator notebook "for fall-back reference," telling the resident when and where to collect the card, and explaining the notifications they will receive ✅ — and telling them to come back to the centre immediately if a failed-registration notification arrives ✅. A national digital identity programme, in its own operating manual, still runs on paper backups and a phone number.

### 5.5 Authentication

Authentication follows issuance: a resident's credential data is held by the IDA instance, and a relying party — the documented bank case is the Authentication Partner / Relying Party ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners) — calls the authentication service with the UIN or VID and one or more factors. The documented factor set is demographic, biometric, OTP and the eKYC variant ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ID Authentication services). The platform's design intent around this step is selective: the permanent identifier need not be shared (Virtual ID), each relying party receives a privacy-enabled token to prevent cross-transaction profiling, and consent determines which attributes a partner can receive ✅ (docs.mosip.io, privacy-and-security; partners). Those properties are the project's own design statements and are marked as such; what a *deployment* actually does with them is a matter for its policy configuration (§8).

### 5.6 Update

Updating an identity is a first-class flow, not an administrative afterthought: the ID Repository's Identity service retrieves and updates UIN status, the VID service creates and updates Virtual IDs and their status, WebSub publishes UIN-update and auth-type-status events, and the Registration Processor's update flow calls IDA's Internal Authentication Service to authenticate the applicant ✅ (docs.mosip.io, id-repository; registration-processor overview). Resident-facing, the docs group Resident Services and the Resident Portal under Identity Management, described as APIs and a reference UI for managing resident data and monitoring ID usage ✅ (docs.mosip.io, identity-management). Biometric re-enrolment on update is what keeps the 1:N gallery current, which is why a stale gallery is a slow-burning operational issue rather than an event.

### 5.7 Deactivation

The lifecycle has an end, and the platform models it explicitly. The Registration Processor's documented responsibilities include activating *or deactivating* the applicant's UIN in the ID Repository ✅ (docs.mosip.io, registration-processor overview). The richest primary evidence for how deactivation actually works at the boundaries comes from the MOSIP–CRVS integration documentation ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/interoperability/integrations/mosip-crvs-integration). The documented standard flows are birth registration and UIN issuance, death registration and identity-status update, and demographic data updates. The documented *exceptional* scenarios are: fraudulent birth registration, triggering a **national ID deactivation request from CRVS**; **reactivation of a deactivated national ID**; and a fraudulent death case, triggering **reversal of the death flag** ✅. The page states plainly that these exceptional scenarios "involve manual verification processes and are not fully automated" ✅.

That is the direct evidence for this guide's thesis, and it deserves to be read slowly. The identity system does not merely *receive* facts from civil registration — it reaches back into it. A death registration can deactivate an identity; an allegation of a fraudulent birth registration can trigger deactivation; a fraudulent death can be reversed. **An identity system is therefore not a database of who exists; it is a set of procedures for deciding who stops existing, and who gets to reverse that.** The mechanics of that are manual, policy-driven and, by the project's own account, not automated ✅. Everything a bank depends on when it verifies a customer's identity — and every protection a person depends on when a state's record of them is wrong — runs through that seam.
## 6. Biometric Deduplication

### 6.1 The 1:N problem, stated plainly

Deduplication is the platform's defining technical task, and it has a shape that makes it unlike almost any other matching problem in financial services. A bank verifying a customer's face against their passport photo solves a **1:1** problem: two images, one claimed identity, one yes-or-no. A national identity platform proving that a new applicant is *not already in the database under another name* solves a **1:N** problem: one set of biometrics, searched against every record the system has ever stored, with the requirement that the search find any match and — critically — that a match, once confirmed, is disqualifying ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication"; ABIS page).

The consequences of the 1:N shape are worth spelling out because they explain most of the platform's design. The *cost* of the search grows with the population. The *error* profile is different: a 1:N search is judged by whether a genuine duplicate is found (and what comes back when it is not), not merely by whether a claimed match verifies. And the *stakes* are asymmetric in a specific way: a missed duplicate corrupts the register forever (two records for one person, or one record that has absorbed two), while a false hit denies a unique person their identity. A platform therefore cannot simply tune one threshold and be done; it has to decide, per modality and per policy, where on that trade-off it wants to sit — and that decision is the adopter's, as Section 6.4 shows.

### 6.2 The two-stage process, in the project's own terms

The documentation describes deduplication as two stages, with an important asymmetry between them ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication"). The whole section that follows is drawn from that page, live in the 1.2.0 documentation set as a 1.1.5-era page, and it is quoted rather than paraphrased because the details are the point.

**Stage one — demographic deduplication.** The system compares some of the resident's demographic data — **name, date of birth and gender** — against the data already present, by "adding Person A's hashed demographic details (i.e. name, date of birth and gender) in the database" and comparing them against all records ✅. Note *hashed*: the comparison runs on hashed demographic fields, not raw ones. If potential matches are found, the resident's biometrics are sent to the ABIS to *confirm* whether the biometrics also match, over the specific candidate set — a documented operation called a **gallery match** ✅. If the ABIS confirms a match within that gallery, **MOSIP rejects the packet**; if not, the packet moves on to biometric deduplication ✅.

The page is also careful about *registration state*, and this detail is more important than it looks. When demographic comparison turns up Person B, C and D: if Person B's registration *failed*, B is not treated as a potential match; if Person C's registration is *not complete* — no UIN generated yet — C is not treated as a potential match for the purposes of demographic deduplication; and only if Person D's registration is *completed with a UIN* is D considered a potential match ✅. The system, in other words, reasons about the completeness of other people's records while deciding this person's uniqueness.

**Stage two — biometric deduplication, which always runs.** The docs are explicit and the emphasis is theirs: "**Any Packet irrespective of it has gone through demographic de-duplication or ABIS gallery match, will have to go through the biometric de-duplication stage**" ✅. This is the design's central commitment — no shortcut, no early exit that skips the 1:N search. The documented flow is: MOSIP sends an **insert** request to the ABIS to insert the resident's biometrics (over the MOSIP-to-ABIS queue); the ABIS processes it and replies on the ABIS-to-MOSIP queue; MOSIP then sends an **identify** request for a **1:N match against all records**; the ABIS returns a list of potential matches ✅. The state logic differs slightly from stage one: if a matched registration was **rejected**, it is not a potential match; if it is **under processing**, it *is* treated as a potential match; if it is **completed with a UIN**, it is a potential match ✅. Finally — and this is the human seam — "the list of Potential Matches for Person A are sent to Manual Adjudication System to take the final decision" ✅.

**Manual adjudication.** "When biometric duplicates are found in ABIS, MOSIP system sends a request for Manual Adjudication to the Manual Adjudication System via a queue. The system integrator can build the Manual Adjudication System, which would be listening to the MOSIP-to-ManualAdjudication queue for any Manual Adjudication requests and send a response back in the ManualAdjudication-to-MOSIP system after verifying the data" ✅. The data sent is "driven by a policy defined in MOSIP and the specification is similar to ABIS identify request" ✅. The documented response structure carries `returnValue`, a `candidateList` with a `count` and per-candidate `referenceId`, and an optional `analytics` block per candidate containing `primaryOperatorID`, `primaryOperatorComments`, `secondaryOperatorID` and `secondaryOperatorComments` ✅ — two independent operators, with their comments, is the audit trail of a two-person review.

Read the whole section as one narrative and the platform's actual posture becomes clear: **MOSIP automates everything up to the point where a person's existence is genuinely in question, and then it hands the question to a human whose work it did not build and whose application it does not supply.** The Manual Adjudication System is a partner role (documented as its own type, "Providers of Manual Adjudication Systems (MAS); enrollment data is shared with MAS") ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners). That is the most honest thing in the entire corpus, and the reader should hold onto it: the platform's designers understood that the last step of "is this person unique?" is not a computable question.

### 6.3 The ABIS contract, and what it never learns

The ABIS is external, and the platform's relationship with it is defined by a published API specification and a small set of operations. The documented operations are **Insert, Identify, Delete, Ping, Pending Jobs, Reference Count** ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS API page). All ABIS operations are "via a message queue and are asynchronous" ✅. The biometric images exchanged are individual (segmented) fingerprints, left and right iris images, and a face image ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page). Communication is one-directional in architecture: MOSIP's ABIS Middleware sends to an inbound queue address and receives from an outbound queue address ✅.

Two privacy properties are stated by the docs in unusually direct language, and both are worth quoting exactly. First: "**The ABIS system never learns about residents' identities. Any Personally Identifiable Information (PII), such as demographic details or an applicant's AID (application ID), is not shared with the ABIS system. Internally, MOSIP maintains a mapping between the ABIS-specific reference ID and the AID of the resident**" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page). Second, on storage: "the ABIS must get biometric data from referenceURL, process it and store it locally within the ABIS reference database," and the `referenceId` is the mapping key between MOSIP and ABIS ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS API page). So the ABIS holds biometric templates keyed by an opaque reference, and the identity behind that reference stays in MOSIP. That is a genuinely good separation and it is the project's own design statement, marked as such.

The deployment requirements reinforce it: "ABIS is recommended to be deployed in the same secure zone (military zone) where the registration processor is deployed," and "ABIS system is not recommended to connect to any external network" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page). Finally, the interface exists to be tested independently: "The interface may be tested using the ABIS testing kit" ✅, and a public repository (`github.com/mosip/abis-testing-kit`) exists ✅. A pluggable interface with a published specification, a testing kit and a stated non-requirement for network exposure is what "vendor-neutral ABIS" looks like in practice.

### 6.4 The one published error metric, quoted precisely

This is the section where the guide's discipline matters most, so here is the entire published record on ABIS accuracy, and nothing but it.

The only numerical error-related specification this pass located is in the **ABIS API specification, spec version 0.9, published date February 05, 2021** (revision history going back to May 2020) ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS API page). It describes a parameter called **`targetFPIR`** — a target false-positive identification rate — and the framing around it is the load-bearing part. The specification states that it is "an example and not a prescribed flag," that these "are optional attributes for MOSIP during an identify request," and that "MOSIP expects the adopters to define these parameters based on the accuracy expectations and workflow requirements" ✅. It defines `targetFPIR` as "**the error rate at which identification requests are expected to return a non-empty candidate list**," and gives the conversion:

```
round (-10 * log10 (target false positive identification rate))
```

with the accompanying example table ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS API page):

| Target false-positive **identification** rate | `targetFPIR` value |
|---|---|
| 1 in 1,000 | 30 |
| 1 in 10,000 | 40 |
| 1 in 100,000 | 50 |

**What this number is, stated exactly.** It is a **configuration example**, published in February 2021, in an interface specification, offered as an illustration of how to set a parameter. It is *not* a measured error rate for any deployment. It is *not* a project guarantee. It is *not* a false-accept rate for identity verification, and it is not a false-reject rate. Its defined meaning is narrow and specific: the rate at which an *identify request is expected to return a non-empty candidate list* — that is, how often the 1:N search comes back with something for a human to look at rather than with nothing. That is a statement about the *loading of the manual-adjudication queue and the risk of a missed duplicate*, not about whether a person is correctly recognised at a bank's counter. A reader who carries "1 in 100,000" away from this paper as "MOSIP's error rate" will have misread a configuration table as a performance report, and that misreading is exactly the sort of thing this guide exists to prevent.

**What is not published, and is therefore not printed here.** This pass found **no false-accept rate (FAR), no false-reject rate (FRR) and no false-non-match rate for any MOSIP deployment anywhere** — not on the project site, not in the documentation, not in the code repository, and not in any dated press statement reached this pass ❌. It is worth being precise about what that absence does and does not mean. It does *not* mean the platform performs badly; it means **no such figure was retrievable this pass**, and a guide that invents one — or that quietly reuses the `targetFPIR` table as though it were one — would be fabricating evidence. The absence is recorded in Section 16 with the same care as the verified facts, and the claims audit in Section 15 carries a row recording that no biometric-accuracy figure was printed without a primary source.

The practical upshot for any reader is that **the accuracy of a MOSIP-based system is a property of the deployment, not of the platform**. The platform supplies the pipeline, the interface, the parameters and the manual-adjudication seam. The country's policy choices (which thresholds, which modalities, which quality floors), its ABIS vendor's algorithms, its biometric devices, its operators and its adjudicators supply the actual error profile — and the project says as much when it tells adopters to define the parameters "based on the accuracy expectations and workflow requirements" ✅. That is an unhelpful answer for anyone who wants a number and a correct answer for anyone who wants to understand the system.

### 6.5 What the capture side fixes, before matching even begins

Matching quality is bounded upstream by capture quality, and the platform specifies that side in detail. The documented biometric image specification is: fingerprints per **ISO 19794-4:2011** (Annex B1 for registration devices, Annex B2 for authentication devices), minimum **500 native DPI**, JPEG 2000 (lossless at registration, lossy or WSQ up to 15:1 at authentication), grayscale 8 bits, quality score **NFIQ v2.0** for registration devices and **NFIQ v1.0** for authentication devices, with the change history recording the removal of NFIQ v2.0 from fingerprint *authentication* devices on 7 May 2021 ✅ (docs.mosip.io, biometric specification page). Iris: **ISO 19794-6:2011** Annex B, minimum iris diameter 210 px at registration and 150 px at authentication ✅. Face: **ISO/IEC 19794-5:2011**, 1080p camera with 90° field of view or above at registration, ICAO full-frontal quality at registration with ±5° rotation, 24-bit RGB and a white background, and a documented **exception image specification** — "Full Frontal with FACE features, two palms next to the face, waist up photo," 60 mm × 40 mm ✅. Biometric data is wrapped in a **CBEFF XML** container ✅. The page notes twice, in both the fingerprint and iris tables, that "MOSIP adopters can change this if needed" ✅.

Two things follow. First, the exception image specification is the specification *for the people the biometrics will not capture* — the two palms beside the face and the waist-up frame are how the platform makes a person with no usable fingerprints or irises documentable at all ✅ (and see the inclusion page's account of marking a modality as an exception and capturing a photograph as evidence). Second, the numeric *quality-score thresholds* an operator sees on the registration client — the field guidance says "aim above 40" ✅ — are configuration, not platform constants, and the exact configuration values were **not re-verified this pass** ⚠. The guide prints the operational guidance (above 40) as the field document states it and declines to print configuration numbers it could not confirm.

### 6.6 The exclusion consequence, stated as a design property

Set the marketing aside and read the mechanics. When the pipeline concludes that an applicant's biometrics match an existing record — after a gallery match, after a full 1:N identify, and where applicable after two human operators have reviewed it — the documented outcome is that **MOSIP rejects the packet** ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication"). No UIN is issued. That is not a bug, a bias or an oversight; it is the platform working exactly as specified, because the platform's entire purpose is to guarantee that one person holds one unique identity.

The same pipeline has a second, less-discussed exclusion surface, and it is a design property too. The docs state that "**Face data is not sent to ABIS for deduplication**" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, privacy-and-security page). That is a deliberate privacy choice — keeping a modality out of the identification search — and it means the 1:N problem is solved with a reduced modality set, which is a trade-off between privacy and matching capability that the deployment inherits whichever way it cuts.

Now the honest reading. **Any system that decides uniqueness will deny uniqueness to someone, and the two ways it can be wrong are not symmetric.** A false rejection of a genuine newcomer (their biometrics resemble someone else's, and adjudication fails to catch it) denies a real person the identity that everyone else in the country holds — and, downstream, the bank account, the benefit, the school, the permit that the identity unlocks. A false acceptance of a duplicate (a real duplicate is missed) corrupts the register, and the corruption is discoverable years later and expensive to unwind. The platform's design response to this asymmetry is visible in the architecture: the second stage always runs, borderline hits go to humans, and the response structure records two independent operator IDs ✅. The platform's design does *not* tell the country how to staff that adjudication, how quickly to decide, what evidence an adjudicator weighs, how a rejected applicant appeals, or what happens to the person in the meantime. Those are policy, and they are Section 12's and Section 13(a)'s subjects. **That the exclusion exists is a property of the design. Whether the exclusion is just is a property of the governance.** The guide takes no position on the second and is precise about the first.

### 6.7 Why this is the thesis-critical section

Section 1's short answer said the platform decides whether you exist, once. Section 6 is the mechanism by which it does, and the closing line of this guide is the same idea in one sentence. Note what the mechanism is *not*. It is not a storage problem — storing a hundred million identity records is a solved engineering task, and several industries do it. It is not a credential problem — that layer is well understood and lives in another guide. It is the **decision**: 1:N over a whole population, with a human at the ambiguous edge, producing a binary outcome that a person cannot easily reverse and that a whole economy then depends on. Everything hard about MOSIP — the manual adjudication queue, the exception path, the CRVS deactivation flows, the grievance-redressal line in the project's own principles, the failure modes in Section 13 — follows from the fact that this decision has to be made, has to be made once, and has to be defensible afterwards.

## 7. The Open-Source Question

### 7.1 What a state actually gets from MPL 2.0

The licence analysis in Section 2.3 is the technical content; this section is about the *strategic* content, which is simpler and more consequential than it first appears. A country adopting MOSIP acquires: the right to run the software in perpetuity, without a vendor's permission; the right to modify it, including extensively; the right to hire whoever it wants to modify and operate it; and the right to keep its own modifications proprietary *except* for changes to files that are themselves MPL-licensed ✅ (docs.mosip.io/license). The docs frame the reason the project chose this shape — MPL permits proprietary and differently-licensed modules to coexist in one repository "as long as MPL obligations are respected" ✅ — and that framing tells you the project wanted a commercial ecosystem to form around the platform. It did.

The strategic consequence is the one the project advertises: **no vendor lock-in and no licence fee.** The project states that its technology "can be freely used by any government or organisation to build a foundational digital ID system, under Mozilla's Public License 2.0," and describes "zero-cost platform adoption, licensing, and capacity-building" ✅ (mosip.io/mosip_project). The principles document gives the economic argument explicitly — competition is encouraged when a vendor-neutral, open platform is the core of an identity solution, which "helps bring down country costs and addresses the question of financial sustainability" ✅ (Country Engagement Principles PDF).

### 7.2 The sovereignty argument

The strongest version of the sovereignty argument is not "no licence fees." It is that an identity system is the kind of infrastructure a state should not be able to lose control of, and that open source is the only structure that guarantees it. The project makes this argument in its own voice: "Governments of adopting nations have sovereign control over their ID systems" ✅ (mosip.io/mosip_project), and the partner page states the position in operational terms: "Country governments have complete agency and ownership over the customisation of their national ID system, the choice of local System Integrator, their Automated Biometric Identification System (ABIS) providers, the kind of biometric modality involved in their national ID system, and the providers of all those biometric devices and solutions" ✅ (mosip.io/country_partners). The ID Repository, correspondingly, "supports data sovereignty (data remains within the country's borders)" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, id-repository), and the ABIS is recommended for deployment inside the same secure zone as the registration processor, off any external network ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page).

That is a coherent and, in this domain, unusually well-specified claim. Three properties align: the *code* can be taken and kept (MPL 2.0), the *suppliers* can be replaced (pluggable ABIS, devices, integrator, wallet), and the *data* can stay home (in-country deployment requirement). The docs' own deployment guidance reinforces the third with a permissive statement — "Apart from these installation models, countries can adopt a model of their choice" ✅ — while strongly recommending Kubernetes for manageability ✅ (docs.mosip.io/deploymentnew/getting-started).

### 7.3 The limits of the argument, honestly

Sovereignty is an outcome, not a property of a licence, and three limits should be stated plainly.

**First, the platform sets the interfaces even where it does not set the choice.** The project's position is that a country owns its ABIS provider, its biometric modality and its device suppliers ✅. That is true, and it is also true that every one of those choices must be made *within the interfaces MOSIP defines* — the ABIS API specification, the SBI device-security standard, the credential types, the partner-policy model, the registration-client packet format. A country that wanted an ABIS that did not speak the documented queue protocol would not be adopting MOSIP; it would be forking it. The docs are candid about this in the deployment chapter, which is the right place for candour. So the honest formulation is: **the platform is agnostic about who supplies a component and prescriptive about how a component plugs in.** That is the correct design for interoperability and an accurate description of where the constraint actually sits.

**Second, running it is the expensive part, and the licence does nothing about that.** "Zero-cost platform adoption" ✅ covers the code. It does not cover registration kits, biometric scanners, iris cameras, printers, power backup, connectivity, registration centres, operators, supervisors, helpdesk staff, adjudicators, system integration, hosting, and the helpdesk years after go-live. Section 8 is the deployment reality; Section 13(c) and 13(d) work the two failure modes where the money runs out mid-programme. A government that hears "free and open source" as "cheap national identity" has heard something the project did not say.

**Third, the project's own principles document names the risk that technical openness cannot address.** It warns that technology "can also work to concentrate power in the hands of identity issuers," and that it is "critical, therefore, to ensure that 'good' ID is adopted" ✅ (Country Engagement Principles PDF). The same document insists that "strong governance frameworks and a policy-making approach that engenders trust are also critical elements," and that "the most challenging task for a country looking to adopt an effective and inclusive foundational identity system is to ensure that law and policy move in tandem with the technology" ✅. Read plainly: open source protects you from a vendor. It does not protect you from your own government, your own adjudicators, or your own data-protection regime — and the project says so in its own document rather than implying that a permissive licence is a rights guarantee.

**A fourth limit, structural rather than principled.** An open-source platform in a low-adoption domain has a talent market problem: the pool of engineers who know the codebase is the ecosystem the project itself has to build. The "125+ Commercial Partners" figure ⚠ (mosip.io home, undated) is the project's own answer to that, and the marketplace exists as the matching layer ✅ — but a country betting on open source should understand that open code without a deep supplier market is cheaper to licence and harder to staff, and that the staff question is the one that bites in year three.

## 8. The Deployment Model

### 8.1 What a country physically installs

MOSIP is "deployed as a combination of microservices," a choice the docs justify on scalability, robustness, resilience and fault tolerance grounds, with services grouped into modules (Kernel, prereg, regproc and others) that "can be deployed independently with all its dependencies as per the business needs" ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/deploymentnew/getting-started). The release artifacts a country consumes are all public: **JARs** to Maven Central, **Docker images** to Docker Hub, **Helm charts** from a public repository (`helm repo add mosip https://mosip.github.io/mosip-helm`), **Kubernetes cluster scripts** in `k8s-infra`, and **deployment scripts** in `mosip-infra` that install the charts in the required sequence ✅ (same page). There are two supported installation models: **V2**, "a simple sandbox-based implementation with very low resources to help understand the working modules," and **V3**, "a scalable deployment with a service mesh, HA and other security protection," supported from v1.2.0.1-B1 ✅. The docs state that V2 is deprecated ✅, and add that beyond these, "countries can adopt a model of their choice," with Kubernetes or equivalent container orchestration recommended for manageability ✅.

The honest reading: a MOSIP deployment is a Kubernetes estate. That is not a detail a bank reader can skip, because it determines the skills, the vendor relationships and the operational posture the adopting state needs, and it is the reason the deployment guide's own page opens with a note that the version of the guide is "currently under revision and enhancement" ✅ — a small but real signal that the operational documentation is still maturing relative to the code.

### 8.2 The stack, so the reader can size it

The documented baseline technology stack (page for 1.2.1.0 and subsequent) includes: **Ubuntu Server 24.04**; **Java SE 21 / OpenJDK 21** as the language runtime; **Vert.x 3.9.13**; **Spring 6.1.x**; **Apache Camel 2.23.0**; **PostgreSQL 15** as the database with **H2** for development; **Keycloak 7.1.18** for identity and access management; **ActiveMQ** and **Kafka** for messaging; **Hazelcast** for distributed data; **MinIO** as the object store; **Nginx**; **Docker / RKE2 / Kubernetes** for orchestration; **Helm**; **Istio** for the service mesh; **Terraform**; **Prometheus / Grafana** for monitoring; **ClamAV** for the virus scanner wired into packet intake; and **BouncyCastle** for cryptography ✅ (docs.mosip.io, technology-stack page). Cloud infrastructure (Azure/AWS) is listed as a commercial option whose applicability "depends on deployment architecture" ✅. The page frames the whole list with the sentence already quoted in Section 4.6: "in deployments, many components can be swapped for equivalent open-source or commercial alternatives, based on your architecture and operational needs" ✅.

Two observations worth carrying forward. The stack is largely commodity open source, which is consistent with the "commodity computing" principle ✅ — nothing here requires exotic hardware or a proprietary database licence. And the presence of Istio, Prometheus/Grafana and Terraform in the baseline is the clearest evidence that operating MOSIP is a *platform engineering* job, not an application administration job.

### 8.3 The in-country requirement, and what it really means

The data-sovereignty property — "data remains within the country's borders" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, id-repository) — and the ABIS deployment guidance — same secure zone as the registration processor, no external network ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page) — together set the deployment topology: the identity store, the biometric matching engine and the processing pipeline are expected to be operated inside the adopting jurisdiction's own trust perimeter. That has three practical consequences a reader should notice.

**It is a hard infrastructure commitment.** In-country operation means the state (or its chosen operator) owns the Kubernetes estate, the database, the object store, the key management and the network. The cloud option is listed as commercial and architecture-dependent ✅; a fully in-country, air-gapped-ish topology is the design intent, and it is the more expensive of the two postures.

**It puts the key material in the country.** The Key Manager handles decryption of packets and encryption of information ✅, the Salt generator produces the salts used to hash and encrypt identity and VID data and must be run before the Identity and VID services are deployed ✅, and the IDA has its own key set generated at deployment ✅ (docs.mosip.io, id-repository; ID Authentication services, key generation). Losing that material, or mishandling the salt tables (`uin_hash_salt`, `uin_encrypt_salt` ✅), is an existential operational event. Sovereignty over keys is a real capability and a real liability, and a country should look at key custody the way a bank looks at its HSM estate.

**It means the country operates a 1:N biometric search infrastructure.** The ABIS is the adopting state's own deployment or its supplier's — recommended inside the secure zone, off external networks ✅. That is a system with substantial compute, storage and licensing-or-building costs of its own, and it is the single most expensive and most specialised component in the estate.

### 8.4 The operator model

The docs describe the assembly task in one line: "Typically, a System Integrator (SI) would assemble all the pieces to build a complete national ID solution," with all entities providing external components being MOSIP Partners ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners). The partner types are documented as: **Authentication Partner / Relying Party** ("Entities that use MOSIP for authentication like banks, telecom, Govt. institutes etc."); **Online Verification Partner** (authorised partners who host IDA to provide authentication services — and "even MOSIP's IDA module is an Online Verification Partner"); **Credential Partner** (printed ID cards, QR codes); **Device Provider**; **FTM Provider**; **Manual Adjudication**; **ABIS Partner**; and **MISP Partner** (network infrastructure/channel to connect authentication partners to the MOSIP system — broadband providers, for example) ✅.

The onboarding mechanics are documented in enough detail to show that this is a governed, certificate-based trust model, not an API-key formality. Authentication partners need a pre-defined policy, a trusted X.509 certificate with a CA chain, registration with a MISP and a MISP licence key, self-registration on the PMS portal, certificate upload, selection of a policy group and policy subject to MOSIP Admin approval, and only then an API key usable with the MISP key against the IDA system ✅. Partner onboarding is "required to be done on any fresh MOSIP installation" ✅ — including a sandbox, where each partner must be onboarded individually ✅.

The policy model is the part a bank should read twice. "The MOSIP Partner Policy establishes a structured framework for collaboration between MOSIP adopters and their partners and defines the rules for data access and sharing" ✅; a registered print provider receives data automatically via WebSub if designated under the print policy, whereas an authentication partner must actively call the authentication system ✅; "if eKYC is permitted, the partner may receive additional personally identifiable information (PII)" ✅; and access is granular per attribute — "one may access only a name while another may obtain both a name and a photograph" ✅. That is the mechanism by which a country decides how much of a resident's identity a bank gets — and it is a *policy* decision that a bank can influence but does not control.

### 8.5 The sandbox and the partner programme

Integration starts in one of two hosted environments, documented plainly: **Collab** (collab.mosip.net) is "our development integration environment that has QA-tested dockers deployed," used by partners and contributors to build on the platform or integrate with the QA-certified version of the latest code, with "regular nightly builds from engineering" deployed and used for continuous development ✅; **Synergy** (synergy.mosip.net) is "our stable environment where the latest released version of the MOSIP platform and applications are deployed for partners to integrate and test" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, sandbox details). The documented entry point for a prospective partner is a form ✅. The **MOSIP Partner Programme** exists to "help stakeholders connect with MOSIP, and become part of an ecosystem invested in building foundational digital ID systems that are trustworthy, secure, efficient, and interoperable while being customized to specific needs" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners).

For a bank considering integration, the documented route is therefore: join the partner programme, get into Collab, integrate against the QA build, move to Synergy for the stable release, onboard as an Authentication Partner (or work through an Online Verification Partner), and negotiate the policy that determines which attributes you may receive ✅. Note what that sequence implies: the bank's integration is *sanctioned* by the adopting country's partner-policy governance, not merely technical. The sandbox is where the technical work happens; the policy is where the actual permission happens.

### 8.6 The integration points that are not identity — CRVS

One more deployment-reality belongs in this section because it changes what the system *is*. The MOSIP–CRVS integration is documented as a first-class interoperability story: MOSIP integrates with Civil Registration and Vital Statistics systems so that identity spans a life, "starting from birth registration, through education and employment, to end-of-life verification," with the stated rationale that "when these systems operate in isolation, it leads to administrative inefficiencies, fragmented data, and challenges in service access" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, MOSIP–CRVS integration). The documented flows are birth registration and UIN issuance, death registration and identity-status update, and demographic data updates; the documented exceptional scenarios — fraudulent birth deactivation requests, reactivation, death-flag reversal — "involve manual verification processes and are not fully automated" ✅ (same page, and see Section 5.7).

The deployment consequence is significant and easy to miss: a country that integrates MOSIP with its civil registry has not merely added a data feed, it has **joined two state systems into one lifecycle.** Birth registration can create an identity; death registration can end one; a fraud allegation can deactivate one. That is the correct architecture for a foundational identity system and it is also the reason the governance questions in Section 12 cannot be waved away: the seam between them is where a person's legal existence is decided, and it runs between two agencies with their own mandates, budgets and error rates.

### 8.7 The roles a state must hire and keep

The documentation describes the operating workforce in enough places that it can be listed as a single roster, and the roster is the most useful planning artefact in the whole corpus because it is where "adopt an open-source platform" turns into a payroll. At the **registration centre**, the field-operations guidance names registration operators (one per workstation), a standby operator, a supervisor present whenever the centre is open, a standby supervisor for the first weeks of go-live, a helpdesk officer or two, and a print-station operator ✅ (docs.mosip.io field operations). Behind them, the work of a centre includes tokenising walk-in queues, checking consent forms, capturing and re-capturing biometrics against a quality threshold, co-signing exception captures, walking packets through a preview-and-submit step, logging RIDs by hand as a fallback, and uploading data typically twice a day ✅.

Beyond the centres, the estate implies roles the field document does not name but the architecture makes unavoidable. Someone operates the **Kubernetes estate** — the docs' baseline includes RKE2 or Kubernetes, Helm, Istio, Terraform and Prometheus/Grafana ✅ (technology-stack page) — which means platform engineers, not generalist administrators. Someone owns **key custody and the salt material**, because the Key Manager handles packet decryption and data encryption ✅ and the one-time Salt generator's output lives in `uin_hash_salt` and `uin_encrypt_salt` and must exist before the Identity and VID services are deployed ✅ (id-repository page); losing or rotating that material is the kind of incident that has no easy recovery. Someone operates or contracts the **ABIS**, which is the country's own deployment or its supplier's, recommended inside the secure zone and off external networks ✅ (ABIS page). Someone runs the **Manual Adjudication System** with the two-operator review the response structure models ✅. Someone runs the **partner-onboarding and policy function**, because every partner needs a certificate chain, a policy group, a policy and an Admin approval before they can call the authentication system ✅ (partners page). And someone owns the **civil-registration integration** and its exceptional, manual scenarios ✅ (section 8.6).

Read the roster as a single sentence and the honest conclusion is: **adopting MOSIP creates a standing institution, not a deployment.** The platform is free and the institution is not, which is precisely the gap that failure mode 13.4 describes — and the project's own principles anticipate it, calling for "planning for financial and operational sustainability without compromising accessibility" ✅ (Country Engagement Principles PDF).
## 9. The Banking and Fintech Angle

### 9.1 The documented bank-facing role

Start with what the platform documentation actually says a bank does here, because it is narrower than the marketing and more useful. The partner-type list defines **Authentication Partner / Relying Party** as "Entities that use MOSIP for authentication like banks, telecom, Govt. institutes etc." ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners). That is the whole of the bank's documented position in the core identity platform: a consumer of authentication and eKYC, onboarded through the partner-management services, governed by a policy that determines which attributes it may receive. The bank does not enrol residents, does not hold the identity record, and does not adjudicate duplicates. It asks a question and receives an answer.

The mechanics of that answer are documented at the level of *what is requested and what comes back*, and a bank engineer should read them as an API contract rather than a strategy: the Authentication Service authenticates a UIN or VID "using one or more authentication types"; the KYC Authentication Service requests eKYC for a UIN or VID "using one or more authentication types"; OTP is its own request service, with the OTP stored in the IDA database for validation ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ID Authentication services). Where the answer is delivered as a signed, verifiable artifact rather than a live API response, that is the credential layer, and it is Section 10's hand-off. The onboarding sequence for an Authentication Partner — X.509 certificate with a CA chain, registration with a MISP and a MISP licence key, self-registration on the PMS portal, policy-group and policy selection subject to Admin approval, then an API key usable with the MISP key ✅ — is documented in Section 8.4 and is the bank's actual integration path.

### 9.2 What this changes about onboarding and inclusion

The economics of onboarding change in one specific place. Today, a bank verifies identity by collecting documents and checking them: passports, national ID cards, utility bills, and increasingly a selfie. Every one of those artifacts is a claim that someone else made, presented by the person with an incentive to misrepresent it. A foundational identification answer is different in kind: it is the *issuing state* asserting, against its own register, that this person has a unique identity and that the person presenting matches it. Where the project's own use-case material demonstrates the pattern, the bank's form is pre-filled from the identity and tax credentials rather than typed from a document ✅ (docs.mosip.io, Collab loan application use case, and Section 10).

Two second-order effects follow, and they point in opposite directions. The first is **cost-to-onboard**: an API call replacing a document-collection-and-review workflow is cheaper per account, faster, and materially harder to defraud with a forged or borrowed document. The second is **what the bank still owes**: none of the bank's own obligations — customer due diligence, beneficial-ownership checks, sanctions and PEP screening, risk rating, ongoing monitoring, record retention, treating customers fairly — are discharged by the state saying "this person exists and this is their identity." The state answers *who*, not *whether*. The repo's existing material on that side is the right place to read it, and this guide cross-references it rather than re-deriving it: [private_banking_guide.md](../banking/private_banking_guide.md) for the KYC/EDD conventions, [aml_certifications_exam_content_guide.md](../banking/aml_certifications_exam_content_guide.md) for the CDD/AML body of knowledge, [mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md](../banking/mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) for the SG/MAS regulatory posture, and [financial_fraud_detection_at_scale_guide.md](../banking/financial_fraud_detection_at_scale_guide.md) for the detection layer that sits on top of an onboarded customer book. The verifiable-credentials mechanics — how an eKYC result is proved, revoked and selectively disclosed — are owned by [verifiable_credentials_guide.md](verifiable_credentials_guide.md) (§8 of that guide covers credential-based KYC).

Inclusion is where the platform's design intent and the bank's commercial intent should align, and the honest version of the claim is this. A population that previously could not satisfy a bank's documentary KYC bar — because it had no birth certificate, no utility bill in its own name, no address artefact the bank would accept — becomes addressable if a foundational identity exists and if the bank's policy permits it to rely on that identity for the verification step. That is the documented thrust of the project's inclusion mechanisms (the introducer route, biometric exception handling, in-home registration) ✅ (docs.mosip.io inclusion page) and of its own framing that the Fayda ID in Ethiopia lets registered individuals "open bank accounts, enrol in schools, obtain business permits, and access key services such as health care" ⚠ (mosip.io/country_partners, quoting a UNHCR publication of May 2024, project-reported and not independently verified this pass). The reverse case also has to be stated: **a foundational identity system expands the addressable market only for the people it successfully enrols.** The excluded — the person whose biometrics will not capture and whose exception path was not staffed, the person whose packet was rejected as a duplicate and who never won adjudication — are exactly the people financial inclusion programmes exist to reach, and they are now excluded from the bank's onboarding for a *new* reason rather than an old one. That is the failure mode in Section 13(a), seen from the bank side.

### 9.3 What the platform does and does not provide to a bank

Because this is where banks most often misread a platform like MOSIP, here is the boundary stated as two lists.

**What the platform provides to a bank.** Authentication of a resident against a UIN or VID with one or more factors ✅; an eKYC variant that can return additional PII where policy permits ✅; a policy-governed, attribute-level grant of exactly which fields the bank may receive ✅; a privacy-preserving token so the bank does not receive the permanent identifier and cannot correlate its customers across transactions ✅ (project design statement, docs.mosip.io privacy-and-security); an OTP channel usable on a feature phone ✅; a documented, certificate-based partner onboarding process ✅; sandbox environments to integrate against ✅; and — in the credential path — signed artifacts the bank can verify without calling the issuer, which is [verifiable_credentials_guide.md](verifiable_credentials_guide.md)'s subject.

**What the platform does not provide to a bank.** It does not make the onboarding decision, or carry any of the bank's regulatory obligations (§9.2). It does not guarantee that the person at the counter is who the record says — authentication answers "matches the record," and a record can be impersonated through the platform's own enrolment channels. It does not provide a credit score, an AML verdict, a sanctions result or a risk rating; the project's loan use case shows a credit score and eligibility *displayed by the bank's own portal*, not issued by MOSIP ✅ (docs.mosip.io, Collab loan application use case). It does not give the bank a right of access: the grant is the adopting country's partner policy, and the country can narrow or revoke it ✅. It does not offer service-level commitments, indemnity or recourse to a bank — there is no commercial relationship between a bank and the MOSIP project, and the deployment is operated by a state. And it does not remove the bank's own obligation to be able to explain, to a regulator, *why* it accepted an identity it relied on.

### 9.4 The dependency question

The last thing a bank's risk function should take from this guide is what a foundational identity does to its third-party risk register. A bank that builds onboarding on a national identity platform has taken on a dependency with properties it would not accept from any commercial vendor: the operator is a sovereign, there is no contract, there is no exit to a competitor (the identity is *the* national identity), an outage is a queue at every bank's counter simultaneously, and the accuracy and coverage of the system are matters of national policy rather than vendor performance. None of that is a reason not to use it — the alternative is often worse — but it belongs in the same assessment that covers a core banking provider or a payment scheme, and it needs an explicit fallback: whether the bank can onboard a customer if the identity service is unavailable, and for how long that is tolerable. Section 13(e) works the version of this failure mode where a bank has forgotten the difference between a foundational identity and an authentication service.

### 9.5 A bank's integration checklist, in the documented order

Strip the strategy away and the sequence a bank actually follows is documented, and it is worth writing down because none of it is obvious from the outside. **Join the ecosystem:** the entry point is the partner programme and the sandbox environments — Collab for integration against the QA-tested build with nightly engineering deployments, Synergy for the stable released version ✅ (docs.mosip.io sandbox details; partners). **Register as a partner:** the documented Authentication Partner path requires a trusted X.509 certificate with a CA chain, registration with a MISP and a MISP licence key, self-registration on the PMS portal with a partner ID, name and organisation name matching the certificate, certificate upload, and then selection of a policy group and policy subject to MOSIP Admin approval ✅ (partners page). **Obtain the calling credential:** on approval, the partner generates an API key used alongside the MISP licence key to interact with the IDA system ✅. **Confirm the attribute grant:** read the assigned policy for exactly which attributes the bank may receive, on the understanding that one partner may receive only a name while another receives a name and a photograph, and that eKYC may release additional PII where permitted ✅. **Choose the authentication factors:** the services available are UIN/VID authentication with one or more auth types, the eKYC variant, and OTP request ✅ (ID Authentication services). **Decide where verification happens for credentials:** live API calls to the authentication service, or signed credentials the bank verifies itself — the latter belongs to [verifiable_credentials_guide.md](verifiable_credentials_guide.md). **Rehearse the failure paths:** identity service unavailable, credential not yet propagated, record disputed, applicant enrolled through the exception path.

Two notes on that checklist that a bank's engineering and compliance functions should read together. First, the technical integration and the permission are deliberately separate steps: passing the sandbox proves the bank *can* call the service, and the policy approval is what determines *what it gets back* — and a policy is something the adopting country can change ✅. Second, the checklist produces a new artefact the bank's compliance function did not previously hold — a record that the bank's identity verification rested on a state's answer under a specific policy version — and the bank should store that artefact per customer from the first onboarding, because reconstructing it after a policy change is not possible.

## 10. The Credential and Wallet Layer, Mapped Not Restated

This is the one place where this guide and `technology/verifiable_credentials_guide.md` share a subject, so the mapping is deliberately one paragraph long and the hand-off is explicit. MOSIP's own modules in this layer are **Inji**, which the project describes as "a verifiable credentialing stack" with three parts — **Inji Certify** for issuance (supporting JSON-LD, SD-JWT and mDOC, with revocation management, ledger and credential status checks, and schema/credential-registry management), **Inji Wallet** for holding and presenting (a mobile app for Android and iOS, plus Inji Web for browser access and for users without smartphones), and **Inji Verify** for verification (scanning and validating credentials, checking validity, expiry and revocation, and integrating into a relying party) ✅ (docs.inji.io, retrieved 16 October 2026) — and **eSignet**, which the project describes as "an open-source, vendor-neutral digital identity authentication platform developed by MOSIP" built on OpenID Connect and functioning as both an authorization and a resource server, designed to operate standalone with any identity system or repository that supports attribute retrieval ✅ (docs.esignet.io, retrieved 16 October 2026), with the core engine being rewritten in Go per that documentation ("eSignet 2.0.0 does not include the Signup product or wallet-based login (wallet login is planned for a future release under OpenID4VP)") ✅ (docs.esignet.io). ## 11. The Comparison

### 11.1 How to read this section

This is a **structural, unranked** comparison. It says nothing about market share, adoption volume, vendor quality or which approach is better, and it deliberately prints no count from any source (for the reason given in Section 15). It compares *shapes*: how a modular open-source foundational platform differs from a centralised proprietary national scheme, and what a surrounding ecosystem has to provide in each case. "Centralised proprietary" here describes an architecture — one integrator, one supplier, closed interfaces, contractually bounded access — not any particular country, and no real deployment is named as an example anywhere in this section.

### 11.2 Structural differences

| Dimension | Modular open-source platform (the MOSIP shape) | Centralised proprietary scheme (the comparison shape) |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the code | The originating institution holds copyright (IIIT-B, on behalf of MOSIP ✅); the adopting state holds a perpetual MPL 2.0 right to run and modify it ✅ | A commercial supplier owns the code; the state holds a licence, usually time-bounded and use-restricted |
| Exit cost | Change the system integrator, the ABIS provider, the device suppliers and the wallet without re-writing the platform ✅ (documented pluggable roles) | Re-procure the whole system, migrate the data, re-certify the integration estate |
| Component choice | Pluggable by contract: ABIS, devices, integrator, modality, wallet ✅ | Fixed by the integration; substitutions are commercial negotiations |
| Where the accuracy lives | In the deployment's policy choices, ABIS vendor, devices and operators; the platform supplies parameters and says adopters must define them ✅ | In the supplier's algorithms and the contract's performance schedule |
| Where the talent lives | A community and a partner ecosystem that the governing project must keep alive ✅ | The supplier's delivery organisation |
| Failure recovery | Fork it, or replace the supplier; the code is available ✅ | Escalate through the supplier; escalation is a contract, not a build |
| Standardisation path | Open standards and public interfaces, with a stated principle of vendor and technology neutrality ✅ | The supplier's interface is the standard for that deployment |
| Data location | In-country by design intent — "data remains within the country's borders" ✅ | As the contract and the sovereign's rules allow |
| Cost profile | Low licence cost, high integration and operating cost (Section 7.3) | High licence and service cost, lower internal capability requirement |
| What the state must supply | The governance, the law, the enrolment logistics, the operator workforce, the adjudicators, the redress mechanism ✅ | The requirements, the budget and the oversight |

### 11.3 What the surrounding ecosystem must provide either way

The comparison above can obscure something more important than the differences: **the platform is a small part of a national identity system either way, and the ecosystem is the rest.** In both shapes the following must exist, and neither the code nor the contract supplies it.

- **Legal identity law and a data-protection regime**, without which the system has no lawful basis and no recourse ✅ (the project's own governance principle calls for "a comprehensive legal and regulatory framework").
- **An enrolment capability**: registration centres, kits, devices, operators, supervisors, power, connectivity and transport (Section 5.2) ✅.
- **An adjudication capability**: the Manual Adjudication System, its operators, its service levels and its evidence standards — built by the country's integrator, not the platform ✅.
- **A biometric matching capability (ABIS)** and the compute and storage it needs ✅.
- **A credential/credential-channels capability**: cards, QR codes, wallets, or a mix ✅ (and [verifiable_credentials_guide.md](verifiable_credentials_guide.md) for the digital end).
- **A relying-party ecosystem**: the banks, telecoms and agencies that make the identity *useful* ✅.
- **Independent oversight and grievance redressal** — the project's principles explicitly call for "independent oversight and adjudication of grievances" ✅ (Country Engagement Principles PDF).
- **Financial and operational sustainability planning** — the project's principles call for "planning for financial and operational sustainability without compromising accessibility" ✅ (same document).

Read that list against the failure modes in Section 13 and the point of this section becomes clear. The architecture choice determines *how replaceable the technical estate is*; it determines almost nothing about whether the programme works. A proprietary scheme with excellent logistics outperforms a modular platform with none, and the reverse is also true. **What the modular choice buys is a cheaper, more survivable recovery from a bad supplier — which is a real and important thing to buy, and is not the same as a working identity system.**

## 12. Governance, Privacy and Ethics

### 12.1 The frame this section takes, stated up front

This section is factual and attributed. **It takes no position for or against national identity systems, and it makes no comment on any country's politics, any government's conduct, or any jurisdiction's law.** Where a claim is the project's own design statement or its own governance principle, it is quoted and credited as such; where a question cannot be answered from a retrieved primary source, it is recorded as a question in Section 16 rather than answered by inference. No jurisdiction's data-protection or identity law is named anywhere in this guide, because no such text was consulted in this pass (Section 16, item 6).

### 12.2 The project's own governance position

The project publishes a governance framework, which is unusual and worth reading in its own words. The **MOSIP Country Engagement Principles** sets out three principle groups ✅ (mosip.io/program_partner/country-engagement-principles.pdf, retrieved 16 October 2026; cover and footer credit Pratiksha Trust). Under **Inclusion**: "ensuring universal coverage for individuals from birth to death, free from discrimination," and "removing barriers to access and usage and disparities in the availability of information and technology" ✅. Under **Design**: "establishing a robust, unique, secure, and accurate identity"; a platform "interoperable and responsive to the needs of various users"; "using open standards and ensuring vendor and technology neutrality"; "protecting user privacy and control through system design"; and "**planning for financial and operational sustainability without compromising accessibility**" ✅. Under **Governance**: "**safeguarding data privacy, security, and user rights through a comprehensive legal and regulatory framework**"; "**establishing clear institutional mandates and accountability**"; and "**enforcing legal and trust frameworks through independent oversight and adjudication of grievances**" ✅.

Three of those lines are the ones this section cares about, and they are the project's, not this guide's: the *sustainability* line, because it names the funding problem (Section 13(d)); the *comprehensive legal and regulatory framework* line, because it concedes that the code cannot supply the lawful basis; and the *independent oversight and adjudication of grievances* line, because it concedes that a system of this kind needs a redress mechanism outside itself. The document also frames the power question in unusually direct language: "historically, governments have wielded significant power in issuing formal credentials – the power to define what qualities constitute proof of identity, and the power to accept or reject a person's identity claims," and technology "can also work to concentrate power in the hands of identity issuers" ✅. It endorses the **Principles on Identification for Sustainable Development**, listing backers that include the ADB, the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation, the Center for Global Development, Mastercard, Secure Identity Alliance, GSMA, UNHCR, UNDP, the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa and the World Bank Group ✅. It cites a "McKinsey Digital Identity Report, 2019" for the observation that many developing-country digital ID programmes saw low adoption with loss of trust as a recurring theme ✅, and it argues that "the most challenging task for a country looking to adopt an effective and inclusive foundational identity system is to ensure that law and policy move in tandem with the technology" ✅.

That is a more candid governance document than a promotional site, and its candour is itself a data point: the project's own framework says the technology is the easy part.

### 12.3 Privacy as a design property, in the project's own statements

The platform documents a set of privacy and security properties, and they are listed here as the project's design claims rather than as verified deployment behaviour. On **privacy**, the docs list: minimal data with selective disclosure on a need-to-know basis; sensitive data protected (not stored or logged in clear form); **consent support — the user decides who can receive what credentials and what attributes**; **"No search on the database (You can find a record only if you know the ID)"**; clear segregation of biometric and demographic data; decentralised ID usage and data ("cannot profile based on usage"); Virtual ID so users are not limited to one permanent identifier; **privacy-enabled tokens** given to every relying party "to prevent profiling across transactions," with the permanent ID never shared; support for wallet-based decentralised ID issuance and usage; and **"Face data is not sent to ABIS for deduplication"** ✅ (docs.mosip.io/1.2.0/readme/principles/privacy-and-security, page last updated ~2 years before this pass). On **security**: encryption of data in flight and at rest; integration only with trusted applications; fraud avoidance by associating authentication with specific transactions; misuse prevention via user lock/unlock of authentication; Virtual ID and tokens to prevent identity theft; all outgoing data digitally signed and all incoming data signed by the entity; data to a relying party encrypted; **every record in the database protected with integrity**; centralised key management; and **all APIs protected with OAuth 2.0** ✅ (same page). The project's overarching framing is "security by design" and "privacy by intent" ✅ (docs.mosip.io overview).

Two of those properties deserve to be singled out for what they *mean*, not just what they say. "**No search on the database**" is a genuinely strong anti-surveillance property: you cannot discover who exists by browsing, only by already knowing an identifier. It is also, in an operational context, a real constraint — a helpdesk cannot look someone up by name, which is why identity recovery becomes a procedural design problem rather than a lookup. And "**face data is not sent to ABIS for deduplication**" is a privacy choice with an accuracy cost, as Section 6.6 noted: the 1:N search runs on a reduced modality set by the project's own design. Both are examples of the same pattern — a privacy decision that is also an engineering decision, and a deployment that inherits both halves.

### 12.4 Consent, and where the consent question actually bites

Consent is documented as a first-class platform feature: the user decides who receives which credentials and which attributes ✅ (docs.mosip.io privacy-and-security), and the principles document describes "a consent framework that lets the individual user choose what to share and when," which "is transparent, lets the user know what they have shared and when, and also allows the user to lock authentication features that they wish to restrict" ✅ (Country Engagement Principles PDF). The field-operations guidance goes further into the mundane reality: **pre-printed consent forms** are listed as a required item at every registration centre and must be filled in and signed ✅ (docs.mosip.io field operations).

Read those two facts together and the honest reading is this: **consent is a design property of the software and a procedural property of the enrolment channel, and the second is where it can fail quietly.** A consent framework that is well built into the platform still depends on a printed form, at a busy counter, in a language the person may read poorly, in a queue they may have travelled hours to join, with a queue of people behind them and their child on their hip. "Consent" in that setting is a real legal act and a thin informational one, and the documentation's own insistence on the printed form is an acknowledgement of how much the software cannot guarantee. That is not a criticism of the platform; it is a precise statement of where the platform's responsibility ends and the country's begins.

### 12.5 The exclusion risk, and the redress question

The exclusion risk has been established mechanically in Section 6.6 and needs no re-argument here: the platform rejects duplicate packets and requires an exception path for people whose biometrics will not capture ✅, and the exception path itself produces a *weaker* identity claim — a photograph captured as evidence in place of a biometric ✅ (docs.mosip.io inclusion page). The governance question that follows is the one the project concedes it cannot answer: who decides, on what evidence, with what service level, and with what recourse for the person. The project's principles name the mechanism a country is supposed to build — "independent oversight and adjudication of grievances" ✅ — and the platform's architecture names the seam where it matters: the Manual Adjudication System, built by the country's own system integrator, whose operators and their comments are the documented record of the close calls ✅ (Section 6.2).

The redress question has a second edge that is worth stating plainly and without advocacy, purely as an architectural observation. A foundational identity system sits upstream of almost everything else a person needs — the account, the benefit, the school, the permit, the SIM, the clinic. If the identity is wrong, every one of those fails, and the failure surfaces far from its cause: the bank's counter, the school's office, the clinic's desk, where nobody has the authority to fix the register. **The distance between where a bad identity decision is made and where its consequences are experienced is the central governance problem of a foundational identity system**, and it is a problem the technology cannot close by itself — only a redress procedure with teeth can. Whether a given country has one is a question about that country, and this guide does not answer it for any of them.

### 12.6 Who governs a platform of this kind

Three governance questions recur, and the honest answer to each in this pass is a mix of fact and gap.

**Who governs the software?** The code is owned and maintained by IIIT-B on behalf of MOSIP ✅; the trademark is registered by IIIT-B ✅; the licence is MPL 2.0 with per-repo hygiene, a DCO-enforced contribution process and per-release SBOMs ✅. What was **not** established this pass is the governance *structure* — board, steering committee, technical decision rights ✅/❌ (Section 16, item 5). A reader should treat that as a genuine gap in the public record retrieved, and note that the licence mitigates it: whatever the governance does, a country can keep running the code it has.

**Who governs a deployment?** The adopting state, by the project's own position: "Country governments have complete agency and ownership over the customisation of their national ID system, the choice of local System Integrator, their Automated Biometric Identification System (ABIS) providers, the kind of biometric modality involved in their national ID system, and the providers of all those biometric devices and solutions" ✅ (mosip.io/country_partners). The limit of that ownership is the limit stated in Section 7.3: the platform sets the interfaces, and the country chooses what plugs into them.

**Who governs the data?** The deployment holds it, in-country by design ✅, with key management, salts, per-record integrity and consent-based selective disclosure as the controls ✅. The residual question — the one no platform can answer — is what the *state* is permitted to do with it, which is law, not code, and which the project's own principles say must come from "a comprehensive legal and regulatory framework" ✅. This guide names no country's framework, because none was consulted in this pass, and it prints no jurisdiction's rule as though it had been.
## 13. The Failure Modes

Five entries, in symptom / cause / guardrail form. Each is grounded in a documented mechanism or a documented operational reality from earlier sections; none is a hypothetical invented for effect.

### 13.1 (a) A deduplication error with a real-world consequence

**Symptom.** A genuine first-time applicant is refused a UIN because the system concludes they are already registered. The refusal is not one decision but a chain: a gallery match or a 1:N identify returned their record as a candidate, and either the match was confirmed or the manual adjudication step confirmed the duplicate ✅ (docs.mosip.io, "Deduplication and Manual Adjudication" — a confirmed match rejects the packet). The person is now, in the register's terms, someone else. They cannot open the bank account, and they will discover the problem at the counter rather than at the registration centre that created it (Section 12.5).

**Cause.** Three documented causes compound. First, biometric capture quality, which the project's own field guidance ties directly to this outcome: "low-quality biometrics translate directly into ABIS false positives, manual adjudication backlogs and authentication failures after issuance" ✅ (docs.mosip.io field operations). A hurried, badly framed or smudged capture at a busy centre is the first link in the chain. Second, the matching regime itself: any 1:N threshold sits on a trade-off between missed duplicates and false hits, and the platform's own position is that the adopter sets the thresholds ("MOSIP expects the adopters to define these parameters based on the accuracy expectations and workflow requirements") ✅ (ABIS API page) — so the error profile is a policy outcome, not a platform constant. Third, the adjudication step: the decision is made by humans on evidence supplied through a policy-driven message, in a system the country's integrator built ✅, and a busy or under-specified adjudication queue is where a false hit survives review. Note also that **no published FAR or FRR exists for any deployment** ❌ (Section 6.4), so a country cannot forecast this rate from the platform documentation — it has to measure it in its own pilot.

**Guardrail.** Treat biometric quality as an operational control, not a training topic: the documented practices are multiple captures keeping the best, watching the on-screen score against the configured threshold, re-calibrating slap scanners on the vendor's schedule, and the project's own rule that "it is always better to spend an extra two minutes at the kit than to send a low-quality packet downstream" ✅ (field operations). Stand up the Manual Adjudication System with two independent operators per case and a documented evidence standard, because the platform's response structure already models two reviewers with their own comments ✅. Instrument the pipeline from day one so the country knows its own false-hit and missed-duplicate rates rather than inheriting someone else's. And build the complaint route before go-live, because the person who is wrongly refused needs a way to be found and fixed — and in a system with "no search on the database" ✅, a refused applicant cannot be looked up by name; the redress procedure has to be designed around that constraint.

### 13.2 (b) An enrolment channel that excludes someone

**Symptom.** A person presents at a registration centre and cannot be enrolled. Their fingerprints will not capture — worn, damaged, or simply not readable by the device in the available time — or they have a missing finger or eye ✅ (docs.mosip.io inclusion page, naming these cases). They have no documents, so the documentary route is closed too. Or they cannot come to a centre at all. The failure is quiet: no error message, no exception thrown, just an enrolment that does not complete, at a desk, in front of a queue.

**Cause.** Every one of these failures has a documented remedy, and the remedy is *configurable and staffable*, which is exactly why it fails. **Biometric exception handling** exists — "marking the biometric modality as an exception and capturing a photograph as evidence" ✅ — but it is a path that must be enabled and, per the documented procedure, supervised: the operator shows the resident which modality is being marked as an exception, ensures the physical reason is visibly captured in the exception photograph, and has a supervisor co-sign so the audit trail is clean ✅ (field operations). If that path is not switched on, or no supervisor is on shift, or the operator has not been trained on it, the person is excluded by an omission rather than by a rule. **The introducer concept** exists for people without formal documents, and it too is a configured, staffed capability ✅ (inclusion page). **In-home registration through the Android Registration Client** exists for people who cannot travel ✅ — and it requires a device, a trained agent and a scheduling process. And the *quality bar itself* is an exclusion surface: a minimum NFIQ quality score for a fingerprint capture is a necessary defence against downstream false positives (13.1) and an absolute barrier for someone whose ridge detail is simply gone.

There is a second-order version of this failure worth naming: the exception path produces a **weaker identity claim** — a photograph as evidence where a biometric was expected. A person enrolled through the exception route is documented in a way that differs from everyone else, and whatever that difference costs them later (an authentication modality they do not have, a matching capability the register cannot use for them) is the price of the inclusion mechanism ✅.

**Guardrail.** Treat the exception path as a first-class service with an owner, a script, a training requirement, staffed supervision every hour the centre is open, and a service-level metric that is reported alongside enrolments — not as a fallback the supervisor can handle "if it comes up." Staff the centres as the field guidance describes, with a trained supervisor "present at all times" and a standby supervisor "particularly for the first weeks of go-live" ✅. Deploy the Android client for in-home registration before a national scale-up, not after, since the people who need it are the ones a centre-based campaign will miss. Audit exception rates by centre; a centre with a suspiciously low exception rate is more likely to be hiding failures than to be fortunate in its population.

### 13.3 (c) A deployment that stalls

**Symptom.** The pilot works and the national rollout does not. Enrolment throughput plateaus, packets back up in processing, manual adjudication develops a queue that no longer clears, authentication failures rise among already-issued identities, and the programme's confidence visibly erodes in public. Nothing is broken; the system is simply behind.

**Cause.** Four documented mechanisms, and they arrive together. **Throughput is physical**: a centre with three registration kits, one hot-standby laptop, one printer per workstation, one dedicated ID-card printer, a supervisor, a helpdesk and a tokenisation queue for walk-ins has a hard ceiling that no server upgrade raises ✅ (field operations). **Connectivity is not uniform**: offline registration is designed for intermittent coverage, and the docs warn that "operators must understand sync windows, packet status and the helpdesk steps for stuck packets" ✅ — which is a skill, not a configuration. **The backend is eventual, not instant**: the documented Authentication Error Eventing feature exists specifically because a person can hold a valid UIN and still fail authentication while their credential has not yet reached the IDA database, "particularly during periods of high registration and UIN generation volumes" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ID Authentication services) — so scale-up can *manufacture* authentication failures in people who are correctly enrolled. **The matching engine is load-sensitive by construction**: 1:N search cost grows with the gallery (Section 6.1), so the ABIS gets slower exactly as the programme gets bigger, and the safest way to buy accuracy — a lower threshold, more candidates, more adjudication — makes the human queue worse. Finally, the deployment guidance itself is still in flux: the getting-started page carries a note that "this version of the guide is currently under revision and enhancement" ✅, and the V2 installation model is deprecated ✅, which is a real signal that the operational path is less settled than the code.

**Guardrail.** Follow the documented advice literally rather than treating it as boilerplate: "**exercise both modes deliberately** — schedule at least one full day of offline registration at one Center, even if connectivity is good. The point is to build muscle memory for the offline run-book before scale-up exposes the country to it for real" ✅ (field operations). Build and staff the Manual Adjudication System to a *forecast* adjudication volume derived from the chosen thresholds, not to the pilot's volume, and treat the adjudication backlog as the programme's leading indicator. Upload registration data at regular intervals during the day — the guidance suggests typically twice a day — "so back-end processing has time to keep up" ✅. Monitor the credential-propagation lag from ID Repository to IDA as a first-class metric, because the documented failure it causes is invisible until it hits a citizen at a counter. And plan the ABIS capacity against the *end-state* population, not the pilot's.

### 13.4 (d) A funding model that does not survive its pilot

**Symptom.** The platform is free and the programme is not. The pilot is funded, the enrolment campaign is funded, and then the operational bill arrives — hosting, the ABIS estate, key management, helpdesk, adjudicators, device refresh, connectivity, the staff who keep the run-book alive — and there is no budget line for year three, because the adoption case was written as a capital project.

**Cause.** "Zero-cost platform adoption" ✅ (mosip.io/mosip_project) is true and covers the code, which is the smallest line in a national identity programme. The project states the funding-relevant facts itself. The licence gives a country the right to run and modify without a fee ✅. The field-operations guidance describes a standing workforce — operators, standby operators, supervisors, standby supervisors, helpdesk officers, print-station operators — plus buildings, power backup, devices, printers and consumables, "present at all times" ✅. The deployment is a Kubernetes estate with a service mesh, monitoring and infrastructure-as-code ✅, which needs platform engineers on a payroll, not a project budget. And the ABIS, the single most expensive and specialised component, is the country's own deployment or its supplier's ✅. The project's own principles name the requirement explicitly: "**planning for financial and operational sustainability without compromising accessibility**" ✅ (Country Engagement Principles PDF). The failure is not that the money is unavailable; it is that a capital-shaped programme is being asked to fund an operations-shaped obligation, and the pilot's success makes the obligation permanent while the pilot's budget ends.

**Guardrail.** Write the sustainability plan before the pilot, with an explicit split between capital (kits, devices, centres, the initial estate) and recurring (hosting, ABIS, key custody, helpdesk, adjudication, device refresh, connectivity, staffing, security patching and upgrades). Price the recurring side from the field-operations document's own inventory ✅, and require a named budget owner for year three and year five, not a promise to find one. Where funding is donor-based, require the recurring commitment to be underwritten for the full identity lifecycle, because an identity is issued once and authenticated forever — the person enrolled in the pilot will still need the system in twenty years. And note the availability trap the project's own principle warns about: cutting operational cost to preserve financial sustainability "without compromising accessibility" is the narrow path, and the cheapest way to hit a budget is to understaff the exception path (13.2) or the adjudication queue (13.1) — which is a saving taken directly out of someone's access to identity.

### 13.5 (e) An integration that treats a foundational identity as an authentication service

**Symptom.** A relying party — most plausibly a bank (Section 9.4) — builds onboarding on the identity platform and then discovers what it has actually integrated. It assumed that "the platform says this person exists" was the same as "this person is verified, creditworthy, sanctions-clear, and safe to onboard." It treats a positive identity answer as a risk decision, and it has no fallback for the day the identity service is unavailable, the credential has not propagated, or the customer's record is under a status they never anticipated.

**Cause.** The confusion is structural, and the platform's own documentation quietly prevents it if read carefully. The documented bank role is *Authentication Partner / Relying Party* — a consumer of authentication and eKYC ✅ (docs.mosip.io, partners) — which answers *who*, not *whether*. eKYC is policy-gated: "if eKYC is permitted, the partner may receive additional personally identifiable information" ✅, and the attribute grant is the adopting country's policy decision, which it can narrow or revoke ✅. The docs also draw a boundary the integrator must not blur: "ABIS is used for 1:N deduplication. For 1:1 authentication, Biometric SDK is used. MOSIP does not recommend using an ABIS for 1:1 authentication" ✅ (docs.mosip.io, ABIS page) — deduplication and authentication are different problems with different components, and an integrator who has understood only one of them will build the wrong thing. And the platform's own loan use case shows the boundary in practice: the credit score and loan eligibility are displayed by the **bank's own portal**, using the bank's own logic, on data supplied by the identity and tax credentials ✅ (docs.mosip.io, Collab loan application use case) — MOSIP supplies the identity attributes, not the decision.

**Guardrail.** Separate the two questions in the design, the code and the process: identity assurance (does this person exist, and do they match the record — the platform's job) from risk decisioning and regulatory obligation (should we onboard them, and can we defend that decision — the bank's job, cross-referenced to [private_banking_guide.md](../banking/private_banking_guide.md) and [aml_certifications_exam_content_guide.md](../banking/aml_certifications_exam_content_guide.md)). Keep a documented fallback path for identity-service unavailability, with a defined tolerance, because there is no vendor relationship to escalate through (Section 9.4). Read the partner policy as a permission that can change, and design the integration so a narrowed attribute grant degrades rather than breaks. And above all, do not let a single bright green "verified" tick do the work of a risk assessment — the platform tells the bank who someone is, which is the beginning of onboarding, not the end of it.

## 14. The Cymbal Bank Worked Example

> **This section is design fiction and is labelled as such. Cymbal Bank does not exist; the jurisdiction below is fictional; and no real country is named as a MOSIP-based deployment anywhere in this example.** Cymbal Bank is the only institution in this worked example, and the only bank persona in this guide. Everything about the *platform* is drawn from the verified material in Sections 1–12; everything about the *bank* and the *country* is invented to exercise one decision.

### 14.1 The setting

Cymbal Bank operates in the **Republic of Veridonia**, a fictional middle-income jurisdiction of roughly forty million people. Veridonia adopted a foundational identity platform of the kind described in this guide, established a civil-registration integration for birth and death events (Section 8.6), and operates the identity estate in-country. The national identity authority has onboarded Cymbal Bank as an **Authentication Partner / Relying Party** ✅ (the documented bank-facing role), with a partner policy that grants the bank a name, date of birth, photograph, and a residence status flag — but **not** the national identifier itself, which the bank receives only as a per-transaction privacy token ✅ (the platform's documented design posture). Cymbal Bank's onboarding journey, therefore, begins with a question it can now ask the state, and ends with an answer only Cymbal Bank can give.

Cymbal Bank has a specific commercial problem it wants the identity platform to solve. It runs a basic-account product in Veridonia's smaller towns, and its application-to-funded-account conversion is poor precisely where it wants to grow. Underwriting analysis blames one step: documentary KYC. Customers in these towns arrive with inconsistent paperwork — a voter card, a hand-written rent receipt, a health clinic slip — and the branch cannot get a satisfactory reference in a reasonable number of visits, so applications lapse. Cymbal Bank's hypothesis is that a reliable national identity answer will shorten the branch visit from three appointments to one, and that conversion will follow.

### 14.2 The decision to work through

**The decision:** for the new basic-account product, should Cymbal Bank accept the national identity system's authentication answer *as the sole identity-verification step*, replacing the documentary checklist — and on what terms?

The platform side of the decision is clear and the limits are documented. Cymbal Bank can authenticate a customer against their UIN or VID with one or more factors ✅. It can request eKYC where policy permits ✅, and in Veridonia the policy permits it, which is why the bank receives the name, date of birth, photograph and residence status. It receives a privacy-enabled token rather than the permanent identifier ✅, which means the bank can transact with the customer without holding the number that everything else in the country keys off. It receives a signed answer, and where it uses credentials rather than a live call it can verify them without calling the issuer ✅ (the credential layer, owned by [verifiable_credentials_guide.md](verifiable_credentials_guide.md)).

The bank side of the decision is where the work is, and it separates into three questions that the platform does not answer.

**Question one — what has actually been verified?** The platform answer establishes that a unique Veridonian identity exists and that the person presenting matches the biometric or OTP factors against that record. It does not establish that the person *is* the person in the sense the bank needs: a record can be impersonated at the counter by someone who has the biometrics (or the phone receiving the OTP) but is not the rightful holder. So Cymbal Bank's control is not "did the platform say yes" but "did the platform say yes *and* did our channel bind the answer to this applicant." The bank therefore makes the live-factor capture part of its own branch flow — the customer authenticates at the bank's own terminal, not by presenting an artifact that could be carried by someone else.

**Question two — what is the bank still obliged to do?** Nothing about the state's answer discharges Cymbal Bank's CDD obligation, its beneficial-ownership or PEP or sanctions screening, its risk rating, or its ability to explain the onboarding decision to a supervisor years later. The state answered *who*; Cymbal Bank answers *whether* ✅ (Section 9.3). Concretely, the bank keeps its sanctions and PEP screening exactly as it is, keeps its risk-rating model, keeps its record retention, and — because it is now relying on a third party's answer — adds a new control: it stores the identity-verification result *with the policy version and the timestamp* under which it was obtained. If Veridonia's partner policy later narrows the grant (which it can ✅), the bank can still prove what it was permitted to do on the day it onboarded each customer.

**Question three — what happens on the bad day?** Cymbal Bank plays out three scenarios on paper before it goes live, and this is where the design earns its keep. *The identity service is unavailable:* the bank's documented fallback is the old documentary route, re-enabled for the outage window and capped at a defined volume, with applications above the cap deferred rather than force-approved — because the alternative is a branch inventing its own verification under pressure. *The customer's record is wrong:* the customer was enrolled and their biometrics authenticate, but their record carries a name spelling that does not match their tax credential, or a residence status flag that a downstream product cannot accept. The bank does not try to fix the register — it cannot, and it has no authority to ✅ — so the process is a documented referral to the identity authority with a named internal owner and a tracked case, and the branch is explicitly forbidden from "working around" the discrepancy. *The customer was enrolled through the biometric exception path* ✅ (the documented mechanism for worn or missing biometrics): their record exists, but their authentication modalities are thinner, and the bank's live-factor capture must be designed to accept the alternatives the record supports — otherwise the inclusion mechanism that got them into the register is undone by the bank's own integration, which is failure mode 13.2 arriving from the other side.

### 14.3 The decision, and the thesis

Cymbal Bank accepts the national identity answer as the **primary** identity-verification step and retires most of the documentary checklist — but not all of it, and not blindly. It accepts it as sole evidence for the identity-assurance step only; it keeps its own live-factor capture so the answer is bound to the applicant in front of the teller; it keeps every risk and regulatory obligation it already had; it records the policy version under which each answer was obtained; it funds a fallback and a referral process before go-live rather than after; and it designs its channel so that a customer enrolled through the exception path is still onboardable. On those terms the commercial hypothesis holds: the branch visit shortens, the applications that used to lapse on paperwork convert, and the addressable market in Veridonia's smaller towns expands — for the people Veridonia's identity system successfully enrolled.

And here is what Cymbal Bank has actually learned from the exercise, which is the point of the section. The bank did not integrate an identity check. It integrated a **dependency on a decision it did not make and cannot reverse** — whether a given person exists, once, in Veridonia's register. Everything the bank now does at the counter rests on a determination that was made at a registration centre, months or years earlier, by an operator and a supervisor, against a biometric gallery, with a human adjudicator at the ambiguous edge, and a state's policy layer above all of it. Cymbal Bank can authenticate against that decision; it cannot appeal it, cannot correct it, and cannot work around it. That is the whole architecture of the thing, seen from one teller's desk: the platform's hard problem is not storing the customer's identity — the bank could store anything. It is that someone had to decide, once, and that everything the bank builds sits downstream of that decision.

## 15. The Anti-Patterns, Then the Claims Audit

### 15.1 The anti-patterns

Ten patterns that get a team into trouble with a platform of this kind, drawn from the mechanisms documented above.

1. **Treating "open source" as "cheap."** The licence removes the software cost and none of the recurring operational cost (Sections 7.3, 13.4). Budget the field operations, the ABIS estate, key custody, adjudication and the helpdesk for the full identity lifecycle.
2. **Treating "modular" as "assemble-any-three-modules."** The code is modular; the assembly is a system with shared infrastructure and one central policy authority (Section 4.6). Plan an integration, not a shopping list.
3. **Skipping the biometric deduplication stage on the assumption that demographic matching caught it.** The docs are explicit that every packet goes through biometric deduplication regardless ✅ (Section 6.2). Any workflow that short-circuits the second stage has changed the product's uniqueness guarantee.
4. **Reading the `targetFPIR` table as a performance guarantee.** It is a configuration example from a February 2021 interface specification, defining the rate at which identify requests return a non-empty candidate list ✅ (Section 6.4). It is not a deployment error rate and not a false-accept or false-reject figure.
5. **Quoting an adoption count as fact.** The project publishes 32 engagements and 203,044,617+ residents; the originating institution publishes 26 countries; the home-page overlay names 28 — all undated and none independently audited this pass ⚠ (Sections 1.2, 16). Say "the project publishes," not "there are."
6. **Printing a funding claim from a logo wall.** Four "Supported by" logos on the project's About page are images with no extractable text ⚠ (Section 2.5). Take funder names only from a source that states them in words.
7. **Treating the exception path as optional.** Biometric exception handling, the introducer route and in-home registration are the difference between inclusion and exclusion, and each must be enabled, staffed and supervised (Sections 3.3, 13.2).
8. **Building adjudication to pilot volumes.** The 1:N search gets slower as the gallery grows, and the safest accuracy settings push more cases into a human queue (Sections 6.1, 13.3). Resource adjudication against the end-state population.
9. **Assuming the ABIS is the authentication engine.** It is the deduplication engine, and the project says not to use it for 1:1 authentication ✅ (Sections 4.5, 13.5).
10. **Letting a verified tick replace a risk decision.** eKYC and authentication answer *who*; the relying party's obligations are untouched (Sections 9.3, 13.5, and the banking siblings).

Two more that are specific to writing or reading a guide like this one, and are recorded because this guide is bound by them:

11. **Upgrading a project's own claim into an independent fact.** The project's framing is cited as the project's framing here throughout; where the underlying figure originates outside MOSIP (the ~850 million identity gap), it is marked ⚠ as project-stated and not verified at its original source ✅/⚠ (Section 3.2).
12. **Filling a governance gap with an inference.** The code owner, the licence and the trademark are verifiable; the board, the steering committee and the decision rights are not, this pass (Section 2.6). The gap is reported as a gap.

### 15.3 The one rule this audit exists to enforce

**No adoption list, no funding claim and no biometric-accuracy figure appears anywhere in this guide without a primary source named above, and the reason is the failure rate of the secondary record on exactly these three subjects.** Adoption is the field where a project's own marketing, a school's summary page and a web overlay disagree by six countries and by an unbounded number of residents, all undated (32 vs 26 vs 28 names) ⚠ — so this guide prints the disagreement rather than picking a winner, and never states a count as a fact. Funding is the field where the only primary statement is **undated** and lives on the originating institution's page while the project's own site shows unreadable logos ⚠ — so this guide prints the one sourced sentence, dates it as undated, and adds no funder name. Biometric accuracy is the field where a single **configuration example** from a February 2021 interface specification is the only number in existence, and is routinely misread as a measured error rate ✅/⚠ — so this guide prints it with its definition and its date, and states explicitly that no FAR, FRR or false-non-match rate for any deployment was found ❌. In each case the rule is the same and it is the rule of this whole guide: **a claim is printed at the strength its source supports, and where the source is weak the weakness is printed too.**

### 16.2 The glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **ABIS** (Automated Biometric Identification System) | The external system that performs the 1:N biometric search for deduplication; pluggable, connects only over message queues, and never receives residents' PII ✅ |
| **Authentication Partner / Relying Party** | The documented partner type for entities that use MOSIP for authentication — "banks, telecom, Govt. institutes etc." ✅ — the bank's role in the ecosystem |
| **CBEFF** | The common biometric exchange file format container (CBEFF XML) in which MOSIP wraps biometric data ✅ |
| **CRVS** (Civil Registration and Vital Statistics) | The system recording life events (birth, death, marriage, divorce); the natural upstream partner of a foundational identity system, integrated for birth/death/update flows ✅ |
| **Deduplication** | Comparing a new individual's demographic and biometric data against everything already stored, to establish uniqueness ✅ |
| **Demographic deduplication** | Stage one: comparing hashed name, date of birth and gender against the whole database, escalating potential matches to an ABIS gallery match ✅ |
| **Gallery match** | An ABIS operation matching one person's biometrics against a *supplied* candidate set rather than the whole gallery ✅ |
| **Foundational identity** | A unique, government-recognised identifier not tied to a single service, forming the base layer that functional (sector) IDs reference ✅ |
| **Functional identity** | A sector-specific identifier for a specific use case (health, finance, social protection, education, voting) that may leverage the foundational identity ✅ |
| **IDA** (ID Authentication) | The independent authentication service, seedable "by any system, including MOSIP," offering UIN/VID authentication, eKYC, OTP and internal variants ✅ |
| **Introducer** | A trusted, verified individual (e.g. a community leader) who vouches for an applicant with no formal documents, enabling enrolment on trust-based verification ✅ |
| **MAS** (Manual Adjudication System) | The human decision layer that receives borderline biometric matches over the MOSIP-to-ManualAdjudication queue; built by the country's system integrator, not by MOSIP ✅ |
| **MPL 2.0** | Mozilla Public License 2.0 — the file-level weak-copyleft licence used across MOSIP's core repositories, MIT and CC-BY-SA 4.0 covering other assets ✅ |
| **NFIQ** | The NIST fingerprint image quality score; specified as v2.0 for registration devices and v1.0 for authentication devices ✅ |
| **PMS** (Partner Management Services) | The module that onboards partners, exchanges certificates and holds the policies governing attribute-level data access ✅ |
| **Pre-registration** | Online entry of basic demographic data and appointment booking, ahead of the centre visit ✅ |
| **Registration Client / ARC** | The offline-first thick desktop capture client, and its Android (portable, in-home) counterpart ✅ |
| **Registration Processor (Regproc)** | The backend pipeline that validates and processes packets and drives UIN generation; internally SEDA, with Vert.x stages and Apache Camel workflow ✅ |
| **SEDA** | Staged event-driven architecture — the pattern the Registration Processor follows, "where data flows via multiple stages till the UIN is issued" ✅ |
| **SBI** | The biometric device software standard (SBI 1.0 for registration, SBI 2.0 with FTM-supported security for authentication) ✅ |
| **targetFPIR** | The ABIS parameter expressing the target rate at which identify requests are expected to return a non-empty candidate list; published as a configuration example, not a guarantee ✅ |
| **UIN** (Unique Identity Number) | The permanent unique identifier issued to a resident once their packet passes validation and deduplication ✅ |
| **VID** (Virtual ID) | A revocable, regenerable alias for a UIN so the permanent identifier is never shared with a relying party ✅ |
| **WebSub / Datashare** | The publish/subscribe mechanism for lifecycle events, and the URL-based service by which policy-governed data is shared with partners ✅ |

### 16.4 The closing summary

MOSIP is a real, working, unusually well-documented open-source platform, and the honest account of it is narrower and more interesting than either its marketing or its critics suggest. It is not an identity system; it is a kit for building one, and the country supplies the law, the centres, the operators, the adjudicators, the money and the redress. Its architecture separates more genuinely than most platforms dare to — the ABIS is a contract, the adjudication system is someone else's application, the authentication layer is seedable by anyone, the licence lets a state leave a bad vendor without re-writing national infrastructure. Its greatest strength is also the reason it takes this guide six sections to reach a comfortable description: the platform is designed to make one decision well, and that decision — whether this person is unique — is the one nothing downstream can undo. The deduplication pipeline always runs; borderline hits go to two human reviewers; a confirmed match rejects the packet; and the excluded person meets the consequence at a bank counter, a clinic or a school, far from the desk that made the call. On the evidence retrieved this pass, the two things a serious reader should carry away are that the accuracy of any such system is a property of its deployment rather than its code — no deployment's error rate is published anywhere this guide could find — and that the licence, not the roadmap, is what guarantees a state can keep running what it has. Everything hard about the platform is downstream of the same fact, and it is the fact this guide has been circling since the first page: storing a hundred million identity records is engineering, and it is solved every day across the industry; deciding who belongs in the register is something else entirely.

**MOSIP's hard problem is not storing an identity; it is deciding, once, who exists.**
