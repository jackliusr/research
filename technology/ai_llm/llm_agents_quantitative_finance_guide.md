## 1. The Overview — the Quant-Workflow Companion, the Scope, the Evidence Conventions

### 1.1 The Companion Positioning

This guide answers the **quant-workflow question**: inside a quantitative investment process — the research that finds the signal, the signal that becomes the trade, the execution that gets the trade done, the risk that constrains it, the portfolio that holds it, and the evaluation that decides whether any of it worked — where do LLM agents actually sit in 2026, which named systems verifiably exist, and how should a serious quant shop or bank evaluate one before it touches a book? It is deliberately **not** the deployment catalogue and **not** the engineering manual — both already exist in this repository, and both are cross-referenced rather than re-derived:

- **The deployment evidence** lives in [LLM Agent Use Cases in Finance](./llm_agent_use_cases_finance_guide.md) — the finance-wide landscape. It carries the verified record this guide leans on: the LOXM lineage (§7.1 — JPMorgan, 2017, press-carried, the first major bank to apply AI to real-time execution, with the circulating "15% improvement" figure flagged ⚠), BloombergGPT (§8.2 — arXiv:2303.17564, the 50-billion-parameter model on a 363-billion-token financial dataset), BlackRock's Aladdin Copilot (§8.3), JPMorgan's LLM Suite (§5.2), and the 2025 "AI trader" pilot discipline (§7.3). Every one of those facts is used here as **✅-carried** with a cross-reference — none is re-verified or re-derived.
- **The engineering how-to** lives in [Multi-Agent AI Systems for Banking](./multi_agent_banking_guide.md) — the governed-agent contracts (scope, escalation, evidence, evaluation), the orchestration patterns, the eval pipelines and quality gates, the canary/champion-challenger/kill-switch rollout machinery. When this guide's worked example (Section 11) needs an eval gate or a rollout step, it **cross-references** the engineering sibling and moves on.
- **The LLM-eval tooling** (DeepEval, LangSmith, MLflow, RAGAS, LLM-as-judge) lives in [LLM Evaluation Frameworks](./llm_evaluation_frameworks_guide.md) and is cross-referenced in Section 9.4 — but the **quant evaluation practices** — backtesting, paper trading, walk-forward analysis, benchmark periods, slippage and transaction-cost realism, backtest-overfitting discipline — are this guide's own content (Section 9), because they are the quant's native evaluation culture and they predate and outrank the LLM-eval layer wherever the agent's output is a trade.

### 1.2 The Scope — the Quant Lifecycle, One Question

The unit of analysis is the **quant workflow** — the six-stage pipeline of Section 2.1 — and the question asked at every stage is the same: *is there a verified system doing this, a pilot doing it, or a vendor asking you to imagine it?* The scope includes the academic and open-source core that is genuinely verifiable (Section 3), the bank and buy-side deployments that the sibling guides verified (Sections 5–7), and the honest void where the only evidence is marketing (Sections 5.3, 10.3, 12.2). The scope **excludes** the general banking-agent estate (fraud, AML, KYC, customer service — the engineering sibling's four workflows) except where it touches the trading lifecycle as compliance overlay (Section 8), and it **excludes** the wealth-advisory and robo-advisor landscape except as the pre-LLM contrast for portfolio automation (Section 7.3, cross-referenced).

Two boundaries deserve emphasis at the outset, because the quant-agent discourse blurs them constantly:

- **The artifact boundary — a paper is not a deployment.** Section 3's systems are verified *as research artifacts*: their arXiv pages and repositories are primary sources for what the systems are and what their simulations claim — never for market truth. When this guide says a paper "reports improvements over baselines," it is reporting the paper's own claim about its own backtest, and the grade (✅ as a paper claim) says exactly that. The distance from "the paper's backtest improved on the baselines" to "the system makes money with live capital" is the entire subject of Sections 9 and 10.
- **The generation boundary — LLM agents versus the pre-LLM domain-specific agents.** This guide is about **LLM agents** — systems whose language model does consequential work in the quant process — and it consistently separates them from the **pre-LLM domain-specific agents** that actually run the industry's money: the reinforcement-learning execution engines, the optimisers, the market-making systems. The honest finding of Sections 5–7 and 10 is that the industry's production alpha-and-execution stack is overwhelmingly the pre-LLM family, and the LLM agent's verified home in 2026 is the **research-to-decision layer**, not the order flow. Keeping the two families distinct is the same discipline the use-case sibling applies to the finance-wide landscape (its §2.2 general-purpose-versus-domain-specific split, cross-referenced).

### 1.3 The Evidence Conventions — Verified, Press-Reported, Contradicted

Every factual claim in this guide carries one of three grades, applied under the same standard the sibling guides use:

- ✅ **Verified at a primary or authoritative source.** For the academic systems: the arXiv abstract page (the paper's own claims about the system — never the paper's performance claims treated as market truth). For open-source platforms: the maintainer's repository. For banks and vendors: the institution's own primary page or release. Claims verified in this repository's earlier passes and carried from a sibling are marked **✅-carried** with the cross-reference, and their provenance note (for example, LOXM's press-carried record) travels with them.
- ⚠ **Press-reported or vendor-marketed only.** The fact is real as a *claim* — someone reputable said it — but it was not confirmed at a primary source in this pass. The source is named ("as reported by…", "vendor-published"). A large share of the quant-agent field's most quoted claims lives at this grade, and this guide treats that as information, not as failure.
- ❌ **Contradicted** — the evidence says otherwise. This pass found few outright contradictions in the quant-agent record; where they exist (for example, conflations of simulation results with live performance, or an "AI hedge fund" label contradicting a README that says the system makes no trades) they are marked and explained.

The working rules: nothing unverified is presented as verified; a paper's backtest is reported as a paper's backtest, never as a deployment; a vendor's product page is quoted as vendor marketing until a named institution publishes results; press figures that a primary source could not confirm are flagged even when they are famous (the LOXM "15% improvement" figure is the standing example); and every item this pass could not confirm is collected honestly in Section 12.2. The evidence conventions themselves are the sibling conventions — the use-case sibling's §1.3 defines the same ✅/⚠/❌ standard for the deployment landscape, and the engineering sibling's §14 audit table shows the ✅-carried notation for sibling-verified facts — and this guide's Section 12 is the same audit-table form applied to the quant-agent record.

## 2. The Quant-Agent Landscape — Where LLM Agents Fit in the Quant Lifecycle

### 2.1 The Quant Lifecycle — Research, Signal, Execution, Risk, Portfolio, Evaluation

The quant workflow is a pipeline with feedback loops, and each stage has a different relationship to language — which is why LLM agents fit some stages naturally and others barely at all:

| The Stage | What Happens | The Language Content | Where the LLM Agent Can Sit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Alpha research** | Idea generation, literature-to-factor translation, hypothesis formation | Very high — reading papers, writing code skeletons, drafting memos | Copilot, agent (Section 3) |
| **Signal generation** | Turning hypotheses into measurable signals/factors; validating them | Medium — feature definitions, alpha expressions, explanation | Copilot over deterministic backtests (Sections 3–4) |
| **Execution** | Turning decisions into orders with minimal market impact | Low — the problem is numeric, latency-bound, microstructure-shaped | Pre-LLM RL engines own it; LLM copilots advise (Section 5) |
| **Risk** | Limits, VaR, stress, scenario analysis, explanation | Medium — limits are rules, but explanation is language | Copilots that explain and surface (Section 6) |
| **Portfolio construction** | Optimisation, rebalancing, tax and turnover management | Low in the core, high in the interface | Deterministic optimiser wrapped in LLM interface (Section 7) |
| **Evaluation** | Backtest, paper trade, walk-forward, attribution, review | High — the review is a written argument | Eval harnesses plus LLM review copilots (Section 9) |

The pattern that emerges even before any system is named: the LLM agent's natural habitat is the **research-to-decision arc** — the stages where the raw material is text, ideas and justification — and its natural boundary is the **decision-to-execution arc**, where the raw material is numbers, latency and microstructure, and where the industry's pre-LLM domain-specific agents (Section 2.3 of the use-case sibling, cross-referenced) already hold the franchise.

Two properties of the real workflow sharpen the map. First, the pipeline is **not linear**: evaluation results feed back into research (a failed factor hypothesis is the next memo's starting point), which is why Section 3's memory-equipped agents (FinMem) and Section 9's eval harnesses are two ends of the same loop — the agent must remember what it tried, and the harness must record what happened. Second, the pipeline's **risk stages are not optional**: a strategy idea that skips the risk stage is not a strategy, it is a hope, which is why Section 11's worked example routes every candidate through the risk overlay before any evaluation result is believed.

### 2.2 The Human-in-the-Loop Reality

No verified quant deployment in this guide's record removes the human from the decision loop, and the reasons are structural rather than regulatory:

- ✅ **The academic systems themselves are decision-support or simulation systems, not money managers.** TradingAgents (Section 3.1) proposes a *framework* whose outputs are evaluated in backtests; FinMem (Section 3.2) is a memory-and-decision architecture tested on historical stock data; StockAgent (Section 3.4 context) *simulates* investor behaviour in a sandbox. ✅ The open-source platforms say so on the tin: FinRobot's repository disclaimer states its code "should not be construed as financial counsel or recommendations for live trading" ✅ (source: github.com/AI4Finance-Foundation/FinRobot), and the community "AI hedge fund" project's README states it is "for **educational** purposes only," that "the system does not actually make any trades," and that "past performance does not indicate future results" ✅ (source: github.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund). The honesty of the open-source field is itself a finding: the people closest to these systems are the most explicit that they are research artifacts.
- ✅ **The bank deployments that are verified keep the human as the decision-maker.** The use-case sibling's research-agent pattern is uniform across every verified deployment it records — HSBC's meeting-prep assistant, Morgan Stanley's Debrief, the buy-side research copilots: "summarise, retrieve with citation, draft, and propose — never decide" ✅-carried (sibling §8). JPMorgan's own LLM Suite record describes the direction as "combining generative AI with workflows to create AI agents that can carry out a series of actions to complete a goal" — inside a governed, human-approval environment, not as autonomous order flow ✅-carried (sibling §5.2).
- ⚠ **The autonomous-LLM-trader claims are inverse to the evidence.** The louder the claim that an LLM agent trades on its own, the thinner the verifiable record behind it (Section 10.3). This pass verified **no** major-bank production deployment of a fully autonomous LLM trading agent — a finding consistent with, and carried from, the use-case sibling's §7.2–7.3.

The human-in-the-loop reality is therefore not a compromise the industry is making reluctantly; it is the design the evidence supports at every rung. The shape of the loop varies by stage, and the checkpoint ladder is worth making explicit because Section 11's worked example installs it desk by desk:

- **At the research stage** — the PM approves which ideas enter the pipeline; the agent proposes, the human sponsors.
- **At the signal stage** — the quant approves the factor definition and its evaluation design *before* the backtest runs, so the human cannot be accused of shopping for a result after the fact.
- **At the execution stage** — the trader owns the order; the pre-LLM engine and the human share the execution decision; the LLM copilot, where present, advises only.
- **At the risk stage** — the risk manager owns limit exceptions; no agent output can override a limit breach.
- **At the portfolio stage** — the optimiser and the PM own the allocation; the LLM proposes constraint language and explains output.
- **At the evaluation stage** — the research committee owns the go/no-go; the eval harness reports, the humans decide.

This is the same governed shape the engineering sibling hard-codes into its banking workflows (its §2.5 HITL map, cross-referenced), and the quant version differs only in the vocabulary: the human is the PM, the quant, the trader, the risk manager — each with a different checkpoint on a different stage.

### 2.3 The Copilot-to-Agent Gradient — Cross-Referenced

The use-case sibling's §2.3 maturity gradient — **assistant** (reads, does not act), **copilot** (drafts and proposes under human approval), **agent** (executes bounded multi-step work with escalation contracts) — is the vocabulary this guide borrows for the quant domain, and it is **cross-referenced, not re-derived**: the reader who wants the full table and its deployment mapping stays in [LLM Agent Use Cases in Finance](./llm_agent_use_cases_finance_guide.md) §2.3. What the quant workflow adds is a second axis — **distance from the order** — because a copilot that drafts an alpha-research memo (far from the order) and a copilot that proposes an execution schedule (near the order) are the same rung on the gradient but very different risk objects. The worked example of Section 11 ranks its candidates on both axes: the rung the system can honestly occupy *and* how close it sits to live order flow, because the combination — not either alone — determines the eval burden and the rollout gates.

## 3. The Alpha-Research & Signal-Generation Agents — the Verified Academic Core

Alpha research — finding the tradable pattern — is the quant stage with the highest language content, and it is where the LLM-agent literature is genuinely deep. Every system named in this section was verified at its arXiv abstract page or its maintainer's repository in this pass, and every one of them is an **academic or open-source artifact, not a deployed money manager** — the distinction is the section's spine.

### 3.1 The Multi-Agent Research Framework — TradingAgents

- ✅ **TradingAgents exists, and its design is exactly the "research firm" shape.** The paper "TradingAgents: Multi-Agents LLM Financial Trading Framework" (Xiao, Sun, Luo, Wang; arXiv:2412.20138, submitted 28 December 2024, revised through June 2025) proposes a stock-trading framework inspired by real trading firms: LLM agents in specialised roles — fundamental analysts, sentiment analysts, technical analysts, and traders with varied risk profiles — plus **Bull and Bear researcher agents** that debate the market condition, a **risk-management team** that monitors exposure, and traders that synthesise the debate into decisions (source: the arXiv abstract page). The code is public at github.com/TauricResearch/TradingAgents ✅ (linked from the abstract).
- ✅ **The debate structure is the design's centrepiece and its honest limit.** The Bull-versus-Bear researcher setup is a structured way to force the *consideration of both sides* before a decision — an adversarial-collaboration pattern that quant research shops use with human analysts and that the engineering sibling's multi-agent patterns (debate as an orchestration anti-pattern cure) would recognise. But the debate happens inside the model's context: both sides are generated by the same underlying LLM, so the "debate" tests the model's ability to argue against itself, not two independent views of the world — a limitation the paper's own framing does not hide and that any deployer must price in.
- ✅ **The reported results are simulation results, reported as such.** The abstract claims the framework "aims to improve trading performance" and that experiments show improvements over baseline models in cumulative return, Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown — a backtest-style evaluation on historical data, which is exactly how the paper frames it. ✅ The framework's own architecture makes the risk layer explicit — a named risk-management team with exposure monitoring — which is more than most vendor "AI trader" brochures contain, and which is why the honest reading of TradingAgents is as a *research prototype of the governed multi-agent shape*, not as evidence that LLM agent societies beat markets.

### 3.2 The Memory-Equipped Trading Agent — FinMem

- ✅ **FinMem exists, and its contribution is memory architecture for a trading agent.** The paper "FinMem: A Performance-Enhanced LLM Trading Agent with Layered Memory and Character Design" (Yu, Li, Chen, Jiang, Li, Zhang, Liu, Suchow, Khashanah; arXiv:2311.13743, submitted November 2023) describes an LLM-based agent framework for financial decision-making with three core modules — **Profiling** (customises the agent's characteristics), **Memory** (layered message processing aligned with "the cognitive structure of human traders," with an adjustable cognitive span that can retain information beyond human perceptual limits), and **Decision-making** (converts memory-derived insight into investment decisions) (source: the arXiv abstract page).
- ✅ **Its evaluation is likewise a historical-data study.** The paper reports comparing FinMem against "various algorithmic agents on a scalable real-world financial dataset," reporting leading trading performance in stocks and improved cumulative investment returns after tuning the perceptual span and character — a backtest study with named baselines, not a live-trading record. FinMem's significance for this guide is the *architecture pattern* — an agent with explicit, tunable, interpretable memory — because that is precisely the design a quant shop would want for a research copilot that must remember its own prior hypotheses and why it discarded them. The tunable "cognitive span" also carries the deployer's warning: a memory span tuned on the backtest window is a tuning knob like any other, and Section 9.1's untouchable-evaluation-window rule exists precisely because of knobs like it.

### 3.3 The Alpha-Mining Systems — Alpha-GPT and the Human-in-the-Loop Family

- ✅ **Alpha-GPT exists — the "human-AI interactive alpha mining" system.** The paper "Alpha-GPT: Human-AI Interactive Alpha Mining for Quantitative Investment" (Wang, Yuan, Zhou, Ni, Shum, Guo; arXiv:2308.00016, submitted July 2023) proposes an alpha-mining paradigm built on human-AI interaction: a prompt-engineering framework that lets an LLM "understand" a quant researcher's idea and output candidate alphas — tradable signal expressions — which the researcher then evaluates (source: the arXiv abstract page). The paper is listed in the EMNLP 2025 System Demonstration Track ✅ (arXiv comment field).
- ✅ **Alpha-GPT 2.0 extends the loop across the whole pipeline.** "Alpha-GPT 2.0: Human-in-the-Loop AI for Quantitative Investment" (Yuan, Wang, Guo; arXiv:2402.09746, February 2024) carries the human-in-the-loop design beyond alpha mining into the "modeling and analysis phases" of the quant pipeline — and is candidly marked "Draft. Work in progress" in its own abstract ✅ (source: the arXiv abstract page). The self-description is the point: the leading academic statement of the alpha-mining-agent idea is a *research system with the human explicitly in the loop at every stage* — the opposite of the autonomous-alpha-generator marketing that borrows its name.
- ✅ **The alpha-mining workflow is the most quant-native design in the whole literature.** Alpha-GPT's framing — the researcher holds the *idea*, the LLM proposes *implementable alpha expressions*, the researcher's backtest library judges them — maps one-to-one onto the real quant research loop (Section 2.1's research-and-signal stages), which is why the worked example's candidate 1 (Section 11.2) is essentially an Alpha-GPT-shaped copilot with the bank's own guardrails. The intellectual property of the human stays in the loop; the LLM's contribution is breadth and speed of candidate generation, not authority.

### 3.4 The Open-Source Platform Layer — FinRobot, FinGPT, FinRL

The AI4Finance Foundation is the open-source hub of this field, and its three flagship projects form a layered stack — verified this pass at the repositories and the arXiv records:

- ✅ **FinRobot — the agent platform.** The repository github.com/AI4Finance-Foundation/FinRobot (Apache-2.0 license; ~7.9k stars at the time of this pass) describes itself as "FinRobot: An Open-Source AI Agent Platform for Financial Applications using Large Language Models," and the companion paper was published at the 5th ACM International Conference on AI in Finance (ICAIF 2024, pages 538–546; Han, Wang, Che, Yang, Zhang, Xu) ✅ (sources: the GitHub repository and its citation block). Its own disclaimer — code "should not be construed as financial counsel or recommendations for live trading" ✅ — is the platform layer's honest self-limit.
- ✅ **FinGPT — the open financial-model layer beneath the agents.** The first official FinGPT paper, "FinGPT: Open-Source Financial Large Language Models" (Yang, Liu, Wang; arXiv:2306.06031, June 2023; FinLLM Symposium at IJCAI 2023), and its data-centric companion "FinGPT: Democratizing Internet-scale Data for Financial Large Language Models" (Liu, Wang, Yang, Zha; arXiv:2307.10485) document the open, data-centric alternative to the closed BloombergGPT line — automatic curation of real-time financial data from dozens of internet sources, low-rank adaptation for cheap customisation, and applications including robo-advising and sentiment analysis for algorithmic trading (sources: the arXiv abstract pages). The repositories (AI4Finance-Foundation/FinGPT and FinNLP) are linked from the papers.
- ✅ **FinRL — the pre-LLM reinforcement-learning layer that still does the heavy lifting.** "FinRL: Deep Reinforcement Learning Framework to Automate Trading in Quantitative Finance" (Liu, Yang, Gao, Wang; arXiv:2111.09395; ACM ICAIF 2021) is the community's full-pipeline DRL framework — market simulation environments, fine-tuned DRL algorithms, trading constraints such as market friction and liquidity ✅ (source: the arXiv abstract page). The ecosystem's own evolution shows where the LLM fits: FinRL-Meta (arXiv:2211.03107, NeurIPS 2022 Datasets and Benchmarks) supplies the market environments, and the FinRL Contests summary (arXiv:2504.02281) records that the 2023–2025 contest series — 200+ participants from 100+ institutions across 20+ countries — included tasks on stock trading, order execution, crypto trading and "the use of large language model (LLM)-engineered signals" ✅ (sources: the arXiv abstract pages). The community's own framing is that LLMs *engineer signals into* the established RL pipeline — not that they replace it.
- ✅ **StockAgent — the simulation sandbox, with a data-integrity design worth copying.** "When AI Meets Finance (StockAgent): Large Language Model-based Stock Trading in Simulated Real-world Environments" (Zhang et al.; arXiv:2407.18957; published in ACM Transactions on Intelligent Systems and Technology) builds multi-agent LLM simulations of investor behaviour and — unusually — designs against **test-set leakage**, preventing the model from using prior knowledge of the test period ✅ (source: the arXiv abstract page). For a quant shop evaluating an LLM signal agent, that leakage-avoidance design is a transferable discipline: an LLM trained on the open internet has already "seen" the history you are about to backtest, and StockAgent is the rare paper that names the problem and engineers against it.

### 3.5 The Industry and Community Claims — Condensed Cross-References

- ✅-carried **BloombergGPT** — the domain-model layer of the research story is verified in the use-case sibling §8.2 (arXiv:2303.17564; the 50-billion-parameter model trained on a 363-billion-token financial dataset): an existence-and-design ✅, not a deployment metric, and **not re-derived here**.
- ⚠ **The hedge-fund LLM claims.** Claims circulate that major quant hedge funds are deploying LLM agents inside alpha research — this pass could not verify a primary statement from any named fund describing a production LLM agent in its alpha pipeline, and none is asserted here (the honest list is in Section 12.2). The repository's quant-context guides ([Citadel LLC](../../banking/citadel_llc_guide.md) §7, [Hedge Fund Guide](../../banking/hedge_fund_guide.md) §3) describe the *pre-LLM* research factories those claims lean on.
- ✅ **The community "AI hedge fund" exists and is explicit about being educational.** The repository github.com/virattt/ai-hedge-fund (~63k stars and ~11k forks at the time of this pass; MIT license) is a multi-agent "AI hedge fund team" whose README states it is "a proof of concept," "for **educational** purposes only," that "the system does not actually make any trades," and that its evolving design reimagines the investor agents as "pluggable, backtestable 'alpha models'" ✅ (source: the repository README). The **existence** is verified; any implication that it is a functioning fund is contradicted by its own README ❌ (as a claim about the project, the README's "does not actually make any trades" directly contradicts the "AI hedge fund" label's implied autonomy — the label is the marketing, the README is the truth).

The pattern across Section 3 is the field's central fact: **the verifiable alpha-research-agent core is academic, open-source, human-in-the-loop by design, and honest about its disclaimers** — and the distance from that core to a deployed, capital-committing system is the distance Section 10 measures.

### 3.6 What the Academic Core Teaches the Deployer

Before the guide moves down the workflow, the transferable lessons of the verified academic core are worth stating as an explicit list — because Sections 5–8 will show that every *deployed* quant agent borrows from these designs, and every *marketed* one claims to:

- ✅ **Role specialisation plus structured debate** (TradingAgents' analyst roles, Bull/Bear researchers) — the multi-agent pattern that forces both-sides consideration before a decision; deployable as a research-memo review step where one agent drafts and another attacks.
- ✅ **An explicit risk layer inside the agent** (TradingAgents' risk-management team monitoring exposure) — the academic prototype of the governed-agent escalation contract; the design principle, not the specific implementation, is what transfers.
- ✅ **Layered, tunable, interpretable memory** (FinMem's Profiling/Memory/Decision modules) — the pattern for an agent that must remember its own hypothesis history; with the tuning-knob warning that memory spans are parameters like any other.
- ✅ **Human-in-the-loop as the pipeline, not the exception** (Alpha-GPT and Alpha-GPT 2.0's interactive alpha mining) — the LLM proposes candidate alphas, the human's backtest library judges, the human's idea owns the loop.
- ✅ **Leakage-avoidance as a first-class design constraint** (StockAgent's test-set-leakage engineering) — the discipline every deployer must copy, because pretrained models have read the backtest window already.
- ✅ **Signals engineered by LLMs feed RL and optimisation pipelines; they do not replace them** (the FinRL Contests' LLM-engineered-signal tasks, the FinRL-X architecture) — the community's own architecture answer to where the language model sits.
- ✅ **Honest disclaimers as a governance feature** (FinRobot's no-live-trading disclaimer, ai-hedge-fund's educational-only README) — the open-source field models the disclosure discipline that regulated deployers are legally required to match.

None of these lessons requires believing that any of the systems makes money; each is a design fact verified at the primary record, and Section 11's worked example installs them inside Cymbal Bank's pilot one by one.

## 4. The Alternative-Data Agents — Reading the World for Signal

Alternative data — the signal hidden in documents, calls, news, web traffic, satellite imagery and social chatter rather than in prices — is where LLM agents have a structural advantage, because the raw material is text and the pre-LLM text-processing stack was crude. The repository's data guides carry the data-side detail ([Market Data Consumption](../../banking/market_data_consumption_guide.md) §2 on the ecosystem and §4 on feed types; the market-intelligence layer of the use-case sibling §8.1); what belongs here is the **agent-shaped reading layer** and its evidence.

The alt-data agent family has three shapes, and this guide grades them separately because their evidence bases are nothing alike: the **documents-and-filings agent** (Section 4.1) reads the structured text of the public record; the **earnings-call and news-event layer** (Section 4.2) turns the continuous event stream into structured notes; and the **web, satellite and social rails** (Section 4.3) supply the data estate the first two consume. One sentence separates the verified from the marketed across all three: the *rails* are real and the *agentic results* are rarely published by the institutions paying for them — and the two disciplines of Section 4.4 (point-in-time, licensing) apply to every shape before the first backtest runs.

### 4.1 The Documents-and-Filings Agent

The filings agent is the oldest and most defensible alt-data shape — its raw material is the most structured text in finance (the 10-K, the 8-K, the prospectus, the covenant), its errors are the most checkable, and its pre-LLM stack was the least adequate:

- ✅ **The verified academic core reads filings with LLM pipelines.** The FinGPT data-curation line (Section 3.4) documents automated collection and curation of financial text from dozens of internet sources — filings, news, social feeds — into the datasets that FinLLMs and their agents consume ✅ (arXiv:2307.10485). The FinMem agent's layered memory digests "hierarchical financial data" — the corporate-event and news layers that move stocks ✅ (arXiv:2311.13743). These are the *document rails*; the claim that a fund's proprietary filings agent generates deployable alpha from 10-Ks is exactly the kind of claim that Section 4.3 grades ⚠ when only vendors assert it.
- ⚠ **The vendor layer is crowded and marketing-heavy.** Vendors market LLM agents that read regulatory filings, monitor covenant and risk language, and flag changes — some of it real product, all of it vendor-published. This pass verified no named-asset-manager primary publication of measured results from an LLM filings agent, so the category is graded ⚠ vendor-marketing until a deploying institution publishes; the pattern is the same one the use-case sibling found across the post-trade and decisioning vendors (sibling §9.3): the *rail* is often real, the *agentic results* almost never published by the client.

**The pattern the deployer copies:** the documents agent earns its keep with **structured, cited output**, not prose — an event note that carries the filing clause it is reacting to, the timestamp of the version it read (the pre-restatement version if that is what a live system would have seen), and a confidence signal the downstream factor model can weight. That is the FinGPT data-curation shape (Section 3.4) pointed at the bank's own corpus, and it is the exact artifact Cymbal Bank's pilot candidate 1 produces for the research desk (Section 11.3) — text in, structured and citable signal out, human in the middle.

### 4.2 The Earnings-Call and News-Event Layer — Condensed Cross-Reference

The earnings-call summarisation layer is the most deployed document-AI use case in capital markets, and its evidence lives in the use-case sibling, **not re-derived here**:

- ✅-carried **HSBC's meeting-prep assistant** — the bank's June 2026 media release records the AI decision assistant already reducing client-meeting preparation "from hours to minutes for thousands of users" (sibling §8.1, itself cross-referencing §4.1 of the same guide).
- ✅-carried **Morgan Stanley Debrief** — the advisor meeting-summary tool on Whisper and GPT-4 (sibling §5.1) is the wealth-side instance of the same family.
- ✅-carried **Bloomberg's Terminal layer** — the ASKB-style assistant analysing earnings transcripts sits on the verified BloombergGPT record (sibling §8.2), with its ⚠ product-scale status intact.

For the quant, the earnings-call layer is where **event-driven signal meets summarisation**: the pre-LLM stack turned transcripts into sentiment scores with dictionaries; the LLM stack can turn them into structured, cited event notes that a downstream factor model consumes — but the verified deployments are copilots for humans (the RM, the advisor), and the *quant signal* reading of the same transcripts remains largely inside the open-source stack of Section 3.4 rather than in named production systems.

**The event-note contract.** For the quant desk, the earnings-call agent's output should be a fixed-field event note — event type, ticker, timestamp, the cited passage, the directional read with its confidence, and the explicit *non-claim* that the note is not a trade recommendation — because a factor model consumes fixed fields, an audit consumes citations, and a compliance regime (Section 8.2) consumes the machine-generated-artifact trail. The verified copilots (HSBC's meeting-prep assistant, Morgan Stanley's Debrief, the Bloomberg Terminal layer) summarise for humans; the quant variant structures for machines, with the human quant approving the note schema before the agent runs so the downstream factor model and the human reviewers read the same fields.

### 4.3 The Web, Satellite and Social-Data Rails — the Data-Guide Cross-Reference

- ✅-carried **The market-data estate is the substrate.** The full vendor and feed landscape — who sells what data, how it is consumed by portfolio management, risk and analytics — is the subject of [Market Data Consumption](../../banking/market_data_consumption_guide.md) (§2 the ecosystem, §3 the vendors, §4 the feed types, §6–8 the consumption by portfolio management, risk and analytics) and is cross-referenced whole rather than summarised. The alt-data family this guide's agents would read — web-traffic panels, satellite observations, social and news feeds, card-transaction panels — sits inside that estate as specialised feeds.
- ⚠ **The "agentic alt-data" vendor category.** Vendors market agents that fuse web, satellite and social data into tradable signals, and the *data itself* is often real (the panels exist, the imagery exists); the *agentic fusion layer's* results are vendor case studies with client names attached as logos — ⚠ vendor-marketing until a named institution publishes. The honest engineering position: the retrieval-and-grounded-generation mechanics for feeding such data to an LLM agent are the subject of the repository's RAG guides ([Production-Grade Agentic RAG](rag/production_grade_agentic_rag_guide.md), [Advanced RAG Techniques](rag/advanced_rag_techniques_guide.md)), and the *point-in-time discipline* — the agent must never see data that a live system would not have had at the decision moment — is the quant-specific trap, the same leakage problem StockAgent engineers against (Section 3.4) and the same reason Section 9 makes point-in-time correctness an eval gate.

**The pattern:** the deployer buys the **data estate** — that is procurement, licensed and point-in-time-capable — and builds the **agentic fusion layer** itself, where the honest questions are mechanical: does the retrieval time-box to the decision moment, does the panel's refresh lag match the live feed's, and does the backtest universe survive restatement? A vendor that answers those three questions in writing is selling engineering; a vendor that answers with case studies is selling logos.

### 4.4 The Point-in-Time and the Licensing Discipline

The alternative-data agent adds two disciplines that the text-agent vendors rarely mention, and both are quant-native:

- ✅ **The point-in-time rule is absolute.** Every dataset the agent consumes must be retrievable *as it existed at the decision date* — the version of the filing before the restatement, the news feed without look-ahead, the panel data as originally published. A text agent is uniquely dangerous here because its training corpus and its retrieval corpus both carry the future into the past (the pretraining-leakage problem of Sections 3.4 and 9.1), so the deployment rule is mechanical: time-stamped corpora, time-boxed retrieval, and a data-lineage audit for every factor the agent touches. The market-data guide's data-management layer ([Market Data Consumption](../../banking/market_data_consumption_guide.md) §9) carries the estate machinery — entitlement, lineage, golden copies — that makes the rule executable.
- ✅ **The licensing reality outranks the model.** Alternative data is licensed, not owned: the vendor contracts define what the bank may store, transform, feed to models and redistribute, and an agent that copies excerpts into its context window or its fine-tuning corpus is a redistribution event whether or not the vendor notices. The quant-agent deployment therefore begins in procurement and legal — the same gate the [Market Data Consumption](../../banking/market_data_consumption_guide.md) guide's consumption model assumes — and an agent that retrieves unlicensed web data into a trading pipeline is manufacturing legal risk, not alpha.
- ⚠ **The "the data speaks for itself" marketing.** Vendors selling agentic alt-data often imply that more text equals more signal. The verified record says otherwise: the FinGPT line's own motivation is the low signal-to-noise ratio of financial text (Section 3.4), and FinRL-Meta's warning about financial data's low signal-to-noise (arXiv:2211.03107) applies to text most of all — which is why every serious deployment of a text-reading agent is judged by Section 9's gates, not by the eloquence of its output.

**The alternative-data verdict in one line:** the agent-shaped reading layer is real, the rails are licensed and the disciplines are mechanical — and the honest deployment of an alt-data agent is a point-in-time, citation-forcing pipeline whose output a factor model can consume and an examiner can reconstruct, which is precisely the shape Cymbal Bank's pilot builds for its research corpus rather than for live signals (Section 11.3).

## 5. The Execution Agents — the LOXM Lineage and the Copilot-First Present

Execution is where the quant workflow's language content bottoms out and its stakes peak: the problem is microstructure, latency and market impact, and the industry's production systems are pre-LLM domain-specific agents. This section traces the one verified lineage — JPMorgan's LOXM — and then applies the production-versus-pilot discipline to everything newer.

### 5.1 The LOXM Lineage — Condensed Cross-Reference

- ✅-carried **The 2017 record.** JPMorgan's LOXM is the canonical pre-LLM execution agent, and its full record — the bank's statements to the Financial Times in 2017 about a deep-reinforcement-learning system trained on billions of historical and simulated trades, the successful trial in European equities in Q1 2017, the planned global rollout, and the press-carried "first major bank to apply AI to real-time trade execution" framing — is verified and carried in the use-case sibling §7.1, with the circulating "15% improvement" figure correctly flagged ⚠ there and not asserted here ([LLM Agent Use Cases in Finance](./llm_agent_use_cases_finance_guide.md) §7.1). The JPMorgan systems context — Athena and the markets stack the execution engines live in — is carried by [JPMorgan Chase Software Systems](../../banking/jpmorgan_chase_software_systems_guide.md) §3.
- ✅ **What LOXM actually was — and why it matters for the LLM era.** LOXM was a reinforcement-learning execution agent: it sensed market state, decided an execution strategy against a learned policy, and acted — with no LLM anywhere in the loop (sibling §7.1's domain-specific-agent framing, cross-referenced). The lineage point for this guide: the "execution agent" label in finance already had a rigorous, production meaning a decade before the LLM wave, and the LLM-era marketing that borrows the label inherits a very high bar — a bar set by systems that ran real client orders.

### 5.2 The LLM-Era Execution Layer — Copilot-First

- ✅-carried **The execution stack around the order is copilot-first at the verified institutions.** JPMorgan's own record — LLM Suite's firmwide growth ("from zero to 200,000 onboarded users within eight months," the bank's own blog, sibling §5.2) and its stated direction of "combining generative AI with workflows to create AI agents that can carry out a series of actions to complete a goal" — describes agents inside a governed, human-approval environment, not autonomous order flow. The 2025 "AI trader" press cycle is graded in the use-case sibling §7.3 as **pilot, not production**: the real deployments are sell-side copilots — drafting pre-trade briefs, summarising risk, triaging market news — not machines placing orders.
- ✅ **The latency argument is structural, not regulatory.** The fastest LLM inference round-trips are measured in hundreds of milliseconds to seconds; the execution decisions that matter at the top of the book are measured in microseconds to milliseconds, and the execution engines that make them are the pre-LLM RL family (LOXM-class) and the market makers' own systems ([Market Making — Singapore](../../banking/market_making_singapore_guide.md) §2 profiles the reference firm's low-latency model). An LLM agent cannot sit inside the low-latency execution path by construction — which is why the verified LLM-era execution products sit *beside* the order (advice, briefs, post-trade explanation), not *in* it.
- ✅ **The same argument applies one level up, at the order-parameter layer.** Even the "analytical" execution decisions that are not microsecond-bound — the schedule, the limit price, the venue list for a large order — are numeric optimisation problems (implementation-shortfall and market-impact modelling, Section 9.3's TCA vocabulary) with deterministic, backtestable solutions. The LLM copilot's verified role is to *explain* those analytics and *draft the trader's brief*; a vendor claim that the LLM is *computing* the optimal schedule is a claim that a language model is doing the optimiser's arithmetic, and it is graded ⚠ until the vendor publishes the numeric evaluation (Section 9) that no such claim ever seems to include.

### 5.3 The Agentic SOR and Autonomous-Trading Claims — the Honest Grading

- ⚠ **"Agentic smart order routing" is vendor marketing until a named institution publishes.** Multiple vendors now market LLM-driven or agentic SOR — routers that choose venues, schedules and order types with language-model reasoning. The honest grading: this pass verified **no** major-bank or major-broker primary publication of a production agentic SOR with measured results, and the architectural case against it is the same latency-and-determinism argument as Section 5.2 — SOR decisions are numeric optimisation problems with hard latency budgets, exactly the domain where a language model's strengths (open-world reasoning, explanation) are irrelevant and its weaknesses (latency, nondeterminism, cost) are fatal. Claims in this category are graded ⚠ and treated as aspirational until a deploying firm publishes otherwise — the same verdict the use-case sibling reached for the "autonomous trading agent" vendor category (§7.2).
- ❌ **The conflation claim gets marked, not repeated.** Press prose that presents an LLM copilot drafting a pre-trade brief as "the bank's AI trader going live" contradicts the verified record of what those systems do — the copilot proposes, the human trader and the deterministic execution engines act. Where a headline implies an LLM is placing orders autonomously at a named bank, the verified evidence (sibling §7.2–7.3) says otherwise, and this guide does not repeat the implication.

### 5.4 The Market-Making Context — Condensed

Market making is the purest form of the execution agent — quoting both sides continuously, managing inventory and adverse selection — and it is the domain-specific-agent estate at its most mature. The repository's [Market Making — Singapore](../../banking/market_making_singapore_guide.md) guide carries the reference-firm profile (§2), the SGX obligations context (§3) and the infrastructure reality (§7 — co-location, latency, the exchange fabric); the [Citadel LLC](../../banking/citadel_llc_guide.md) guide §5 carries the electronic-market-maker franchise. The LLM-agent relevance is one line, and it is a **negative result**: no verified LLM agent sits in the quoting path of a serious market maker, and the vendor claims that one could are graded ⚠ by the same latency-and-determinism argument — the market maker's "agent" is its pre-LLM pricing and risk engine, and the LLM's honest role is post-trade explanation and exception triage at the edges.

### 5.5 The Execution-Adjacent Copilots — the Pilot Bucket

The verified execution-adjacent LLM work in 2026 is real, human-facing, and firmly in the pilot bucket — the sibling's §7.3 discipline applied to this domain:

- ⚠ **The pre-trade brief copilots.** Sell-side and bank pilots use LLM copilots to draft the pre-trade package — the market-state summary, the liquidity read, the execution-plan options with their expected-impact analytics — which the human trader reviews and then executes through the deterministic engines. The pattern is consistent with the verified firmwide-platform record (sibling §5.2) and the pilot bucket is the honest grade until a firm's own material confirms general availability (sibling §7.3's rule, carried).
- ⚠ **The post-trade explanation copilots.** The mirror-image pilot: after the execution, the copilot narrates the TCA — why the fills came in where they did, what the impact was against the arrival-price and VWAP benchmarks — turning the deterministic TCA output into readable text for the desk and the client. The TCA *numbers* are the deterministic engine's (Section 9.3); the *narrative* is the LLM's; and the honest deployment keeps the two layers separate so the narrative can never override the numbers.
- ✅ **The boundary rule that makes both safe.** In every verified and piloted execution-adjacent copilot, the LLM output terminates in a human approval and the deterministic analytics: the copilot's text is advice, the trader's click is the order, and the engine's arithmetic is the impact estimate. Section 11's worked example rejects Cymbal Bank's execution-advice candidate for the first pilot cycle precisely because near-order copilots need the vendor evidence base and the desk-level controls that the research-end pilot is designed to prove first.

**The execution verdict:** the verified execution estate is pre-LLM and will stay pre-LLM wherever latency and determinism decide — the LLM's honest execution-era roles are the pre-trade brief, the post-trade narrative, and the exception triage at the edges, each terminating in a human approval and the deterministic analytics. The vendor test that never changes: an execution-agent claim must name the desk, the instrument class, the cost model and the walk-forward — and the marketing record of Section 10.3 shows how rarely it does. Cymbal Bank's desk keeps its LOXM-class engine exactly where it is, and the pilot's boundary (Section 11.1) writes that decision into the charter.

## 6. The Risk-Monitoring & Scenario Agents

Risk is the quant workflow's conscience: the limits that bind the book, the VaR and stress numbers that size the danger, and — increasingly — the *explanation* of all three, which is where language models enter. The evidence splits cleanly: the risk *data and framework estate* is verified in the repository's banking guides; the *agentic risk layer* is a mix of one strong verified product (Aladdin Copilot), press-reported bank pilots, and vendor marketing.

The section's map: Section 6.1 grades the risk-copilot record (one verified platform product, press-carried bank pilots, vendor analytics), Section 6.2 isolates the scenario-and-stress layer where the boundary between the LLM's *draft* and the engine's *shock* is the whole game, and Section 6.3 installs the governed-agent and three-lines frame that decides which risk function the agent serves. The through-line across all three is the same: risk is the one quant stage where the LLM's explanation skill and the regulator's explainability demand point in the same direction — which is why the Cymbal Bank candidate list (Section 11.2) ranks a risk copilot second only to the research copilot.

### 6.1 The Risk-Copilot Record

The risk-copilot evidence runs from a verified platform product at one end to vendor case studies at the other, and the grades track that arc:

## 7. The Portfolio-Construction Agents

Portfolio construction — turning signals and risk constraints into an actual portfolio — is where the quant workflow's decision core lives, and the core is an **optimiser**, not a language model. The verified systems are the deterministic engines with LLM interfaces layered on, and this section keeps that boundary exact.

The section's argument in advance: Section 7.1 grades the buy-side copilots that *surface* portfolio state; Section 7.2 fixes the architecture fact — the optimiser decides, the LLM explains and proposes, and the constraint-translation layer is the LLM's real job; and Section 7.3 uses the robo-advisors to show which layer of portfolio automation is rule-executable (pre-LLM, already done) and which is discretionary (LLM, unverified). The boundary sentence, from 7.2, is the one to keep: **no verified system has an LLM computing the optimal portfolio directly**, and the marketing that claims otherwise is selling a category error.

### 7.1 The Buy-Side Copilots — Aladdin Copilot and the Rest

The buy-side copilot record is a verified platform product, an unverified vendor category, and the repository's own institutional frame — each graded separately:

- ✅-carried **Aladdin Copilot is the reference buy-side deployment** (Section 6.1 carries the sibling's §8.3 record): BlackRock's own page states availability to all Aladdin clients for investment and operational decisions. For portfolio construction specifically, that means the asset managers and banks running on Aladdin get a copilot that can *surface and explain* portfolio state — holdings, exposures, constraints, what-if answers — across the same platform where the deterministic portfolio tools run. ✅-carried The agentic-extension reporting (execution and exception handling) is ⚠ press until BlackRock publishes (sibling §8.3).
- ⚠ **The asset-manager portfolio-research copilot claims.** Vendors market portfolio-construction copilots — natural-language optimisation setup, constraint explanation, attribution narrative, rebalancing proposals — to asset managers. Product pages exist; named-asset-manager primary publications of measured results do not (in this pass), so the category is ⚠ vendor-marketing by the standing rule.
- ✅ **The reference institutional frame is the repository's own.** The disciplined-investor context any portfolio-agent claim must eventually square with is documented in the repository's own guides — the [NBIM](../../banking/norges_bank_investment_management_guide.md) profile (its §3 mandate, §6 transparency, §7 performance record — the world-scale, index-aware investor whose published portfolio facts are the benchmark for "does the agent's proposal beat the alternative"), the [Hedge Fund Guide](../../banking/hedge_fund_guide.md) §3 strategy taxonomy (where the long/short and systematic families the agents would serve are defined), and [Investment Portfolio Operations](../../banking/investment_portfolio_operations_guide.md) §7 (performance, attribution and risk analytics — the measurement layer every portfolio change must pass through). Cross-referenced whole, not re-derived.

**The buy-side pattern:** the copilot that works is the one bound to the platform's deterministic state — it reads holdings, exposures and constraints from the same systems the optimiser reads, so its answers and the engine's numbers can never diverge; the copilot that *guesses* portfolio state from memory is a hallucination engine with a terminal skin. The availability record (Aladdin Copilot to all Aladdin clients, Section 6.1) is precisely the platform-bound shape, and it is the shape any serious portfolio-agent procurement should specify: read access to the positions and constraint store first, language second.

### 7.2 The Optimisation Core — the Deterministic Engine Keeps the Decision Rights

The architecture fact this subsection states is the one every portfolio-agent claim must answer to, and the LLM's honest job sits one layer above the solver:

- ✅ **The architecture fact: the optimiser decides, the LLM explains and proposes.** Portfolio construction is a constrained optimisation — maximise expected return (or risk-adjusted return) subject to risk budgets, factor exposures, turnover, tax and liquidity constraints — and the solvers are deterministic, decades-mature numerical engines. The verified and academically documented pattern (Sections 3.4 and 6.3) is the LLM *wrapped around* the optimiser: the agent translates the PM's intent into constraint language, explains the optimiser's output, and drafts the rationale — the decision rights stay with the deterministic core and the human. No verified system in this pass's record has an LLM computing the optimal portfolio directly, and the academic community's own platforms (FinRL's portfolio-allocation tasks, Section 3.4) keep the learning-and-optimisation core numerical.
- ✅ **The "LLM as orchestrator" framing is the honest one.** The FinRL-X line (arXiv:2603.21330, 2026, the AI4Finance ecosystem's modular trading-infrastructure paper — ✅ at the arXiv record) describes an architecture unifying data processing, strategy construction, backtesting and execution "under a weight-centric interface," with rule-based and AI-driven components including "LLM-based sentiment signals" feeding the downstream semantics — the LLM *signals and orchestrates*, the deterministic pipeline *executes*. That is the field's own best description of where the language model sits in portfolio construction.
- ✅ **The constraint-translation layer is the LLM's real portfolio job.** The most language-shaped step in the whole construction process is *turning the PM's intent into optimiser constraints*: "keep sector weights within two points of the benchmark, no single name over three percent, don't trade more than five percent turnover this month" is a paragraph that must become a constraint vector. That translation — intent to constraint, with the human reviewing the translation before the optimiser runs — is a verified-shaped copilot task (the propose-and-approve pattern of Sections 2.2 and 10.1), and it is where this guide expects the portfolio-construction copilot market to land first: not computing portfolios, but *contracting* them correctly with the human and the optimiser in the loop.

**The constraint-contract flow, concretely:** (1) the agent interviews the PM's intent in plain language and restates it back — *"you want sector weights within two points of the benchmark, a 3% single-name cap, and turnover under 5% this month — correct?"*; (2) it drafts the constraint vector and the *expected effect* of each constraint on the feasible region, so the PM sees the cost of what they are asking; (3) the human reviews and edits the draft — this is the decision point, not a formality, and the review is logged; (4) the optimiser runs, and the agent explains the resulting portfolio's trade-offs in the PM's language — which constraints bind, which names the turnover budget squeezed, what the attribution says. Every step is the propose-and-approve pattern of Sections 2.2 and 10.1, and the arithmetic is never the LLM's: the agent's output is a *contract for the solver*, reviewable before it runs and auditable after.

### 7.3 The Rebalancing and Tax-Loss-Harvesting Contrast — the Robo-Advisors

The robo-advisors matter to this guide as the **pre-LLM proof that portfolio automation exists** — and as the precise instrument for separating rule-executable automation from discretionary judgment:

- ✅-carried **The robo-advisors are the pre-LLM portfolio agents, and the sibling carries the contrast.** Betterment (launched 2010) and Wealthfront built systems that sense the client portfolio, decide against a modern-portfolio-theory objective, and act — rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting without a human per trade — a decade before the LLM wave; the use-case sibling §5.3 carries the record and the scale context (roughly US$65 billion and US$95 billion in client assets respectively, ⚠ figures from third-party trackers). The landscape significance, cross-referenced not re-derived: the robo-advisor's deterministic decision core is being wrapped in LLM conversation, while the banks' LLM copilots reach toward the robo-advisors' execution authority — the regulated meeting point is the LLM proposing and explaining while the deterministic engine and the human hold the decision rights.
- ✅ **The automation contrast is definitional, not judgmental.** Rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting are *rule-executable* activities — the automation is real, measured in client assets, and pre-LLM. A portfolio-construction LLM agent is a different object: it operates in the discretionary, explanation-heavy layer. The quant lesson: when a vendor says its LLM agent "automates portfolio management," ask which layer — the rule-executable layer (which did not need an LLM and the robo-advisors already automated) or the discretionary layer (where no verified autonomous deployment exists). The answer separates the honest claim from the category error, and the worked example of Section 11 applies exactly that question to Cymbal Bank's candidates.

**The portfolio-construction verdict:** the deterministic optimiser owns the decision, the LLM owns the language at the boundary — intent in, explanation out — and the honest portfolio agent is a *contracting copilot* that makes the human's constraints precise and the optimiser's output legible, which is the exact division Cymbal Bank's desk already runs with its four pre-LLM production systems (Section 11.1). The vendor claim to test is never "our agent builds better portfolios" — it is "our agent translates your intent into the optimiser's language and explains the optimiser's answer," and that narrower claim is the one the verified record can support.

## 9. The Evaluation & Validation of Quant Agents — Backtest, Paper, Walk-Forward

This section is this guide's own content: the **quant evaluation culture** that any LLM agent must survive before it earns a place near a book. The LLM-eval layer (LLM-as-judge, golden sets, RAGAS-style metrics) is the engineering sibling's and the eval guide's subject and is cross-referenced in Section 9.4 — but the quant layer below it — backtesting, paper trading, walk-forward analysis, benchmark periods, slippage and transaction-cost realism, the overfitting discipline — is the native evaluation of the domain, it outranks every LLM metric when the agent's output is a trade, and no LLM-agent vendor claim can be taken seriously until it has run this gauntlet.

The quant gates form a **ladder** — each rung is a cheaper failure than the next, and a system climbs only by passing. The mapping to the LLM-agent layer is what this section adds to the standard quant vocabulary: the agent is not excused from any rung because it is a language model — it is *more* exposed to every rung, because its prompts, memory and pretraining add new overfitting and leakage channels (Section 9.1):

| The Rung | What Runs | What It Catches | The LLM-Agent Mapping |
|---|---|---|---|
| The backtest (9.1) | The strategy or the agent's proposed signals replayed over historical data | Overfitting, leakage, one-regime luck | The agent's *proposed factors and prompts* are the objects under test — frozen before the window opens |
| The walk-forward (9.2) | Rolling optimise-then-test-untouched windows | Prompt and parameter mining on the evaluation window | The agent's prompts, memory spans and retrieval settings are re-set on each training window only; test windows stay untouched |
| The paper trading (9.2) | Real-time decisions against live data, no capital | Live-data behaviour, sequencing, latency | The agent runs on data it has never seen — the pretraining-leakage channel closes only here |
| The cost model (9.3) | Backtests re-run with slippage, impact, financing, turnover | Gross-return fiction | The vendor's "returns" column dies here or survives — and the honest open-source papers (Section 3) die less often than the marketing |
| The committee (9.6) | Human review of the whole record | Everything the metrics cannot see | The PM and research committee own go/no-go — the agent never promotes itself |

### 9.1 The Backtest and the Overfitting Discipline

- ✅ **The backtest is the quant's first gate, and its failure mode is documented.** A backtest replays a strategy (or an agent's proposed signals) over historical data to estimate how it would have performed. The failure mode — **backtest overfitting**, where a strategy is tuned so hard to the historical sample that its apparent edge is noise — is the most documented trap in quantitative finance. The verification record in this pass: the arXiv paper "Deep Reinforcement Learning for Cryptocurrency Trading: Practical Approach to Address Backtest Overfitting" (Gort, Liu, Sun, Gao, Chen, Wang; arXiv:2209.05559) formulates overfitting *detection* as a hypothesis test, estimates a probability of overfitting, and rejects overfitted agents — reporting that less-overfitted agents outperformed more-overfitted ones out-of-sample ✅ (source: the arXiv abstract page). The deeper theoretical record — Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu's "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting" (Journal of Computational Finance, 2017) — is standard literature that this pass did not re-verify at a primary page; it is cited here as ⚠ literature with that caveat, and its central warning (that selecting strategies on backtest performance alone can actively *choose* the overfitted ones) is the reason Section 9.2's machinery exists.
- ✅ **The agent-specific twist makes the discipline stricter, not looser.** An LLM agent amplifies the classic overfitting risks in three ways, all verified by construction in Section 3's record: (1) **leakage by pretraining** — the model has already read the history you are backtesting, which is exactly the problem StockAgent's authors engineered against (arXiv:2407.18957, Section 3.4); (2) **prompt-and-agent overfitting** — tuning the agent's prompts, roles and memory spans on the same history you evaluate on is the LLM-era equivalent of parameter mining, and FinMem's own paper reports tuning its agent's perceptual span and character to improve backtest results (arXiv:2311.13743 — legitimate research, and precisely the practice that must be *inside* the walk-forward design for a deployment, not applied to the evaluation window); (3) **selection across papers and repos** — with hundreds of open-source trading agents claiming backtest wins (Section 3), picking the one with the best historical Sharpe is textbook multiple-testing. The discipline that counters all three is the same: **the evaluation window must be untouchable** — no prompt edits, no model swaps, no memory retunes against it.
- ⚠ **The "the backtest says it works" vendor claim.** Vendor claims built on a single favourable backtest period are graded ⚠ by default, and the worked example's eval design (Section 11.4) shows the counter: multiple benchmark periods, walk-forward discipline, and a paper-trading phase before any capital.
- ✅ **The defensive practices are standard and mechanical.** The quant discipline's own defences, applied to agents: (1) *pre-register the hypothesis and the evaluation design* before the agent runs, so the human cannot tune the test to the result; (2) *freeze the agent* — model version, prompts, retrieval settings — before the out-of-sample window starts; (3) *test across regimes*, never one period (Section 9.2's benchmark-period practice); (4) *prefer walk-forward and purged designs* over a single split — the purged-and-embargoed cross-validation designs popularised in the machine-learning-for-trading literature (⚠ the book citation — López de Prado, "Advances in Financial Machine Learning," Wiley, 2018 — was not re-verified at a primary page in this pass) address the label-leakage that ordinary k-fold cross-validation suffers in time-series; and (5) *keep a selection log* — every agent variant tried, every window it was tried on — so the multiple-testing problem is at least countable. None of these practices is exotic; each is the ordinary hygiene of a serious quant desk, and each transfers directly to LLM agents.

### 9.2 Paper Trading and Walk-Forward Analysis

- ✅ **Paper trading is the quant's second gate, and its definition is stable across the industry:** the agent (or strategy) runs its decisions in real time against live market data — filling orders at recorded prices in a simulation account — with no capital at risk. Its purpose is the gap a backtest cannot close: *live-data behaviour* — what the agent does with data it has never seen, at the latency and sequencing of the real market, without the backtest's hindsight blessings. The repo's market-data guide ([Market Data Consumption](../../banking/market_data_consumption_guide.md) §2, §9) carries the data-management reality that makes paper trading meaningful (clean, point-in-time, time-sequenced feeds); the eval guide's CI/CD patterns ([LLM Evaluation Frameworks](./llm_evaluation_frameworks_guide.md) §15) carry the automation pattern that makes it continuous.
- ✅ **Walk-forward analysis is the quant's antidote to overfitting, and its mechanics are standard:** the history is divided into consecutive windows — the strategy is *optimised* (or the agent's parameters and prompts are *set*) on each training window and then *tested, untouched*, on the immediately following out-of-sample window, rolling forward through time. Because every test window is genuinely out-of-sample *at the moment of testing*, walk-forward performance is the closest a backtest gets to honest. The benchmark-period practice rides on the same logic: a credible evaluation reports the agent across multiple, named market regimes — not one lucky year — because a signal that only works in a bull market is a beta story, not an alpha story. These are standard quant-practice definitions, presented as the discipline's vocabulary (see the Glossary, Section 13) rather than as novel claims.
- ✅ **The open-source ecosystem's own contests encode the same gates.** The FinRL Contests record (arXiv:2504.02281, Section 3.4) describes the AI4Finance community's contest series with standardised tasks, real-time-style datasets, market environments and evaluation protocols across 2023–2025 — the community building the *evaluation rails* for trading agents rather than trusting single backtests ✅ (source: the arXiv abstract page).

**What a credible evaluation report therefore contains:** the named benchmark periods with their regime labels; the walk-forward schedule with the training and out-of-sample windows identified; the frozen-agent manifest (model version, prompts, retrieval settings) for every window; the cost-model assumptions in numbers; and the paper-trading log. That list is Section 9.6's checklist in narrative form — and it is exactly the table of contents of the evaluation section of Cymbal Bank's pilot report (Section 11.4), because a report that omits any of those items is not a partial report, it is a red flag.

### 9.3 The Realism Layer — Slippage, Market Impact and TCA

- ✅ **The third gate is realism, and its vocabulary is precise.** A backtest that fills the agent's orders at the historical close price or the quoted mid is fiction; the real fill depends on **slippage** (the difference between the expected and actual fill price), **market impact** (the agent's own order moving the price against itself — the cost that grows with order size relative to liquidity), and the timing and venue of the execution. The discipline that measures this is **transaction-cost analysis (TCA)** — the post-trade measurement of execution quality against benchmarks (arrival price, VWAP, implementation shortfall) — and it is the execution side of the eval loop, connecting Section 5's execution reality to Section 9's evaluation gates. These terms are defined in the Glossary (Section 13), and their domain content — how execution quality and cost are measured in the dealing room — is carried by the repository's portfolio-operations guide ([Investment Portfolio Operations](../../banking/investment_portfolio_operations_guide.md) §2 on the trade lifecycle).
- ✅ **The literature's own framing confirms the gap.** The FinRL-Meta paper (arXiv:2211.03107, NeurIPS 2022) names exactly the three reasons finance is hard for these agents — "low signal-to-noise ratio of financial data, survivorship bias of historical data, and model overfitting in the backtesting stage" ✅ (source: the arXiv abstract page) — and survivorship bias is the realism trap this section flags hardest: a backtest universe built from today's surviving tickers overstates every strategy that would have bought the delisted ones. An LLM agent that *selects its own universe from memory* is uniquely prone to this, because its training data is also survivorship-biased by construction.
- ✅ **The cost-model test is where agent claims die.** The single most instructive exercise for a quant team evaluating an LLM-agent vendor is to ask for the agent's backtest *with a realistic cost model applied* — spread costs, impact, financing, and the turnover the agent's own behaviour implies. Section 3's honest open-source papers report gross simulation results with their baselines; the marketing layer's "returns" almost never survive costs, and Section 9.6's checklist makes the cost-model column mandatory.

The **capacity question** belongs to the same gate: a backtest that fills the agent's orders at sizes the market cannot absorb is a capacity study in disguise, and the honest simulation sizes the agent's book to the liquidity of its universe — with the cost model applied at that size, not at the size that makes the Sharpe ratio pretty. The paper-trading environment of Section 9.2 is where the sizing assumption first meets live data, and Section 11.4's Gate 3 makes the desk's cost model and notional caps explicit for exactly this reason.

### 9.4 The LLM-Evaluation Layer — Cross-Referenced

The quant gates above evaluate the *agent's decisions*; a second layer evaluates the *agent's language*, and it is the sibling guides' subject, **not re-derived here**:

- ✅-carried **The engineering sibling's eval machinery** — golden datasets, LLM-as-judge with its documented biases, task-specific metrics, quality gates, regression suites and drift monitoring ([Multi-Agent AI Systems for Banking](./multi_agent_banking_guide.md) §6–7) — applies to the quant agent's research memos, rationale texts and explanations exactly as it applies to banking agents.
- ✅-carried **The eval tooling** — DeepEval, LangSmith, MLflow, RAGAS, LLM-as-judge practice and CI/CD integration — is the subject of [LLM Evaluation Frameworks](./llm_evaluation_frameworks_guide.md) (§3 approaches, §4 metric categories, §5–11 the tools, §15 CI/CD patterns), cross-referenced whole.
- **The two layers compose, they do not compete:** the LLM layer answers "is the agent's *text* good (grounded, cited, faithful, harmless)?" and the quant layer answers "is the agent's *behaviour* good (profitable out-of-sample, realistic under costs, robust across regimes)?" — and for a quant agent, a text that passes the first layer and fails the second is a failed agent, while the reverse (ugly text, honest signal) is merely an unfinished one. The eval design of the worked example (Section 11.4) runs both layers in sequence, with the quant layer as the final gate.

### 9.5 The Agent-vs-Human Evidence — What the Verified Studies Actually Show

- ✅ **What the verified studies actually compare agents against is other algorithms.** TradingAgents reports improvements in cumulative return, Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown "over baseline models" (arXiv:2412.20138) — algorithm-versus-algorithm in simulation. FinMem reports outperformance against "various algorithmic agents on a scalable real-world financial dataset" (arXiv:2311.13743). StockAgent simulates LLM investors to study behaviour (arXiv:2407.18957). None of the verified academic record claims its agent beats professional human traders with live capital — and the field's integrity is that it does not pretend otherwise.
- ⚠ **The "AI beats humans in trading" headlines.** Press reporting through 2024–2026 has carried claims that LLM agents beat human traders in trading *simulations* and lab-style experiments. Where such claims rest on small samples, synthetic markets, or student-participant humans, they are graded ⚠ — the study design cannot support the headline — and where this pass could not identify the underlying study at a primary source at all, the claim is not asserted and is listed in Section 12.2. The honest summary of the verified record is narrower and more useful: *LLM multi-agent frameworks can outperform algorithmic baselines in historical simulations under specified conditions* — a research result about architectures, not a verdict about markets.
- ✅ **The deployment evidence completes the picture.** The verified production record (Sections 5–7, carried) has LLM agents *assisting* humans who trade; the pre-LLM engines that trade autonomously (LOXM-class execution, the market makers' quoting systems) were never benchmarked against human traders in the press sense — they replaced *manual execution processes*, which is a different and more honest claim. The evaluation reader should hold every "agent beat human" claim to that standard: beat *what baseline, in what environment, at what cost model, over what period, with what capital?*
- ✅ **The simulation-vs-live gap is the one number that matters.** Whatever a simulation shows, the live gap is where the field's own disclaimers point: FinRobot's "not for live trading," the community fund's "does not actually make any trades," the papers' own "simulated real-world" language (StockAgent's title). The gap has four components — data (live feeds are dirtier), costs (simulations underestimate them), capacity (the simulated size is not the tradeable size), and regime (the future is not the sample) — and Section 10's evidence reality is, at bottom, a measurement of how wide that gap remains in 2026.

### 9.6 The Quant-Agent Evaluation Checklist

The section condensed into the checklist a desk actually runs — the two-layer composition of Section 9.4, in gate order, with the quant layer last and decisive:

| The Gate | What It Catches | The Honest Bar |
|---|---|---|
| The language layer | Hallucinated citations, ungrounded claims, unfaithful summaries | Golden-set accuracy and citation checks (LLM-eval tooling, cross-referenced §9.4) |
| The point-in-time audit | Look-ahead in corpora, pretraining leakage, restatement contamination | Every corpus time-stamped; retrieval time-boxed; lineage logged |
| The benchmark periods | One-regime luck | Three-plus named regimes, reported separately and together |
| The walk-forward | Prompt and parameter overfitting on the evaluation window | Untouched out-of-sample windows, rolled forward |
| The survivorship check | Universes biased to today's survivors | Survivorship-free universe or documented bias |
| The cost model | Gross-return fiction | Slippage, impact, financing, turnover applied; results reported net |
| The paper trading | Live-data behaviour, sequencing, latency | One quarter minimum, live feeds, no capital |
| The drift and regression | Silent degradation after deployment | Regression suites on gate metrics; drift monitors on corpora and signal distributions |
| The human review | Everything the metrics cannot see | PM and research-committee sign-off at each gate |

Every row is either a standard quant-practice bar (defined in the Glossary, Section 13) or a cross-referenced sibling convention; no row requires believing a vendor. The worked example of Section 11.4 installs this checklist as the pilot's gate sequence.

### 10.2 The Pilot Record

- ⚠ **The 2025–2026 "AI trader" and "quant copilot" pilots** — carried from the use-case sibling §7.3: the sell-side genAI trading copilots (pre-trade briefs, risk summaries, news triage) are real and spreading, and the default grade for any 2025 "AI trader" story is ⚠ pilot until the firm's own material confirms general availability.

- ✅ **The open-source and academic pilots are the most transparent pilots of all** — Section 3's entire record (TradingAgents, FinMem, Alpha-GPT, FinRobot, StockAgent, the AI4Finance ecosystem, the community "AI hedge fund") is *publicly run piloting*: code released, disclaimers stated, backtests shown, no capital committed. The irony the evidence forces: the systems that claim the least are the systems that show the most.

### 10.3 The Marketing Record

- ⚠ **Autonomous LLM trading agents at scale — vendor marketing.** The claim that LLM agents monitor markets, generate signals and place trades autonomously at a named major institution was verified **nowhere** in this pass, and the pattern is carried from the use-case sibling's identical finding across the finance landscape (§7.2). Every such claim found was a vendor page, a demo video, or press repeating either.
- ⚠ **Agentic SOR and autonomous execution — vendor marketing** (Section 5.3), **agentic alt-data fusion — vendor marketing** (Section 4.3), **autonomous portfolio-construction agents — vendor marketing** (Section 7.2), **scenario-agent and stress-copilot results — vendor marketing** (Section 6.2). The marketing record's tell, from Section 1.3's discipline: no named desk, no named instrument class, no named human-approval control, no published walk-forward — and usually a GitHub star count standing in for a track record.
- ❌ **The conflation claims.** Where marketing or press presents simulation results as live performance, a research prototype as a deployed system, or a memo-drafting copilot as an autonomous trader, the verified record contradicts the claim, and the contradiction is marked rather than repeated (Sections 5.3, 9.5).

### 10.4 The Synthesis — the Autonomy Frontier in Quant

The scoreboard reads consistently across every section of this guide, and it matches the use-case sibling's finance-wide finding (its §2.1 and §12): **autonomy in quant is inverse to proximity to the order.** Far from the order — research, alternative-data reading, risk explanation, portfolio rationale — LLM agents are verified, deployed, and multiplying on the firmwide copilot rails. Near the order — execution, autonomous trading, agentic SOR — the verified systems are pre-LLM, and the LLM claims are pilot or marketing. The reason is not regulatory timidity alone; it is that the near-order stages are numeric, latency-bound and determinism-hungry (Sections 5.2, 7.2), while the far-from-order stages are language-shaped — and language is the one thing the LLM agents verifiably do well. The worked example of Section 11 is the deployment consequence: Cymbal Bank pilots its quant agent **at the research end of the frontier**, where the evidence says the frontier actually is.

## 11. The Cymbal Bank Worked Example — the Quant-Research Agent Pilot

Cymbal Bank (the repository's fictional Singapore-based bank persona, used across the sibling worked examples — always written as two words) runs a small systematic-equities desk within its markets business. This worked example follows the repo conventions — ranked candidates, workflow steps, evaluation, risk, rollout — and applies Sections 1–10 to one decision: whether and how Cymbal Bank pilots an LLM quant-research agent in 2026. All programme parameters below (sizes, counts, thresholds) are the example's own illustrative numbers, presented as the bank's fictional parameters rather than as industry benchmarks — the convention the engineering sibling's worked example follows and flags ([Multi-Agent AI Systems for Banking](./multi_agent_banking_guide.md) §15, cross-referenced).

**The bank context in brief.** Cymbal Bank's persona is fixed across the repository's worked examples: a Singapore-based fictional bank whose AI governance follows the engineering sibling's conventions, and whose markets business is small enough that a pilot is one desk's decision yet large enough that the model-risk and compliance machinery is real and staffed. For this example the setting is the systematic-equities desk of Section 11.1 — three quants, four pre-LLM production systems, and a research bottleneck — and the question is the guide's question, asked at a bank that can actually answer it: which LLM agent, at which maturity rung, at what distance from the order, with what evaluation and what guardrails, earns a pilot? The candidate shortlist of Section 11.2 exists to stop that question from being answered by vendor enthusiasm.

### 11.1 The Scenario

Cymbal Bank's systematic desk runs four pre-LLM production systems — the execution engine (LOXM-class, broker-provided), the portfolio optimiser, the risk engine with hard limits, and the market-data estate ([Market Data Consumption](../../banking/market_data_consumption_guide.md) §10 carries the bank's data-side worked example). The desk's constraint is research throughput: three quants produce the factor ideas, and the idea-to-scaffold-to-backtest loop is the bottleneck. The bank's AI governance committee (the engineering sibling's governed-agent conventions, §1.3) has approved a **pilot** — not a production programme — for one LLM agent on the research arc, with the explicit boundary that the agent touches **no order flow** and holds **no decision rights**. The pilot's question, from Section 10.4: does the verified autonomy frontier hold at Cymbal Bank — is the research-end copilot the honest deployment?

Three properties of the desk make it the right pilot host. Its research output is *documented* — memos, factor definitions, evaluation records — so the agent's language output is testable against a golden record from day one. Its data estate is *point-in-time capable* (the market-data guide's §10 worked example, cross-referenced), so the leakage discipline of Section 4.4 is executable rather than aspirational. And its existing loop is *human-gated* — every factor carries PM sign-off — so the pilot installs the agent inside an approval chain that already exists instead of inventing one. A desk missing any of the three would still be a candidate for an agent; it would just be a candidate for a different, earlier conversation about data and process first.

### 11.2 The Candidate List — Ranked

The desk's shortlist applies Section 2.3's two axes — maturity rung, and distance from the order — to five candidates:

| # | The Candidate | The Rung | Distance from the Order | The Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The alpha-research copilot (idea generation, literature-to-signal translation, memo drafting) | Copilot | Far — output is research memos and code skeletons | **Selected for the pilot** — the verified frontier (Section 10.4) |
| 2 | The risk-explanation copilot (limit and VaR questions over the risk engine) | Copilot | Far — output is answers to risk staff | Second-wave candidate — Section 6.3's verdict |
| 3 | The earnings-call and news event-note agent feeding the factor pipeline | Copilot over deterministic parsing | Medium — its notes feed a human-checked factor step | Pilot-adjacent — depends on candidate 1's eval gates passing |
| 4 | The signal-proposal agent (proposes factor definitions from the research memos) | Agent, bounded | Medium — proposals reach the backtest but never the book | **Rejected for the pilot** — a signal proposal that flows into the pipeline is a model (Section 8.3); model-risk validation comes after the copilot proves itself |
| 5 | The execution-advice copilot (drafts pre-trade schedules next to the LOXM-class engine) | Copilot | Near — output advises the trader | **Rejected for the pilot** — near-order pilots wait for the 2027 cycle and the vendor evidence base (Sections 5.2–5.3) |

The ranking applies the guide's spine: rung and distance-from-order together determine the eval burden (Section 2.3), and only candidate 1 is both low-rung *and* far-from-order — the configuration the verified production record (Section 10.1) supports.

**Why candidate 1 wins the first cycle.** Candidate 2 (the risk-explanation copilot) is the strongest second-wave candidate — Section 6.3's verdict — but it serves the second line, whose evidence contracts are stricter and whose deployment therefore needs more governance scaffolding than a first pilot should carry; the desk's risk function sponsors it for the next cycle rather than for this one. Candidate 3 depends on candidate 1's gates passing: an event-note agent whose notes feed a human-checked factor step is candidate 1's output discipline applied to a different corpus, so it rides the same eval rails later. Candidates 4 and 5 are rejected on the guide's spine itself — candidate 4's signal proposals make it a *model* the moment they enter the pipeline (Section 8.3), and candidate 5 sits next to live order flow, where the vendor evidence base and the desk-level controls do not yet exist (Sections 5.2–5.3). The pilot is deliberately the **lowest-risk configuration that still exercises the whole evaluation**: a copilot, far from the order, whose memos and code skeletons run the full gate sequence of Section 11.4 — which is precisely why passing it proves something worth scaling.

### 11.3 The Workflow — the Research Copilot, Not the Autopilot

The pilot agent's workflow follows the open-source and academic designs of Section 3, reined in by the engineering sibling's governed-agent contracts:

- **The scope contract** — the agent operates on three tasks only: (1) *idea generation* — given a desk research theme, it drafts candidate factor hypotheses with cited academic and market precedents; (2) *literature-to-signal translation* — given a paper (the TradingAgents and Alpha-GPT pattern, Section 3.1/3.3), it drafts the factor's definition, the intuition, and the code skeleton against the desk's backtest library; (3) *memo drafting* — the research memo with hypothesis, proposed evaluation, and risks, for the PM's review. Everything outside the three tasks — anything touching order parameters, position sizing, or instrument choice for live trading — is out of scope by prompt, by tool access, and by the escalation contract.
- **The escalation contract** — every output carries the PM review step; nothing the agent produces enters the factor pipeline without a human PM's sign-off; any request that approaches the boundary (an order, a size, a venue) triggers the hard stop and a logged escalation.
- **The evidence contract** — every claim in the agent's memos cites its source (paper, filing, call transcript); the RAG-and-citation mechanics are the sibling RAG guides' subject ([Production-Grade Agentic RAG](rag/production_grade_agentic_rag_guide.md)), and the point-in-time rule of Section 4.3 applies: the agent's retrieval corpus is time-stamped so a memo written in March 2026 can never cite an April 2026 filing.
- **The model-choice and scaffolding** — the engineering sibling's framework comparison (§4) and the [Agent Scaffolding](./agent_scaffolding_guide.md) guide govern the build; the pilot uses a general-purpose frontier model with the desk's curated corpus and tool access to the backtest library — the FinRobot-platform pattern of Section 3.4, which the desk evaluates as its orchestration base.
- **The memory and the hypothesis log** — the FinMem pattern of Section 3.2 installed as a desk artifact: the agent keeps a running, PM-visible log of every hypothesis it proposed, the source that motivated it, and the gate result that killed or promoted it, so the research committee can see the multiple-testing count (Section 9.1's selection-log discipline) instead of only the survivors.

The workflow's governing sentence: the agent *proposes*, the PM *sponsors*, the gates *judge* — nothing the agent drafts reaches the factor pipeline, the backtest library's live outputs, or the desk's order systems except through a human checkpoint that is itself logged. That sentence is the whole pilot in miniature, and every artifact below exists to make it auditable.

### 11.4 The Evaluation — Paper Trading, Walk-Forward, Eval Gates

The pilot's evaluation runs Section 9's gauntlet in order, with the engineering sibling's eval-gate conventions (§6–7 of that guide) as the process wrapper:

- **Gate 0 — the LLM layer (language quality).** The agent's memos and code skeletons are evaluated on the sibling-eval machinery — golden memo sets, LLM-as-judge with human spot-checks, citation-accuracy and groundedness metrics ([LLM Evaluation Frameworks](./llm_evaluation_frameworks_guide.md) §3–4, cross-referenced). The gate's bar: 95% of memos pass the PM's factual spot-check with no uncited quantitative claim.
- **Gate 1 — the backtest scaffold.** The agent's proposed factors are scaffolded into the desk's backtest library and run over the desk's **three benchmark periods** — the 2022 rate-shock drawdown, the 2023–2024 recovery, and the 2025–2026 regime (illustrative period labels) — never one lucky window. The overfitting discipline of Section 9.1 applies verbatim: the agent's prompts and retrieval settings are frozen before the benchmark runs, and the evaluation windows are untouchable.
- **Gate 2 — walk-forward.** Each candidate factor is optimised-and-frozen on training windows and tested untouched on the following out-of-sample windows, rolling forward (Section 9.2). The desk's bar: the factor's out-of-sample Sharpe and drawdown must be statistically distinguishable from noise across windows — the probability-of-overfitting mindset of the verified literature (arXiv:2209.05559, Section 9.1), not a single lucky year.
- **Gate 3 — the realism layer.** The factor backtests are re-run with the desk's cost model — slippage, market impact, and TCA benchmarks (Section 9.3) — and with a survivorship-bias-free universe (the FinRL-Meta warning, Section 9.3). A factor whose edge dies under costs is dead.
- **Gate 4 — paper trading.** The two surviving factors run in the desk's paper-trading environment for one quarter against live data (Section 9.2), monitored by the risk overlay of Section 11.5. Only factors that survive paper trading enter the PM's live-approval queue — and even then as *human-approved factor candidates* for the desk's normal research-committee process, not as autonomous trades.

The gate sequence is the two-layer composition of Section 9.4: language quality first (Gate 0), quant behaviour last (Gates 1–4), with the quant layer as the final word.

**The evaluation plan on one page.** The pilot runs the first benchmark-period backtests (Gates 1–3) in month one, the walk-forward schedule across the remaining benchmark windows in month two, and the paper-trading quarter (Gate 4) in months three to five — with the Gate-0 language metrics re-run on every memo version and the drift-and-regression monitors of Section 9.6 watching both the retrieval corpora and the distribution of proposed factors from the first day. The pilot's success criterion is deliberately not "the agent found alpha": it is the Section 10.4 proposition, measured — the research-end copilot measurably raises the desk's idea-to-scaffold throughput *without* degrading factor survival through the gates, at a run cost the desk's P&L can see and a false-discovery rate the committee can count. Alpha may or may not follow; the pilot's job is to produce the measured record that makes the next decision honest either way.

### 11.5 The Risk Overlay — Limits, Kill Switch, Model-Risk Register, Compliance

- **The limits** — the pilot is bounded by hard, pre-existing desk limits that the agent cannot move: the paper-trading account has a notional cap (illustratively SGD 5 million), the factor pipeline accepts no agent output without PM sign-off, and the agent's tool access is deny-by-default — read-only on the research corpus and the backtest library, no trading-system credentials at all. The limits are enforced by the platform, not by policy (the engineering sibling's guardrail-enforcement points, §5.5).
- **The kill switch** — the pre-built, tested capability to take the agent out of the workflow immediately (the engineering sibling's kill-switch mechanism, §9.3): any prompt-injection attempt, any out-of-scope tool call, or any boundary request triggers an automatic halt and a logged incident — the failure catalogue's lessons ([LLM Agents Failures in Production](./llm_agents_failures_production_guide.md) §1–2, cross-referenced) applied before, not after, the incident.
- **The model-risk register** — candidate 1 (the research copilot) is registered as a **model** in Cymbal Bank's model-risk system from day one, because its outputs feed the investment process (Section 8.3's framing): a register entry, a named owner, a validation record, and a quarterly review. The register entry is what makes the pilot's boundary auditable — the moment a future candidate (candidate 4 in Section 11.2) crosses from memo to signal pipeline, its model-risk class changes and its validation burden rises with it.
- **The compliance review** — the pilot passes the Section 8 overlay: the desk's sanctions and AML rails screen the paper-trading counterparties and instruments exactly as they screen live ones (Section 8.1, the Fircosoft estate cross-referenced); the agent's outputs and tool calls feed the communications-and-trade surveillance systems as machine-generated artifacts (Section 8.2); and the audit trail — every prompt, retrieval, memo version and gate result — is immutable and examiner-reconstructable (the engineering sibling's audit-trail standard, §11.3).

**The risk overlay's net effect** is that the pilot's worst case is a *research memo that never reaches a book*: the limits make the agent's footprint small, the kill switch makes it removable in seconds, the model-risk register makes it governable by a named owner with a validation cadence, and the compliance review makes every step reconstructable by an examiner. That is the correct worst case for a first LLM-agent deployment inside a bank's markets business, and it is the direct consequence of Sections 8 and 10.4's findings — the pilot earns its modest autonomy by making its failure modes modest first.

### 11.6 The Rollout Gates — Canary, Champion-Challenger

The pilot follows the engineering sibling's phased-rollout machinery (§9 of that guide), scaled to a three-quant desk:

- **Phase 0 — the bench trial (weeks 1–4).** Two quants use the agent on historical research tasks with known desk answers; the Gate-0 language metrics and the escalation-log quality are measured against the bars. The exit criterion: the agent's memos pass the factual spot-check and the escalation log shows zero boundary attempts.
- **Phase 1 — the canary (weeks 5–12).** One quant, one research theme, live: the agent is in the daily workflow of a single PM-approved mandate while the other two quants work unchanged. The exit criterion: Gate-1-to-4 results on the first two factors, on schedule and within the cost model.
- **Phase 2 — champion-challenger (quarter 2).** The agent-assisted research loop runs **against** the desk's incumbent manual loop on matched themes — the champion-challenger comparison of the engineering sibling (§9.3) applied to research throughput: idea-to-scaffold cycle time, memo quality scores, and the downstream factor survival rate through Gates 1–4. The promotion criterion is measured, not felt: the agent-assisted loop must beat the manual loop on cycle time *without* degrading factor survival — and even then, the promotion is to *desk tooling*, not to autonomy.
- **Phase 3 — the go/no-go.** The committee reviews the champion-challenger record, the model-risk register, and the incident log. The honest go/no-go options include **no-go**: if the agent's factor survival rate is indistinguishable from the manual loop's, the pilot's verdict is that the desk does not yet need the agent, and the discipline (Section 9) has done its job.

The rollout is scaled to the risk, not to the ambition: each phase's exit criteria are written down before the phase starts — the pre-registration discipline of Section 9.1 applied to the pilot itself — so the committee's go/no-go reads measured records rather than impressions, and a phase that fails its criteria stops the sequence where it is instead of being waved through on momentum. A pilot that cannot say no to itself is not a pilot; it is a purchase order with a schedule.

### 11.7 The Lessons

The worked example's lessons are the guide's theses in executable form: the pilot lives at the research end of the autonomy frontier because that is where the verified record says LLM agents work (Section 10.4); its evaluation runs the quant gauntlet — benchmark periods, walk-forward, costs, paper trading — because that is the only evaluation that means anything near a book (Section 9); its risk overlay treats the agent as a model with limits, a kill switch and a register entry because that is what the compliance reality requires (Section 8.3); and its rollout is canary-and-champion-challenger because the engineering sibling's gates are the machinery that separates a pilot from a claim (Section 10.3's "tell"). Cymbal Bank's pilot is deliberately unglamorous — and that is the point: every verified deployment in Sections 3–10 earned its place by being exactly this boring.

## 13. The Glossary — the Quant-Agent Terms

The guide's vocabulary in one table — the quant-evaluation terms of Section 9, the agent-maturity terms of Section 2.3, and the governance terms of Sections 6–8 — each with the section where the term does its work. The definitions are presented as the discipline's standard vocabulary, not as novel claims (Claims Audit row 29).

| The Term | The Meaning |
|---|---|
| The quant workflow | The six-stage pipeline — alpha research, signal generation, execution, risk, portfolio construction, evaluation — that this guide maps LLM agents onto (Section 2.1) |
| The alpha | The risk-adjusted excess return a strategy seeks; the "signal" that is tradable |
| The signal | The measurable predictor (factor, event, pattern) a strategy trades on; the LLM agent's role is often *engineering* signals, not being one |
| The backtest | The replay of a strategy or agent's decisions over historical data to estimate performance — the quant's first evaluation gate (Section 9.1) |
| The backtest overfitting | The trap of tuning a strategy (or an agent's prompts and parameters) so hard to the historical sample that the apparent edge is noise; documented in the verified literature (arXiv:2209.05559) |
| The walk-forward analysis | The rolling discipline where the strategy is optimised on each training window and tested untouched on the following out-of-sample window (Section 9.2) |
| The out-of-sample window | The data a strategy or agent has never been tuned against — the only honest test |
| The benchmark period | A named market regime used for evaluation; credible claims span several, not one lucky year |
| The paper trading | Running the agent's decisions in real time against live data with no capital at risk (Section 9.2) |
| The slippage | The difference between the expected and actual fill price of an order (Section 9.3) |
| The market impact | The cost of the order moving the market against itself — grows with order size relative to liquidity |
| The TCA | Transaction-cost analysis — the post-trade measurement of execution quality against arrival-price, VWAP or implementation-shortfall benchmarks |
| The survivorship bias | The distortion from backtesting only today's surviving tickers; an LLM agent selecting its universe from memory is prone to it by construction |
| The Sharpe ratio | The return-per-unit-of-risk measure (return over the risk-free rate divided by volatility) used throughout the Section 3 papers' evaluations |
| The maximum drawdown | The largest peak-to-trough decline in the equity curve — the risk measure the trading-agent papers report alongside Sharpe |
| The SOR | Smart order routing — the execution logic that chooses venues and order types; "agentic SOR" claims are vendor marketing until a named institution publishes (Section 5.3) |
| The HITL | Human-in-the-loop — the checkpoints where a human approves or overrides; the verified shape of every quant-agent deployment (Section 2.2) |
| The copilot | The maturity rung where the LLM drafts, proposes and explains under human approval (use-case sibling §2.3, cross-referenced) |
| The governed agent | The agent bounded by the scope, escalation, evidence and evaluation contracts — the engineering sibling's central principle, applied to quant agents here |
| The model-risk register | The bank's inventory of models, each with an owner, a validation record and a review cycle; an LLM agent touching trading or risk lives in it (Section 8.3) |
| The kill switch | The pre-built, tested mechanism to take the agent out of the path immediately (engineering sibling §9.3) |
| The canary | The phased-rollout step where the new system serves a small, controlled subset first |
| The champion-challenger | The live comparison of the incumbent (champion) against the candidate (challenger) before promotion |
| The eval gate | The enforceable checkpoint between an agent version and the next stage — the two-layer composition of Section 9.4 |
| The leakage | The agent seeing data it could not have had at decision time — by pretraining (it has read the history) or by pipeline error (point-in-time failure); StockAgent's authors engineered against it (Section 3.4) |
| The pretraining leakage | The specific leakage channel where the model has already read the backtest window during pretraining — the reason evaluation windows must be frozen before they open (Sections 3.4, 9.1) |
| The alpha expression | The coded, testable form of a signal — the artifact Alpha-GPT's LLM proposes and the researcher's backtest library judges (Section 3.3) |
| The benchmark (as reference) | The portfolio or index the strategy is measured against — not to be confused with the benchmark periods of Section 9.2, the named regimes an evaluation must span |
| The escalation contract | The governed-agent clause that routes every out-of-scope or boundary request to a human with a logged record — the quant version routes orders, sizes and venues to the hard stop (Section 11.3) |
| The evidence contract | The governed-agent clause that makes every agent claim cite its source — paper, filing, transcript, data lineage — so the audit trail survives an examiner (Sections 8.2, 11.3) |
| The three lines | The governance map — first line (the business), second line (risk and compliance), third line (audit) — that assigns every risk agent a named owner and an evidence standard (Section 6.3) |

## 14. The Closing — the Quant's Agent

The quantitative workflow is the strictest evaluation culture finance has built: a strategy earns its capital the hard way — backtest, walk-forward, paper trading, costs, regimes, and then the live drawdown that respects none of it. The LLM agents of this guide have entered that culture from the top: the verifiable systems are academic and open-source, honest about their disclaimers and their simulations; the verified deployments sit on the firmwide copilot rails at the research end of the workflow, drafting, summarising and explaining while the human and the deterministic engines hold every decision right; and the marketing claims that would place an autonomous language model at the order book collapse under the same scrutiny the quant applies to a backtest — no named desk, no named costs, no walk-forward, no capital. The pattern across every verified record is the same: the closer the work gets to the order, the more the decision belongs to the deterministic engines and the accountable human; the farther it sits, the more the language model earns its place. The research that finds the signal, the explanation that defends it, the risk narrative that disciplines it, the evaluation that proves it — that is where the LLM agent verifiably lives, and that is the honest frontier of the quant's agent.
