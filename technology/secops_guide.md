## 1. The Overview

### 1.1 The Short Answer

**SecOps — security operations — is the discipline of running the defence as a continuous operation: monitoring the estate, detecting the adversary's activity, deciding what is real, and responding fast enough to matter, 24-7, measured and improved like any other production operation.** It is the *operational* realisation of the security program — the difference between having security controls (a firewall, an EDR agent, a policy document) and *operating* security (someone watching the alerts at 3 a.m., a playbook that contains a phishing campaign in minutes, a metric that proves dwell time is shrinking). The discipline's centre of gravity is the **SOC — the security operations centre** (the definition is verified in [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.1: the dedicated function where the enterprise's information systems are **monitored, assessed and defended** — people, processes and technology under a governance and compliance framework). SecOps is the *way of running* that function: the tiers, the shifts, the tools, the playbooks, the intelligence, the metrics and the vendors that make the SOC actually work.

**The one-sentence operating thesis:** the SOC exists to compress **dwell time** — the days an attacker is present before detection (the M-Trends definition, ✅ via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.4) — and everything in this guide — the tiers (§2), the detection stack (§3), the response stack (§4), the managed services (§5), the intelligence (§6), the vulnerability loop (§7) and the metrics (§8) — is a machine for that one purpose, operated under the regulatory reality of a bank (§9). The worked example (§10) assembles the whole machine for a Cymbal Bank.

### 1.2 The Definition — the Security-Operations Discipline

- **The term and its family (⚠-structural for the exact coinage — see the ledger).** "SecOps" is the industry's compression of *security operations* — the family that Gartner's analyst vocabulary groups as **security operations** (the SOC, the SIEM/SOAR/XDR tooling, threat detection and response, vulnerability management, and the metrics around them). Unlike "DevSecOps", which has a traceable cultural origin (the DevOps-meets-security movement of the 2010s — ✅ via [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6), "SecOps" as a word has no single canonical coinage record ⚠; it is uniformly used to name the *operating* side of security — what runs after the design-time work ships. The architect's reading: **SecOps is the runtime; security-by-design is the build-time; the SOC is the organisation; DevSecOps is the culture that joins build-time and runtime.** The four are one program viewed from four angles — and this guide's job is the runtime angle.
- **The discipline (✅-structural — the parts are each verified below or in the siblings).** Security operations as a discipline has five permanent workstreams: **(1) detection** — the collection, correlation and alerting machinery of §3 (SIEM, EDR/XDR, NDR); **(2) response** — the triage, containment, eradication and recovery machinery of §4 (SOAR playbooks, case management, the NIST SP 800-61 lifecycle); **(3) intelligence** — the external context that makes detection targeted, §6 (CTI feeds, TIPs); **(4) vulnerability management** — the discovery-and-remediation loop that removes the *initial-access* openings, §7 (scanning, CVSS/EPSS/KEV prioritization, patching); and **(5) the operating model itself** — the people, shifts, tiers and metrics that run all of the above, §2 and §8.
- **The distinction from incident response.** IR is the *episodic* discipline — the lifecycle run when an incident is declared (NIST SP 800-61, ✅ via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.5). SecOps is the *standing* discipline — the operation that runs whether or not an incident is active, so that when one is declared, the IR lifecycle has telemetry to analyse, playbooks to run and a team that is already warm. **IR is a phase of SecOps, not a separate function** — the same analysts, the same tools, the same case file, escalated in formality and authority.
- **The discipline in the bank (⚠-structural framing; every element cross-ref'd to verified cluster material).** In a Cymbal Bank, SecOps carries weights most industries do not: the **regulator-facing incident timeline** (detection timestamp, containment timestamp, notification timestamp — the evidence chain DORA and the MAS TRM demand, cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1 and §8.4), the **crown-jewel estates** (payment rails, client-confidential data, market positions, privileged identities, the key estate — the §9.1 list of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md)), the **legacy-core telemetry gap** (AS/400-era platforms whose logs are the hardest onboarding project — cross-ref [vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md](vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md) and [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) §2), and the **24-7 reality** of a global payments operation (the follow-the-sun mechanics of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.2).

### 1.3 The SecOps-vs-DevSecOps-vs-SOC Distinction

The three terms are the most-confused triple in the security vocabulary, and the distinction is the foundation of this guide:

| Term | What it is | The time axis | The owner | Cross-ref |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SecOps** | The **operating** discipline — detect, respond, remediate, measure, continuously | The **runtime** — after the system ships, forever | The SOC and the security-operations function | This guide, §1–§8 |
| **DevSecOps** | The **cultural and pipeline** practice — security as code, gates in CI/CD, shared responsibility between dev and security | The **build-time** — shift-left, before and during delivery | The delivery teams with embedded security engineers | [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §6 |
| **SOC** | The **organisation** — the people, process and technology unit that executes the detection-and-response mission | **Standing** — the operating model of §2 | The CISO's second-line function | [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.1; this guide §2 |

**The three in one sentence (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** DevSecOps makes the system *safe to ship*; the SOC is the *organisation that keeps it safe at runtime*; SecOps is the *discipline of running that organisation well* — and a mature bank runs all three as one loop, with the DevSecOps pipeline feeding the SOC's asset inventory and the SOC's findings feeding the DevSecOps remediation backlog (the purple loop of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.6 is the same idea at the engagement level).

### 1.6 The SecOps Canon — Where the Discipline Comes From

The security-operations discipline has a traceable history, and each era's layer is still running in a modern SOC — the same "every decade adds a layer" pattern the discipline map identifies for cybersecurity as a whole ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §1.5):

- **The 1970s–80s — the watch floor and the reactive function (⚠-structural via the sibling's timeline; the physical-centre lineage ✅-historical).** The first "operations centres" were physical watch floors (the military/SIGINT heritage — the Wikipedia SOC record's photographs of the NSA's National Security Operations Center in 1975 document the physical-centre lineage ✅-historical), and the Morris worm with the CERT Coordination Center (1988, ⚠-structural via the sibling's timeline) institutionalised the *reactive* function — the ancestor of the SOC. The network operations centre (NOC) culture of the 1990s gave the SOC its shift-and-watch operating shape ⚠-structural.
- **The 2000s — the detection-engine era (✅ via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.2).** The Gartner SIEM coinage (2005, Nicolett & Williams) turned log collection into real-time correlation — the SOC's detection spine — and the compliance drivers (PCI-DSS-class logging) gave the SIEM its retention mandate.
- **The 2010s — the endpoint and the automation era (✅/⚠ as flagged).** The EDR class emerged as the endpoint-detection layer (CrowdStrike's Falcon shipped June 2013 ✅; SentinelOne founded 2013 ✅), ATT&CK (2013, ✅ via [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3) gave the discipline its adversary language, and the SOAR category (Gartner 2017 ⚠-structural via the sibling) automated the response half.
- **The late-2010s/2020s — the platform-consolidation era (✅ as verified).** The NTA→NDR wave (around 2019–2020, ✅ the Wikipedia NDR record) added the network behavioural plane; the XDR consolidation made the vendor platform the correlation plane (Microsoft Defender XDR ✅ live); the MDR market went mainstream (the Gartner 50%-by-2025 prediction ⚠-secondary); the intel-and-prioritization standards matured — **EPSS (the FIRST SIG's ML exploitation-likelihood score ✅), CVSS v4.0 (initial release November 2023 ✅), the CISA KEV catalog with its due-date discipline ✅** — and the instrument landscape migrated (the MAS Notice-655 → FSM-N05 transition ✅ via the sibling).
- **The 2026 direction — the agentic SOC (⚠-secondary, consistently visible across the verified vendor pages).** Every platform this guide verified live is marketing the same direction — the AI-native SIEM, the agentic SOAR, the autonomous-SOC thesis (CrowdStrike's "Agentic SOAR" and "Next-Gen SIEM", Splunk ES's "agentic AI", SentinelOne's "autonomous security operations centers" — all ⚠-secondary vendor language). The architectural reading: the automation layer of §4.1 is getting a reasoning layer, but the §2 operating model and the §8 metrics survive the change — the agentic SOC still has tiers, shifts, playbooks and a scoreboard; the agents just do more of the T1/T2 work. The discipline's canon is the same as the support world's: **every layer gets added, none gets retired.**

## 2. The SOC Operating Model

### 2.1 The Tier-1/2/3 Model — Triage, Investigation, Hunting

- **The model (⚠-structural — flagged honestly, with the sibling's backing).** The SOC's analyst workforce is organised in the **tiered model**: **Tier 1 — triage** (the frontline that reviews the alert queue, classifies events, dismisses the false positives, and escalates the real ones), **Tier 2 — investigation** (the analysts who deep-dive the escalated alerts: hunt the related events, contain the confirmed incident, coordinate the fix), **Tier 3 — hunting and forensics** (the senior specialists who proactively search for the adversary the rules missed — the threat-hunting layer — and perform the deep forensics and the detection-content engineering). This is the same escalating-expertise logic as the L1/L2/L3 support model — [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.1 — applied to the security mission, and the sibling's §3.1 documents the same shape ("Tier 1 triage, Tier 2 investigation, Tier 3 hunting/forensics") ⚠-structural there too. Verification note: the vendor explainer pages (CrowdStrike, SentinelOne, IBM, Exabeam, Cisco) all 404'd or redirected this pass and the search backend returned empty throughout, so **no single primary source was extracted live for the exact tier definitions** — the model is uniformly described across the industry record and consistent with the sibling's ledger, hence ⚠-structural rather than ✅.
- **The tier economics (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of uniform practice).** The tiers exist for the same reason the support tiers do: **routine work is handled by the cheapest capable resource, and hard problems reach specialists without the frontline guessing.** Tier 1 resolves or dismisses the bulk of the alert volume (the "first-contact resolution" benchmark of the support world — 60–80% at L1 ⚠-structural per [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.1 — has its SOC analogue in the *triage-closure rate*); Tier 2 does the real diagnosis; Tier 3 does the work that cannot be scheduled. The two operating rules transfer directly from the support model: **escalation is by defined criteria, not by frustration** (the T1 analyst escalates when the playbook is exhausted or the severity threshold is crossed — never because the queue is scary), and **knowledge flows downward** (every T3 hunting finding that can be codified becomes a T2 investigation checklist, every T2 investigation that can be scripted becomes a T1 playbook or a SIEM rule — the toil-reduction loop of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §6.2 applied to detection content).
- **The tier anatomy (⚠-structural — the standard practice shape; the playbook examples are this guide's synthesis):**
  - **Tier 1 — the triage analyst.** Watches the alert queue (the SIEM's correlation output of §3.1, the EDR's detections of §3.2, the intel matches of §6), validates each alert against the playbook, dismisses the known-benign, opens the case for the real ones, and executes the *automated first response* the SOAR already started (§4.1). The T1 success metric is *accuracy under volume* — the false-positive dismissal rate and the escalation quality, not the alert count handled.
  - **Tier 2 — the investigator.** Owns the escalated case end-to-end: hunts the related events across the data lake, determines scope (what systems, what data, what impact — the Detection and Analysis phase of NIST SP 800-61, cross-ref [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.5), executes the containment actions the playbook requires (host isolation, credential reset, egress blocking), and hands the evidence chain to the IR coordinator when the incident is declared. The T2 success metric is *time-to-contained* — the §8.1 MTTR in action.
  - **Tier 3 — the hunter / detection engineer.** The proactive layer: formulates hunting hypotheses from the threat model and the intel feed ("who in our estate could be running ATT&CK T1078 valid-accounts right now?" — the ATT&CK language of [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3 and [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §6.5), runs the hunts, performs the deep forensics on the contained hosts, and — the force-multiplier role — **writes the new detection content** that turns every confirmed finding into a permanent rule (the use-case engineering of §3.1). The T3 success metric is the *detection-gap closure rate* and the dwell-time trend.
- **The IR coordinator and the shift leads (⚠-structural).** Two roles complete the model: the **shift lead** (the senior analyst who runs the shift, owns the escalation matrix of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.4, and is the first rung of hierarchical escalation) and the **IR coordinator / incident commander** (the role that takes over when an incident is declared and runs the §4.3 lifecycle — the bridge, the timeline, the regulator notification clock). In a small SOC the shift lead and the IR coordinator are the same person; in a bank they are separated because the IR commander's timeline is a supervisory artefact (§9.2).
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** the tiered model's failure modes are the bank's regulatory risks. **T1 as a dismissal machine** (alerts closed without investigation — the "triage wall") produces the *missed incident* that the regulator finds first; **T2 as a pass-through** (cases escalated to T3 without investigation) burns the expensive tier and delays containment; **T3 burnout** (every hard case becomes T3 work because T2 never learned the estate) is the staffing crisis that the MDR augmentation of §5 exists to absorb. The controls are the same as the support world's: defined escalation criteria, knowledge-flow discipline, and the metrics of §8 that make the failure visible.

### 2.2 The Shift Patterns — the 24-7 Coverage Models

The SOC's mission is time-critical by construction — the attacker does not observe business hours, and the dwell-time metric (§8.1) runs continuously — so the operating model must produce **24-7 coverage**. The four standard patterns (✅-structural via [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4, which verifies the mechanics and the SRE-book facts):

- **Pattern 1 — the on-site shift rotation (⚠-structural).** Analysts work fixed shifts covering the 24 hours (the classic 4-on-4-off or the 3-shift rotation). Strengths: continuous hands-on coverage with the full toolset. Costs: the night-shift health and retention problem (the SRE book's finding that night shifts are "detrimental to people's health" — ✅ via [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.2), and the staffing math — a single-site 24/7 rotation with two people per shift needs **a minimum of eight analysts** on the SRE model (✅ ch. 11 facts cross-ref'd from the sibling), which is why small SOCs do not run this pattern alone.
- **Pattern 2 — follow-the-sun (✅ via the SRE book, cross-ref [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.2).** Multi-site handoff of the open work with daylight: Singapore hands the queue to Paris/London; London hands it to New York; New York hands it back — 24-hour coverage with day-shift-quality staff at every hour and nobody working nights. The operating requirements: **at least two sites in distant time zones** (the classic Cymbal Bank pairing — the APAC hub and the European home, cross-ref [../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md](../banking/credit_agricole_software_systems_guide.md) §10), a **documented handover** (the open-incident state, the pending changes, the overnight alerts and known noise, the vendor tickets, the risk register of the day — the handover checklist the sibling spells out), **shared tooling** (the case file travels with the sun), and **clear ownership of the handoff moment** (the 30-minute overlap where incidents get dropped). The trade-off: multi-site teams "incur communication and coordination overhead" (✅) — justified when the watched systems are important enough, which for a bank's payment rails they are.
- **Pattern 3 — on-call escalation (✅ via the SRE book, cross-ref [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.3).** The named analysts carry the pager for nights and weekends, with response targets in minutes for the critical services ("5 minutes for user-facing or otherwise highly time-critical services, 30 minutes for less time-sensitive systems" — ✅), a **primary and secondary rotation** (✅), and the sustainability caps — **no more than 25% of time on-call** and at least 50% of engineering time on non-operational work (✅). The SOC variant: on-call covers the *escalation* rung of the overnight queue — T1 triage may be staffed or automated, but the T2/T3 brain is on the pager.
- **Pattern 4 — the managed augmentation (⚠-structural, the economics in §5).** The MDR/MSSP provider (§5) carries the overnight or the overflow queue — the "follow-the-sun via a vendor" pattern that the sibling's decision map names ("MSSP augmentation is the standard compromise", [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.7). The bank shape: in-house core days + managed nights/weekends, with the vendor triaging and escalating to the in-house on-call for the decisions only the bank can make (the regulator-facing ones).
- **The 24-7 decision map (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the pattern chosen is a function of three variables — **the estate's criticality** (a global bank's payment rails justify follow-the-sun; an internal tool does not), **the analyst headcount** (the §2.1 tier economics vs the 8-analyst minimum for a self-run rotation), and **the regulatory evidence burden** (the incident timeline must be owned in-house even when the monitoring is outsourced — §9.2). The maturity path is the sibling's: MSSP-only → hybrid → in-house-plus ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.7).

### 2.3 The SOC Maturity — the Ladder and the Frameworks

- **The ladder (⚠-structural — flagged; consistent with the sibling's §3.1 reading, no single framework source extracted live this pass).** SOC maturity is commonly described in five levels, and the direction matters more than the labels: **Level 1 — reactive** (ad-hoc alerting, no defined triage), **Level 2 — standardised** (defined processes, documented playbooks), **Level 3 — measured** (metrics trended — MTTA/MTTR of §8.1 reviewed), **Level 4 — proactive** (scheduled hunting and purple engagements — the §3.4/§3.6 machinery of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md)), **Level 5 — predictive** (intel-led, automation-assisted decisioning — the agentic-SOC direction the vendors are marketing in 2026, ⚠-secondary). The named maturity frameworks (the SOC-CMM family ⚠) differ in labels, not in direction; the sibling's §3.1 reading is this guide's: **maturity = the shift from reactive alert consumption to proactive, metric-driven operation.**
- **The maturity table as a design tool (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis).** The ladder is best used as a *gap map*, not a badge: each level names the next capability to build — L2's playbooks, L3's metrics, L4's hunting calendar, L5's automation — and the §10 worked example sequences exactly that build. The regulator's version of the ladder is the *evidence* version: an MAS or DORA examination samples the artefacts (the playbooks, the metrics trend, the incident timeline, the tabletop records — §9.3), and the maturity level is read off what exists, not what is claimed.
- **The SANS survey material (⚠ — flagged per the task framing).** The SANS SOC-survey series is the standard practitioner benchmark for SOC structure (tiers, tooling, staffing ratios, alert volumes), but sans.org blocked extraction in the sibling's pass ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.4's ledger) and remained unavailable this pass (the search backend returned empty). **No SANS figures are quoted in this guide** — the honest flag is recorded in the ledger (§13).

## 3. The Detection Stack

### 3.1 The SIEM Correlation — the Use-Case Engineering

- **The verified foundation (✅ via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.2 — cross-ref, not re-derived).** The SIEM — security information and event management — is the detection engine: SIM (long-term log collection and reporting) + SEM (real-time alerting), the term coined by Gartner's Mark Nicolett and Amrit Williams in 2005 ✅; the pipeline is collect → normalise → correlate → alert → retain; the SIEMs are central to SOCs ✅. This guide deepens the *correlation* step, because that is where the SOC's detection quality is actually made.
- **The use-case engineering discipline (⚠-structural — the practice is universal detection-engineering; the framing is this guide's synthesis).** A **use case** is the full chain from a *named threat* to a *working alert*: **threat** (an ATT&CK technique or an intel-flagged campaign — the adversary language of [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3) → **data source** (which log feeds can see it — the Windows security event, the EDR telemetry, the cloud audit trail, the identity logs) → **correlation logic** (the rule: this event + that condition, or this sequence over a time window) → **alert** (the actionable output with the context the T1 analyst needs) → **playbook** (§4.1 — the response the alert triggers) → **measurement** (§8 — the false-positive rate that proves the use case earns its noise). The discipline's two rules: **one use case per named threat** (a rule that fires for "suspicious stuff" fires for everything and means nothing), and **every use case has an owner and a review date** (detection content decays as the estate changes — the same review discipline as the threat register of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §2.8).
- **The correlation techniques (⚠-structural — standard detection-engineering practice):** **signature correlation** (the known-bad match — IoC-based, the tactical-intel layer of §6.1), **behavioural correlation** (the deviation from baseline — the UEBA family: a service account logging in at 3 a.m., a workstation beaconing out), **sequence/timeline correlation** (the ATT&CK-chain pattern — initial access + execution + persistence in a window is worth more than any single event), and **context enrichment** (the alert is only as good as the asset data attached — the CMDB/blast-radius query of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §5.1: "which business service does this host sit behind?"). The bank's crown jewels (§9.1 of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md)) get the richest use cases; the internal tools get the hygiene ones.
- **The Sigma angle (✅ — verified live this pass, github.com/SigmaHQ/sigma).** **Sigma** is the open, generic signature format for SIEM/EDR detection rules — "the main Sigma rule repository" (10.9k stars, ~16.9k commits, the Detection Rule License 1.1), with a conversion ecosystem (SOC Prime translates the community rules to 25+ SIEM/EDR/XDR platforms; Security Onion and Sekoia ship Sigma natively). Its operational meaning for the SOC: **detection content is portable and shareable** — the bank's detection engineers write or import Sigma rules, convert them to the house SIEM's query language, and contribute the estate-specific rules back to the community (or keep them internal). The purple-team loop ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.6) and the Sigma backlog are the same content pipeline: a detection gap found in an engagement becomes a Sigma rule.
- **The alert-flood control (⚠-structural — the sibling names the failure, §3.2's "alert flood"; this guide owns the control).** The correlation layer's default output is noise, and the control is the **tuning loop**: every use case is measured on its false-positive rate (§8.3), every confirmed-benign alert is a tuning signal (threshold up, condition narrowed, data source corrected), and the *detection backlog* — the queue of use cases to write, tune and retire — is managed like a product backlog with the T3 analysts as the product owners. The SIEM programme's real work in a bank is the **log-source onboarding** project (the sibling's §3.2 bank reading: the legacy core lacks modern telemetry — cross-ref [vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md](vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md) for the scanning analogue); a use case over a log source that is not onboarded is a use case that does not exist.

### 3.2 The EDR/XDR — CrowdStrike Falcon, SentinelOne, Microsoft Defender XDR

The endpoint plane is the SOC's richest telemetry source (the sibling's §7.3 names it: "EDR on every endpoint — the SOC's richest telemetry source"), and the market's centre is the **EDR → XDR** consolidation: **EDR (endpoint detection and response)** watches the endpoint; **XDR (extended detection and response)** correlates the endpoint plane with identity, email, network and cloud signals at the vendor platform (the sibling's §3.2 framing: "XDR is the vendor-ecosystem correlation plane; a mature estate runs both with the XDR alerts feeding the SIEM's case management"). The three platforms the market names most (verified this pass):

- **CrowdStrike Falcon (✅ platform facts via Wikipedia and the vendor site; the marketing claims ⚠-secondary).** CrowdStrike — founded **2011** (George Kurtz, Dmitri Alperovitch, Gregg Marston), HQ Austin, the 2024 faulty-update outage in the record — launched **Falcon in June 2013** as its first product; the company's business is **endpoint security, threat intelligence and cyberattack-response services** ✅. The current platform positioning (vendor pages, ⚠-secondary for the superlatives): "the leader in EPP and EDR" with **threat intelligence and hunting** (the Falcon OverWatch MDR-style service), **Next-Gen SIEM** ("the world's only AI-native SOC platform" — vendor claim), **Agentic SOAR** (the Charlotte AI agentic automation), and **Falcon Complete** as the "elite 24/7 managed detection and response (MDR)" service. The architect's reading: CrowdStrike's differentiation is the **intelligence-to-endpoint loop** — the same telemetry that powers its threat-intel products feeds the detection content, which is why its MDR arm is a first-class offering (§5).
- **SentinelOne (✅ platform facts via Wikipedia).** SentinelOne — founded **2013** (Tomer Weingarten, Almog Cohen, Ehud Shamir), HQ Mountain View, NYSE: S — is built on **machine-learning/behavioural-AI detection** (the patented behavioural-AI model, AV-TEST certified ✅). The platform is **Singularity** (the name surfaces in the verified record), extended by acquisition into the full XDR breadth: **Scalyr** (2021, the data/observability engine), **Attivo Networks** (2022, identity detection and response), **PingSafe** (2024, cloud security), **Prompt Security** (2025, GenAI/agent security), and **Observo AI** (2025, the AI-native telemetry pipeline "before it reaches a SIEM or data lake", positioned as accelerating "the vision of autonomous security operations centers"). The architect's reading: SentinelOne's differentiation is the **AI-native detection core** and the visible 2025 push toward the AI-SIEM/autonomous-SOC thesis — the same agentic direction CrowdStrike and Splunk market, from the endpoint side.
- **Microsoft Defender XDR (✅ verified live this pass — learn.microsoft.com).** Microsoft Defender XDR is **"a unified pre- and post-breach enterprise defense suite that natively coordinates detection, prevention, investigation, and response across endpoints, identities, email, and applications"** ✅. It correlates the signals of the Microsoft security product family — **Defender for Endpoint, Defender for Office 365, Defender for Identity, Defender for Cloud Apps, Defender Vulnerability Management, Defender for Cloud, Entra ID Protection, DLP, App Governance, Purview Insider Risk Management, and Security Exposure Management** — into a combined incidents queue, with **automatic attack disruption** (correlates high-confidence signals and "automatically applies containment actions to stop in-progress attacks and limit lateral movement" ✅), **self-healing** (automated remediation of endpoints, identities and mailboxes), and **cross-product threat hunting** (query-based access to 30 days of raw signals ✅). The architect's reading: Microsoft's differentiation is **native breadth** — the same vendor already owns the identity plane (Entra) and the email plane (M365), so the XDR correlation is built-in rather than integrated, which makes it the natural choice for estates that are already Microsoft-homogeneous (a common bank shape) — and its Vulnerability Management module feeds directly into the §7 workflow.
- **The selection logic (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis — never vendor claims):** the EDR/XDR choice is an *estate* decision, not a benchmark decision: **CrowdStrike** when the threat-intel-to-endpoint loop and the MDR arm are the priority; **SentinelOne** when the autonomous/AI-native detection thesis and the multi-vector Singularity breadth fit; **Defender XDR** when the Microsoft identity/email/endpoint estate makes native correlation cheaper than integration. The non-negotiable for the SOC: whichever platform, its **alerts must flow into the SIEM's case management as first-class events** (§4.2) — an XDR that traps its alerts in its own console is a detection island, and the §8 coverage metrics cannot see it.

### 3.3 The NDR — the Network Plane

- **The category (✅ verified live this pass — the Wikipedia NDR article; the notability flag on that article noted).** **Network detection and response (NDR)** is "a category of network security products that detect abnormal system behaviors by continuously analyzing network traffic", applying **behavioral analytics to raw network packets and metadata for both internal (east-west) and external (north-south) communications** ✅. It detects and contains post-breach activity — **ransomware, lateral movement, data exfiltration** — by spotting anomalies rather than signatures, and it is explicitly positioned as **complementing EDR's blind spot** (the network plane sees what the endpoint agents do not: the unmanaged device, the rogue IoT, the traffic between segments). Origins: the network-traffic-analysis (NTA) wave around 2019–2020, which evolved into NDR as a distinct category with response workflows ✅. The Gartner-listed vendor set (per the article's citation): Vectra AI, Darktrace, ExtraHop, Corelight, Trend Micro, Trellix, Arista, Cisco, LinkShadow ✅-secondary.
- **The flagged nuance (⚠ — flagged honestly).** The NDR *category* is verified, but two things are flagged: (1) the Wikipedia article itself carries a notability maintenance flag (the category is real — Gartner has run an NDR Magic Quadrant and every major vendor sells the capability — but the encyclopaedic record is thin), and (2) the *market positioning* claim that NDR "competes against broader platforms like SIEM and XDR for security budgets" (✅ per the article) is the architecturally interesting point: NDR is both a **SIEM feeder** (network-flow and metadata telemetry into the correlation layer) and a **standalone detection plane** (its own console with its own response actions) — the mature estate treats it as the former, the SIEM-reluctant estate as the latter.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** for a Cymbal Bank, the NDR plane's value is concentrated in two places: the **east-west traffic inside the data-centre and cloud segments** (the lateral-movement detection the endpoint agents partially miss — the ATT&CK lateral-movement tactic of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §6.5) and the **legacy-core estate** where endpoint agents cannot be installed (the AS/400-era platforms of [vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md](vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md) — the network sensor is the only visibility they offer). The zero-trust overlay (cross-ref [zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md](zero_trust_network_architecture_guide.md)) makes NDR the *verification* instrument of segmentation: if the segments are right, the east-west anomalies the NDR flags are the exceptions worth chasing.

## 4. The Response Stack

### 4.1 The SOAR Playbooks — Cortex XSOAR and Splunk SOAR

- **The verified foundation (⚠-structural via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.3 — cross-ref, not re-derived).** The SOAR — security orchestration, automation and response — is the layer that orchestrates playbooks across tools, automates the repeatable response steps, and manages the incident case from detection to closure; its value is **consistency and throughput** — the playbook encodes the senior analyst's decision logic so the Tier-1 analyst executes it identically at 3 a.m. This guide deepens the *playbook* itself and the two platforms the market names most.
- **The playbook anatomy (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of uniform practice).** A SOAR playbook is the executable form of the IR procedure — the same anatomy as the IR playbook of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.5, made machine-readable: **trigger** (the alert type that starts it), **enrichment steps** (look up the IP/hash/account in the intel feeds and the asset DB), **decision branches** (is the host critical? is the user privileged? — each branch a different path), **automated actions** (block the IP, quarantine the hash, disable the account, isolate the host — the low-judgement, high-volume steps), **human handoffs** (the containment-strategy decision, the forensic preservation, the regulator notification — the high-judgement steps the playbook *pauses* on), and **the case file** (every step logged into the case record of §4.2 — the evidence chain). The design rules: **automate the repeatable, pause on the judgemental** (the sibling's §3.3 rule), **every playbook has an owner and an exercise date** (the tabletop of §9.3), and **playbooks are versioned like code** (a playbook that changed in production but not in the repo is a liability).
- **Palo Alto Cortex XSOAR (✅ platform facts via the vendor page, extracted live; the marketing claims ⚠-secondary).** Cortex XSOAR (the former Demisto, acquired by Palo Alto Networks in 2019 ⚠-canon for the acquisition details — widely documented, not re-verified this pass) is positioned as the security-automation platform: **900+ prebuilt integration and automation packs** and "1,000s of security actions for DIY playbooks" with a **visual playbook editor**; the **virtual war room** for real-time investigation and collaboration (ChatOps, CLI, auto-documentation for audit reporting); the threat-intel automation ("automate indicator processing and scoring... auto-push the latest indicators to EDLs", with Unit 42 intel); and the Marketplace content ecosystem. The vendor's headline claims — "reduce time spent on incidents by 90%" and the KuppingerCole "Overall Leader" ranking — are **⚠-secondary vendor marketing** (the 90% figure is self-reported "from aggregated customer use cases"), and the public-sector case study (North Dakota IT: "196 playbooks help close over 60% of incidents") is a vendor-published story ⚠-secondary. The architectural point that survives the marketing: XSOAR's differentiation is the **content marketplace** — the playbook/integration catalogue that makes the automation estate shareable and the war-room collaboration model.
- **Splunk SOAR (✅ platform facts via the vendor page, extracted live; the marketing claims ⚠-secondary).** Splunk SOAR (formerly **Splunk Phantom** — the f.k.a. is visible in the vendor's own community link ✅) is positioned as the orchestration layer that "connects with **300+ third-party tools** and supports **2,800+ automated actions**", with a **visual playbook editor**, **comprehensive case management** ("task segmentation, assignment, and documentation"), prebuilt playbooks that "leverage the **MITRE ATT&CK and D3FEND** frameworks" ✅, built-in threat research, and cloud/on-prem/hybrid deployment. The 2026 convergence (✅ visible on the vendor page): "**Splunk SOAR is now a native capability within Splunk Enterprise Security**" — the unified SecOps platform "seamlessly integrated with agentic AI, SOAR, UEBA, and SIEM". The architectural point: Splunk's differentiation is the **SIEM-to-SOAR adjacency** — the same vendor's detection and response layers share the data model, which is the convergence direction the whole market is moving (the sibling's §3.3 note: "SIEM and SOAR consolidating into next-gen/agentic platforms" — now verified in both the Splunk and CrowdStrike product lines ⚠-secondary for the marketing framing).
- **The XSOAR vs Splunk SOAR reading (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** both platforms deliver the same §4.1 anatomy; the selection differentiators are the **content ecosystem** (XSOAR's marketplace breadth), the **SIEM adjacency** (Splunk SOAR inside Enterprise Security), and the **estate integration** (which platform already speaks the house's tools). The non-negotiable for the SOC: the SOAR must be **the case-management system of record** (§4.2), or it is just an automation toy.

### 4.2 The Case Management — the Ticketing Layer

- **The requirement (⚠-structural — the case file is the spine of every response; cross-ref the ITSM mechanics of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §5.1).** Every alert that survives triage becomes a **case** — the persistent, auditable record of the investigation: the events examined, the decisions made, the actions taken, the evidence preserved, the timeline logged. The case file is simultaneously three things: the **work queue** (who owns it, what is next), the **audit trail** (what was done, when, by whom — the evidence chain a bank's examiner will read), and the **knowledge base** (what the investigation learned — the feed for the post-incident review and the playbook updates). The tooling question — build the case layer in the SOAR (XSOAR's war room, Splunk SOAR's case management), in the ITSM platform (ServiceNow-class — the sibling's §5.1 system of record), or in both — is resolved by one rule: **the security case must flow into the enterprise ticketing and change machinery without losing its security-specific fields** (the ATT&CK tags, the IoCs, the timeline, the evidence hashes). In a bank the case file is also the **regulator artefact**: the incident timeline of §9.2 is the case file's timestamps rendered as a report.
- **The case lifecycle (⚠-structural, standard practice):** **open** (an alert escalates from T1), **investigating** (T2 owns it, the enrichment and scope work happen), **contained** (the adversary's spread is stopped — the phase where the incident may be declared and the IR lifecycle of §4.3 takes over), **eradicated/recovered** (the adversary removed, the service restored), **closed** (the post-incident review logged, the lessons fed to the playbook and the detection backlog), and **reopened** (new evidence — the case file is the memory the next analyst starts from). The operational discipline: **one case per incident, linked, never duplicated** — the dedup rule that keeps the metrics of §8 honest.
- **The banking twist (⚠-structural):** the case file's audit trail must survive an examination — the maker-checker discipline of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §3.6 applies to the *containment actions* (who isolated the host, who approved it), and the timestamps must come from the system clock, not the analyst's memory. Cross-ref the audit-as-code angle ([audit_as_code_guide.md](audit_as_code_guide.md)) for the evidence-as-code philosophy.

### 4.3 The IR Orchestration — the NIST SP 800-61 Lifecycle in the SOC

- **The verified foundation (✅ via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.5 — cross-ref, not re-derived).** The incident-response lifecycle is NIST SP 800-61: **Preparation → Detection and Analysis → Containment, Eradication and Recovery → Post-Incident Activity** — Rev. 2 (August 2012), **withdrawn 3 April 2025 and superseded by Rev. 3** (April 2025, the CSF 2.0 community profile) ✅. The sibling owns the lifecycle facts and the playbook anatomy; this guide deepens the *orchestration* — how the SOC's standing machinery (§2–§4.2) plugs into the lifecycle when an incident is declared.
- **The orchestration mechanics (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of the verified parts):**
  - **Preparation ↔ the standing SOC.** The preparation phase is where the §2–§4 machinery is *built*: the tiered team, the playbooks, the case management, the tabletop exercises (§9.3), the escalation matrix ([operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.4), and the **regulator notification map** (who must be told, by when — the DORA/MAS clock, cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1 and §8.4). The SOC that trains is the SOC that prepared.
  - **Detection and Analysis ↔ the detection stack.** The phase runs on §3: the alert that survived triage is the incident candidate; the T2 investigation (scope: what systems, what data, what impact) is the analysis; the case file (§4.2) is the record. The sibling's warning is the orchestration point: "the phase where most incidents are lost (misclassified or unanalysed)" — which is why the §8 metrics (time-to-detect, false-positive rate) are the phase's scoreboard.
  - **Containment, Eradication and Recovery ↔ the response stack.** The containment strategy (disconnect-and-preserve vs keep-and-observe) is the human handoff the playbooks pause on (§4.1); the containment *execution* (host isolation, credential reset, egress blocking) is the SOAR automation; eradication (removing the adversary — the T3 forensics feed) and recovery (restoring the service — the resilience machinery of [zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md](zero_downtime_system_design_guide.md)) are the cross-functional bridge where the SOC hands to the business and the IT operations teams with the case file as the shared record.
  - **Post-Incident Activity ↔ the improvement loop.** The lessons-learned review closes back into Preparation: the playbook update, the detection-content change (the new Sigma rule of §3.1), the metric review (what would have compressed the MTTA/MTTR of §8.1), and the **action-closure rate** (the sibling's IR metric that proves the loop lands).
- **The Rev. 3 nuance (✅ via the sibling's §3.5):** the 2025 update reframes IR as an activity embedded in the **NIST CSF 2.0** risk-management cycle (cross-ref the sibling's §6.2) — the orchestration consequence is that the SOC's IR evidence (the case files, the timelines, the metrics) should be structured so it can be *reported through the CSF lens* (Detect/Respond/Recover outcomes), not just as standalone handling records. The four-phase lifecycle remains the operating model; the CSF 2.0 profile is the reporting model.

## 5. The Managed Services

### 5.1 The MDR — the Managed Detection and Response Model

- **The definition (✅ verified live this pass — the Wikipedia MDR record, itself grounded in the Trend Micro definition, the Gartner Peer Insights market page and the CrowdStrike explainer).** **Managed detection and response (MDR)** is "a type of cybersecurity service providing customers with a **cyberdefense technology and the associated remotely delivered human expertise**" — services that "help organizations **monitor, detect, analyze and respond** to advanced cyber threats" ✅. MDR is "a form of managed security service (MSS)" ✅ — the modern, detection-and-response-focused descendant of the older MSSP model (§5.2). The verified feature set: **24/7 monitoring and analysis by security experts, investigation and prioritization of threats, detailed remediation recommendations, access to advanced tools and threat intelligence, and ongoing threat-hunting services** ✅. The market record: Gartner's prediction that **50% of all enterprises will have adopted MDR services by 2025** ⚠-secondary (cited via the Wikipedia record — the Gartner original is paywalled), and the 2025 trendline — "detection engineering and security posture management" as the emerging MDR services (✅ via the Forrester Wave MDR Q1 2025 announcement, cited in the record).
- **The MDR operating model (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of the verified definition).** The MDR provider runs a *remote SOC* on the customer's behalf — the provider's analysts (its own tiered team, §2.1) monitor the customer's estate through the provider's platform (typically the provider's EDR/XDR, §3.2, or the customer's existing tools), triage and investigate the alerts, and either execute the response actions directly or deliver the remediation playbook for the customer's in-house team to run. The model's two shapes: **technology-plus-service** (the provider's sensors and platform, fully operated — CrowdStrike's Falcon Complete shape, "elite 24/7 managed detection and response" ⚠-secondary vendor language) and **bring-your-own-tools** (the provider operates the customer's SIEM/EDR stack). The MDR value proposition against the skills gap: the customer buys the §2.1 tiered model (especially the T2/T3 depth) without the hiring burden — the Wikipedia record's framing: MDR "aims to address the growing cybersecurity skills gap... and overwhelmed security teams dealing with increasing volumes of alerts" ✅.
- **The MDR-vs-MSSP distinction (✅-structural — the definitions support it):** the classic **MSSP** delivered *monitoring and alerting* — the customer still did the investigation and the response; the **MDR** delivers the *full detection-and-response loop* — investigation, prioritization, containment guidance, threat hunting (✅ the verified feature set above). The sibling's decision map ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.7) names the direction: "the MSSP delivers telemetry and triage, the bank owns the decisions and the regulator relationship" — MDR is the market's answer to the half that the MSSP left with the customer.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** for a Cymbal Bank, the MDR model's attraction is the **24-7 T2/T3 depth on demand** — the overnight and weekend investigation capacity of §2.2 without the eight-analyst-per-site staffing math — and its constraint is the **regulatory evidence burden**: the MDR's case files, timelines and decision records are *the customer's* evidence (§9.2), so the contract must specify the evidence format, the notification SLAs, and the escalation path to the in-house IR coordinator. The MDR is a *capacity buy*, not an *accountability transfer* — the sibling's rule: "the incident timeline and the audit trail must be owned, even when the monitoring is outsourced."

### 5.2 The MSSP Angle and the Co-Managed SOC

- **The MSSP baseline (✅ via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.1 — cross-ref, not re-derived).** The SOC "can be in-house or outsourced to a managed security service provider (MSSP) — the common shape for smaller organisations" ✅. The MSSP delivers monitoring, detection and analysis as a service; the customer retains the response decisions. The §3.7 decision map of the sibling is this guide's framing: the build-buy-outsource axis runs on **crown-jewel density, the 24×7 reality, and the regulatory evidence burden**.
- **The co-managed SOC (⚠ — flagged honestly; the model is uniformly described in the vendor/managed-service literature but no single primary source was extracted live this pass).** The **co-managed SOC** is the hybrid: the customer runs its own SOC core (the T1/T2 in-house team, the crown-jewel use cases, the IR authority, the regulator relationship) and the provider supplies the *overflow and the gaps* — the overnight shift, the weekend queue, the T3 hunting depth, the niche skill (a forensics surge, a cloud-incident specialist). The division of labour is contractual and operational: **the in-house SOC owns the decisions and the evidence; the provider owns the capacity and the execution of the delegated steps** — with the case file (§4.2) as the shared record and the handover mechanics of §2.2 (documented handover, shared tooling, clear ownership of the handoff moment) as the operating discipline. The distinction from pure MDR: co-managed implies a *standing in-house SOC that is being augmented*; MDR implies the provider *is* the SOC. The banking shape (⚠-structural, consistent with the sibling's §3.7 "hybrid" and §9.3 "in-house core + MSSP follow-the-sun"): the co-managed model is what a bank's SOC actually looks like in 2026 — in-house core days, managed nights/weekends, MDR-style hunting on the vendor side, and the bank's own IR command throughout.
- **The contracting essentials (⚠-structural — the universal managed-service pitfalls, framed for the bank):** the **scope** (which log sources, which estates, which hours — the un-scoped contract monitors nothing), the **SLA ladder** (time-to-triage, time-to-notify, the escalation thresholds — the §8 metrics written into the contract), the **evidence obligations** (case-file format, timeline access, audit rights — the regulator artefact of §9.2), the **exit plan** (data portability, playbook ownership, the knowledge transfer — the ITIL L4/vendor-governance rule of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.1: "L4 work is governed, not performed"), and the **subcontractor chain** (who the provider's provider is — the supply-chain angle of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §2.6 applied to the security estate itself).

## 6. The Threat Intelligence

### 6.1 The CTI Feeds — the Commercial and the Open

- **The discipline (✅ verified live this pass — the Wikipedia CTI record).** **Cyber threat intelligence (CTI)** is "a part of cybersecurity that focuses on collecting, analyzing, and sharing information about potential or existing cyber threats", giving organisations "the information needed to predict, prevent, and respond to cyberattacks" ✅. The canonical three levels (✅): **tactical** — the most technical, the specific **indicators of compromise (IOCs)** — IP addresses, domains, file hashes, malware signatures — consumed directly by the SIEM rules, firewalls and EDR ("security operations teams use these indicators to identify and block threats in real time" ✅); **operational** — the adversary-behaviour level — who the actors are, their **TTPs**, infrastructure patterns, ongoing campaigns — consumed by the analysts and the hunters; **strategic** — the board/risk level — the threat landscape, the sector exposure, the investment implications (the [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §2 landscape reports are strategic intelligence). The sources (✅ per the record): open-source data, social media, device log files, forensic analysis, internet traffic, and the dark/deep web.
- **The feed landscape (⚠-structural for the specific feed list — the *categories* are standard; the named examples are illustrative and widely documented, not re-verified live this pass):**
  - **The open/commercial feeds.** The open baseline includes the **CISA KEV catalog** (the exploited-in-the-wild list — ✅ verified live, §7.2), the **vendor advisories** (CrowdStrike, Mandiant, Microsoft, Palo Alto Unit 42 — the threat-research arms of the §3.2 platforms), the **ISAC/ISAO sharing groups** (sector information-sharing — the financial sector's FS-ISAC is the banking-relevant one ⚠-structural; the CTI record confirms ISACs as a sharing mechanism ✅), and the **open-source feeds** (the abuse.ch/URLhaus-class blocklists ⚠-structural, and the open detection content of §3.1's Sigma ecosystem). The commercial feeds (Recorded Future, Mandiant, CrowdStrike Falcon Intelligence, etc. ⚠-structural for the specific positioning) differentiate on **operational/strategic depth** — the TTP-level actor reporting and the analyst access — not on the IOC lists alone.
  - **The feed-to-SOC pipeline (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the tactical IOCs flow to the **automatic matching layer** (the SIEM and EDR check inbound events against the feed — the §3.4 intel-matching row); the operational TTPs flow to the **analyst layer** (the hunt calendar and the use-case backlog of §3.1 — "which of our estates could be running the technique this campaign uses?"); the strategic intelligence flows to the **governance layer** (the risk register and the steering-committee briefing — the §5.3 machinery of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md)). The pipeline's quality rule: **feeds are scored on relevance, timeliness and actionability — an intel feed that never changes a rule or a hunt is decoration.**

### 6.2 The TIPs — the Threat-Intelligence Platforms

- **The category (⚠ — flagged honestly).** The **threat-intelligence platform (TIP)** is the tooling layer that aggregates the feeds (§6.1), normalises and de-duplicates the indicators, manages the intelligence lifecycle (collection → processing → analysis → dissemination), and pushes the curated indicators into the detection and blocking layers (the SIEM, the EDR, the firewalls, the SOAR's indicator automation of §4.1). The TIP is the *intel data plane*: without it, the SOC's intel consumption is a spreadsheet and a bookmark folder. **Verification note: the dedicated Wikipedia article does not exist (404'd live this pass), and no single primary source was extracted live — the category is ⚠-structural at best, with the CTI record's reference to "threat intelligence platforms" as a section of the discipline (✅ that the *concept* is part of the CTI canon, per the CTI article's structure).** The STIX/TAXII angle (✅ via the CTI record's mention of structured threat intelligence — the OASIS standards for expressing and exchanging indicators) is the TIP's native language: a TIP that cannot speak STIX/TAXII is a silo.
- **The TIP's operating value (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** three jobs — **aggregation and de-duplication** (N feeds in, one clean indicator store out — the same indicator from three feeds is one indicator, not three alerts), **enrichment and scoring** (the indicator gets its context: who reported it, when, against what sector — the §4.1 enrichment step), and **dissemination** (the curated indicator sets pushed to the blocking and detection layers on a schedule — the "auto-push the latest indicators to EDLs" the XSOAR page describes, ⚠-secondary). For the bank, the TIP is also the **sharing gateway** — the place where the bank's own intelligence (the TTPs observed in its own estate) is formatted for ISAC sharing, the CTI record's "intelligence sharing" direction ✅.
- **The build-vs-buy note (⚠-structural):** the SIEM vendors embed increasing intel-management capability (the Splunk ES "built-in threat intelligence" of §4.1, the XSOAR indicator processing — both ⚠-secondary vendor claims), which compresses the standalone-TIP market; the architect's rule is the same as the SIEM/XDR one — **the intel layer must feed the detection layer as a first-class stream or it does not exist.**

### 6.3 The Intel-Driven Detection — the Feed-to-Rule Loop

- **The loop (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of the verified parts):** the mature SOC runs intelligence as a *feedback loop*, not a subscription: **(1) consume** — the feeds of §6.1 land in the TIP of §6.2; **(2) match** — the tactical IOCs auto-match against the telemetry (the §3.4 intel-matching row); **(3) investigate** — the matches that survive triage become §4.2 cases; **(4) convert** — the confirmed TTPs become **new detection content** (the §3.1 use cases and Sigma rules) and **hunting hypotheses** (the §2.1 T3 calendar — "who in our estate could be running this campaign's technique?"); **(5) share** — the estate's own observations flow back to the ISAC and the vendors; **(6) measure** — the intel-to-detection conversion rate (how many intel items became rules) is a §8 metric. The loop's failure mode is the **intel graveyard** — the feed subscription that produces reports nobody reads and IOCs nobody matches; the control is the conversion metric.
- **The ATT&CK tie-in (✅ via [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3 and [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §6.5 — cross-ref, not re-derived):** ATT&CK is the shared vocabulary that makes the loop work — the intel reports name techniques by ID, the detection content is tagged by ID, the hunting hypotheses are written by ID, and the purple-team gap reports ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.6) are ID-mapped. An intel feed that arrives without ATT&CK mapping is harder to operationalise; the TIP's enrichment step should attach the IDs.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** the bank's intel priorities follow its crown jewels ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §9.1): the **payment-rails TTPs** (the SWIFT/instant-payment fraud chains), the **client-data exfiltration tradecraft**, the **privileged-identity attacks** (the identity plane of [distributed_auth_guide.md](distributed_auth_guide.md)), and the **supply-chain campaigns** against the vendor estate (cross-ref [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §2.6). The FS-ISAC-class sector sharing is the force multiplier — the bank's own incident observations become the sector's early warning.

## 7. The Vulnerability Management

### 7.1 The Scanning — the Continuous Discovery Layer

- **The problem (✅ the vector, via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §2.1 — cross-ref, not re-derived):** the Verizon DBIR 2026's headline — **31% of breaches now start with software vulnerabilities, the top initial vector** — makes vulnerability management a SecOps *frontline* discipline, not an audit chore: the unpatched endpoint is the initial access. The scanning layer is the discovery machinery, and the cluster already owns its hardest corner: [vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md](vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md) documents the legacy-estate reality (the C/Pro\*C/COBOL codebases that the enterprise scanners barely see — the platform-level tools, the limited SAST coverage, the custom-scanning workarounds) — cross-ref §1–§6 of that guide for the mechanics; this guide owns the *workflow* around the scanner.
- **The scanning stack (⚠-structural — the tool taxonomy is universal; the specifics are this guide's synthesis):** **(1) external attack-surface scanning** (the internet-facing estate — the ASM tools that see the exposed APIs and misconfigured services — the attack-surface-management angle also in the bug-bounty context of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §4.4); **(2) internal network scanning** (the credentialed scans of the internal estate — the "continuous vulnerability management" control of the CIS Controls v8, ✅ via [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §6.4); **(3) agent-based posture assessment** (the EDR/XDR agents of §3.2 reporting the software inventory and the missing patches from inside the host — the Defender Vulnerability Management module of §3.2 is the named example ✅); **(4) application-layer scanning** — the SAST/DAST/SCA pipeline of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §5 (the build-time half); and **(5) the container/cloud posture layer** — the CSPM of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §7 (the misconfiguration drift). The SecOps point: **all five feed one vulnerability backlog** — the same register that the threat model ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §2.8) and the purple loop ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.6) feed — or the remediation effort fragments and the 31% vector wins.
- **The scanning cadence and the false-positive problem (⚠-structural):** the scanner's output is a findings list, and the list's quality is the scanner's *tuning* (the false-positive rate of §8.3 applied to vulnerabilities — an untuned scanner floods the remediation queue with noise and buries the real exploitable finding). The cadence rule: **continuous for the exposed surfaces, scheduled-deep for the internal estate, event-driven after every material change** (the same rule as the pentest cadence of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §4.2).

### 7.2 The Prioritization — the CVSS, the EPSS and the CISA KEV

The scanner produces more findings than any team can patch — prioritization is the actual discipline, and the modern stack is three instruments used together:

- **The CVSS (✅ verified live this pass — first.org/cvss).** The **Common Vulnerability Scoring System** is the FIRST-standard "way to capture the principal characteristics of a vulnerability and produce a **numerical score reflecting its severity**", translatable "into a qualitative representation (such as low, medium, high, and critical) to help organizations properly assess and prioritize their vulnerability management processes" ✅. The current version is **CVSS v4.0** (the initial release November 2023, with ongoing SIG updates and the Consumer Implementation Guide) ✅; **v3.1** is the prior widely-deployed version ⚠-structural for the transition state (v4.0's adoption across the NVD and the vendor ecosystem is a migration in progress, not a completed fact). The architectural caveat (⚠-structural, the FIRST "why EPSS" argument makes the same point): **CVSS measures intrinsic severity, not exploitation likelihood** — a critical-CVSS bug nobody exploits is less urgent than a medium-CVSS bug that is being actively weaponised.
- **The EPSS (✅ verified live this pass — first.org/epss).** The **Exploit Prediction Scoring System** is "a data-driven machine-learning model that **estimates the probability that a published CVE will be exploited in the wild in the next 30 days**" ✅ — it "offsets subjective judgments with empirical signals from observed exploitation and ongoing activity", publishes a **0–1 probability with ranking percentiles** for every CVE **daily**, and is "freely and openly accessible via CSV and API" ✅. It is maintained by the **FIRST EPSS Special Interest Group**, generated by Empirical Security, and fed by exploitation-activity partners including VulnCheck KEV, the Shadowserver Foundation and LevelBlue ✅. The operational reading: **EPSS is the likelihood axis the CVSS lacks** — a high-EPSS finding is the one to patch this week; a low-EPSS critical is the one to schedule.
- **The CISA KEV (✅ verified live this pass — cisa.gov).** The **Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog** is "the authoritative source of vulnerabilities that have been exploited in the wild", which "organizations should use... as an input to their vulnerability management prioritization framework" ✅. Live-verified facts this pass: the catalog stood at **~1,676 entries** (the current page's count), each with a **Date Added** and a **Due Date**, the action guidance referencing **BOD 26-04 ("Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk")** — the CISA directive generation that replaced the original BOD 22-01 framing ⚠-structural for the directive-history reading (the page references BOD 26-04; the 22-01→26-04 progression is the documented record) — the **CSV/JSON machine feeds** ✅, the **nomination channel** for unlisted exploited vulnerabilities ✅, and the **ransomware-campaign flag** on entries ("Known To Be Used in Ransomware Campaigns") ✅.
- **The three-instrument stack (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of the verified parts — the FIRST "using EPSS" guidance itself recommends combining EPSS with CVSS and KEV):** the working rule — **KEV first** (if it is in the catalog, it is being exploited *now*: the due-date discipline applies, for a bank with the regulator's version of urgency), **then EPSS** (the likelihood ranking that orders the rest of the queue), **then CVSS** (the severity context that sizes the effort). The output is the **prioritized backlog**: the exploited-in-the-wild, the likely-exploited-soon, and the severity-scheduled remainder — the queue §7.3's patching SLA consumes.
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** the bank's regulatory posture makes the stack *evidence*: the MAS TRM/FSM-N05 regime and DORA both expect a vulnerability-management process that demonstrably prioritizes — the KEV/EPSS/CVSS stack is the defensible, machine-readable prioritization record the examiner can sample (cross-ref [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §8.1 and §8.4). The legacy-core corner (the AS/400-era estate of [vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md](vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md)) is where the stack's *coverage* fails — the un-scannable codebase is the prioritization blind spot, mitigated by compensating controls (segmentation, the NDR of §3.3, the network-level protections).

### 7.3 The Patching — the SLA and the Remediation Loop

- **The SLA (⚠ — flagged honestly).** The patching SLA — the promised time between a finding's confirmation and its remediation — is a **house-defined contract, not a universal standard**: the only external anchors are the KEV catalog's per-entry due dates (the CISA BOD-driven deadlines for the US federal civilian estate, ✅ verified as part of the catalog's structure — a bank's due dates are its own, informed by the same urgency logic) and the regulatory expectation of a *defined and evidenced* remediation process (⚠-structural, the MAS/DORA posture). The common enterprise shape (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of uniform practice): **critical/KEV findings in days (24–72 hours), high in weeks, medium/low on the scheduled maintenance cycle** — with the *SLA clock* starting at the confirmation of the finding (a scanner hit is not yet a confirmed exploitable finding; the false-positive triage of §7.1 happens before the clock starts, and the SLA discipline demands that triage be timed too).
- **The remediation loop (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the loop is the SecOps version of the support problem-management cycle ([operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §3.2): **triage** (the false-positive filter), **prioritize** (the §7.2 stack), **assign** (the owner per asset — the patch goes to the system team, the compensating control goes to the security engineers), **remediate** (the patch, or the accepted-risk decision with a sign-off and a review date — the governance machinery of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §5.3), **verify** (the re-scan — the only honest proof a patch landed), and **report** (the SLA-compliance rate — the §8 metric that proves the loop). The loop's failure modes: the **unowned asset** (the finding with no owner — the inventory problem the CIS Controls IG1 addresses, ✅ [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §6.4), the **silent risk acceptance** (the finding that lapsed into "accepted" without a signature — the risk-register discipline of [security_by_design_guide.md](security_by_design_guide.md) §2.8), and the **patch-caused outage** (the availability-versus-security tension — the change-enablement discipline of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §3.3 and the staged-rollout practice).
- **The bank reading (⚠-structural):** the patching tension is sharpest in the bank: the **payment rails and the trading floor resist patches** (availability-first, the change-window culture), while the **31%-vulnerability-vector reality** ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §2.1) says the unpatched surface is the attacker's front door. The resolution is the same as the OT discipline ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §7.8): **segmentation buys time** — the unpatched legacy core is compensated by the network controls (§3.3), and the *compensating control* is a first-class remediation outcome, not an excuse.

## 8. The SecOps Metrics

### 8.1 The MTTA/MTTR — the Time-Based Scoreboard

- **The family (✅-structural for the definitions — MTTR verified live; the SecOps naming ⚠-structural, consistent with the sibling's TTD/TTR framing in [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.1 and §3.5).** The SOC's primary scoreboard is the time-based family: **MTTA — mean time to acknowledge** (the clock from the alert's creation to the analyst's first action — the *responsiveness* metric), **MTTD — mean time to detect** (the clock from the attacker's activity to its detection — the *coverage* metric, the dwell-time view from inside), **MTTR — mean time to respond/repair** (the clock from detection to containment/restoration — the *effectiveness* metric). The verified anchors: **MTTR** is "the average time it takes to repair a system or piece of equipment after it has failed... includes the time it takes to detect the failure, diagnose the issue and fix the problem", computed as **total repair time ÷ number of repairs** ✅ (Wikipedia + the IBM explainer, extracted live — the IBM explainer also notes MTTR "sometimes referred to as mean time to recovery"); the sibling's IR-metric list — **TTD, TTC (time to contain), time to eradicate, time to recover** ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.5) — is the same family with the incident-lifecycle granularity. MTTA as a distinct name is ⚠-structural (the concept — acknowledge latency — is universal practice; the exact initialism is one of several).
- **The scoreboard discipline (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the time metrics are only meaningful with **defined clocks**: when does the detection clock start (the alert timestamp? the attacker's first event — which the hunt may only discover later?), when does the response clock stop (containment? eradication? full recovery?). The M-Trends dwell-time definition ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.4) is the honest anchor: **the detection clock ideally starts at the attacker's first action, not at the alert** — which is why the *hunting* activity of §2.1 is the only thing that improves the true MTTD, and why the incident timeline of §9.2 must record the event timestamps, not just the alert timestamps. The trend, not the absolute: the SOC that reduces its MTTA/MTTR trend line is improving; the SOC that reports a static number is reporting a ritual.
- **The target-setting (⚠-structural):** targets are house-defined (no universal benchmark was verified this pass — the SANS survey figures are flagged in §2.3), with two honest anchors: the **on-call response targets** of the SRE world (5 minutes for the time-critical services, 30 minutes for the rest — ✅ via [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.3) give the *acknowledge* tier its shape, and the **KEV due-date logic** (§7.2) gives the *remediate* tier its urgency. For the bank, the regulator's clock (the notification deadline) is the hard outer bound on MTTR: the containment must beat the notification deadline, or the timeline shows the SOC reporting an uncontained incident.

### 8.2 The Detection Coverage — the ATT&CK View

- **The metric (⚠ — flagged honestly).** **Detection coverage** is the share of the adversary's behaviour space the SOC can see — and the industry's working language for it is **ATT&CK** (✅ via [threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §5.3 and [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §6.5): the SOC's use cases and Sigma rules are tagged by technique ID (§3.1), so the coverage view is the matrix of *techniques with at least one detection* versus the techniques the estate's threat model ([threat_modeling_guide.md](threat_modeling_guide.md) §9) says matter. The flagged nuance: **coverage counts detections, not quality** — a technique with one untuned rule and a technique with three tuned rules both show as "covered", so the honest metric pair is **coverage × the false-positive rate** (§8.3): the coverage map shows where the SOC *looks*, the false-positive rate shows how *clearly* it looks. The Sigma ecosystem's ATT&CK heatmap (the SigmaHQ repo's own coverage tracking, visible in the repository structure ✅) is the community's version of the same view.
- **The operating use (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the coverage matrix is the **detection backlog's planning instrument**: the purple-team gap reports ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.6) land as "no detection for T1021" and become use cases; the intel-driven loop (§6.3) adds the campaign-relevant techniques; the hunt calendar (§2.1) fills the gaps that rules cannot cover yet. The bank's coverage priorities follow the crown jewels ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §9.1): the payment-rails techniques, the privileged-identity techniques, the exfiltration techniques — full coverage of the estate's *whole* matrix is the aspiration, coverage of the crown-jewel paths is the requirement.

### 8.3 The False-Positive Rate and the Alert Fatigue

- **The definitions (⚠-structural — the concepts are universal; the benchmarks are not).** The **false-positive rate** is the share of alerts that are not real incidents — the noise the §3.1 tuning loop exists to compress; **alert fatigue** is the human consequence — the analyst who has learned that most alerts are noise starts dismissing the queue reflexively, and the *real* alert gets dismissed with the noise. The two are the SecOps version of the support world's pager problem ([operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.3 names "alert fatigue" as an on-call failure mode ✅-structural) and the sibling names the SIEM failure mode ("the alert flood... the control is tuning against the false-positive rate", [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.2 ⚠-structural). **No universal FP-rate benchmark is quoted here** (the SANS survey figures are flagged in §2.3; the vendor claims of single-digit rates are marketing ⚠-secondary) — the honest framing is the *direction and the loop*: FP rate trended down, mean-time-to-dismiss (the T1 speed at closing false positives) trended down, and the *true-positive yield* (real incidents per 1,000 alerts) trended up.
- **The fatigue controls (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis of the verified parts):** **the tuning loop** (§3.1 — every confirmed-benign alert is a rule-change signal), **the tier economics** (§2.1 — T1 absorbs the dismissal volume so T2/T3's attention is preserved), **the automation-first rule** (the SOAR of §4.1 auto-handles the known-benign classes so the analyst never sees them), **the noise budget** (the SRE-style cap: a use case whose FP rate exceeds the agreed budget is disabled until fixed — the error-budget idea of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §6.3 applied to detection content), and **the fatigue metric itself** (the alert-per-analyst-per-shift load, reviewed against the SRE on-call sustainability caps — "no more than 25% of time on-call" ✅ ch. 11). The bank's version of the control is sharper: an alert queue so noisy that a real payment-rail intrusion is dismissed is not a metrics problem — it is the regulatory finding of §9.2.

### 9.3 The Tabletop Exercises — the Rehearsed Response

- **The practice (⚠-structural — flagged honestly; the tabletop is universal preparedness practice and the NIST 800-61 Preparation phase implies it, but no single primary source was extracted live this pass).** The **tabletop exercise** is the rehearsal of the IR lifecycle without the production impact: the IR coordinator and the stakeholders sit around the scenario ("a ransomware outbreak has encrypted the payments file shares; the exfiltration channel is still open; it is 9 p.m. on a Friday") and walk the response — the decisions, the notifications, the timeline — on paper. Its SecOps value is threefold: it **trains the muscle** (the tiered team rehearses the §4.3 orchestration before the real incident), it **surfaces the gaps** (the missing contact, the unclear decision authority, the playbook step that does not work — the same gap-finding logic as the purple engagements of [cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.6, at the process level rather than the detection level), and it **produces the evidence** (the exercise record, the lessons list and the follow-up actions are exactly what a TRM/DORA examination asks for — the supervised-artefact logic of §9.2).
- **The exercise programme (⚠-structural, this guide's synthesis):** the mature bank runs a **cadence of tabletops** — the top-five playbooks ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.5's list: phishing/credential theft, ransomware, BEC/payment fraud, supply-chain compromise, insider exfiltration) each exercised at least annually, the **regulator-notification scenario** exercised with the compliance function present (the DORA/MAS clock is the scenario's dramatic tension), and the **crisis-communication scenario** with the executives (the hierarchical escalation rung of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.4). The exercise's output contract: the after-action report naming the gaps, the owners and the due dates — tracked in the same risk-register discipline as the purple-team findings.

## 10. The Worked Example — a SecOps Operating-Model Design for a Cymbal Bank

### 10.1 The Scenario — the Familiar Context

The scenario is the house itself, reduced to its security-operations-relevant shape: a **Cymbal Bank in Singapore** — the CIB markets estate (e-trading, treasury, connectivity) beside the group's mutualized platforms (shared identity, payments, the digital layer), regulated by MAS (FSM-N05, §9.2), with the payments envelope in the HSM/PCI-DSS class and a compliance machinery consuming security evidence. The brief: **design the SecOps operating model** — not a product purchase, but the whole machine: the tiered model and the staffing (§10.2), the tooling stack (§10.3), the metrics and the scoreboard (§10.4), assembled from the §2–§8 material and cross-ref'd to the cluster. The crown jewels are the discipline map's list ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §9.1): the payment rails, the client-confidential data, the market positions, the privileged identities, the key estate — every design decision below follows from naming what the SOC exists to protect.

### 10.2 The Design — the Tiered Model and the Staffing

- **The tiers (§2.1):** **Tier 1 — triage**, in-house in Singapore for the day window (4–6 analysts on rotation), covering the alert queue across the estates; **Tier 2 — investigation**, in-house (4–6 analysts) with the follow-the-sun handoff to the group's European SOC for the overnight window ([operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.2 — the documented handover, the shared case file, the 30-minute overlap discipline); **Tier 3 — hunting and detection engineering**, in-house (2–3 senior specialists) owning the hunt calendar and the use-case backlog; **the IR coordinator** (1–2, the regulator-facing timeline); **the shift leads** (1 per shift, the escalation-matrix owners); and the **MDR augmentation** (§5.1) carrying the overnight T2 depth and the weekend queue — the hybrid the discipline map's §9.3 already picks ("in-house core + MSSP follow-the-sun"). The staffing math is the sibling's: the single-site 24/7 rotation needs a minimum of eight analysts per position on the SRE model (✅ ch. 11 facts) — the follow-the-sun-plus-MDR shape is exactly what makes that math affordable.
- **The shift pattern (§2.2):** the composite model — Singapore days in-house, European days via follow-the-sun, nights/weekends via the MDR provider with the in-house on-call (primary + secondary, the 5/30-minute response targets ✅) as the escalation rung. The handover checklist of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.2 (open incidents, pending changes, overnight alerts and known noise, vendor tickets, the risk register of the day, the outgoing site's gut) is the standing ritual at every transition.
- **The operating rules (§2.1):** escalation by defined criteria (severity × playbook-exhaustion, never frustration); knowledge flows downward (every T3 finding becomes a use case, every T2 investigation becomes a T1 playbook — the toil-reduction loop of [operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §6.2); and the case file (§4.2) as the one shared record across the in-house and the managed teams.

### 10.3 The Design — the Tooling Stack

- **The detection stack (§3):** one **SIEM** as the enterprise correlation plane with the log-source onboarding project as the real work (the legacy core last — [vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md](vuln_scanning_c_proc_cobol.md) §8's reality check); the **XDR/EDR** on the endpoints chosen by the estate logic of §3.2 (the Microsoft-homogeneous group platforms favour Defender XDR's native identity/email/endpoint correlation; the markets estate's low-latency endpoints favour the agent-light options — the selection is per-estate, with every platform's alerts flowing into the SIEM's case management as first-class events); the **NDR** (§3.3) on the east-west data-centre segments and the legacy-core network where agents cannot install; the **use-case backlog** written in Sigma (§3.1) and tagged with ATT&CK IDs, with the crown-jewel paths at full coverage (§8.2).
- **The response stack (§4):** the **SOAR** as the playbook engine and the case-management spine — the two verified platforms' differentiators (§4.1) weighed against the house's SIEM choice (the Splunk SOAR-native-in-ES shape if the SIEM is Splunk-class; the XSOAR marketplace shape if the content ecosystem wins); the top-five playbooks ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §3.5) encoded first — phishing/credential theft, ransomware, BEC/payment fraud, supply-chain compromise, insider exfiltration — each with the automated steps (enrichment, blocking, account disable) and the human handoffs (containment strategy, forensic preservation, regulator notification); the **case management** integrated with the ITSM platform ([operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §5.1) so the security case flows into the enterprise change machinery; the **IR orchestration** (§4.3) running the NIST 800-61 lifecycle with the timeline as a first-class data product.
- **The intelligence and the vulnerability layers (§6–§7):** the **TIP** aggregating the commercial and the open feeds with the tactical IOCs auto-matched, the operational TTPs feeding the hunt calendar, the strategic briefs feeding the steering committee (§6.2); the **vulnerability backlog** prioritized by the KEV→EPSS→CVSS stack (§7.2) with the patching SLA defined per estate (the payments envelope's change windows vs the 31%-vector urgency, §7.3); and the **MDR contract** (§5.2) specifying the evidence format, the notification SLAs and the escalation path to the in-house IR coordinator.

### 10.4 The Design — the Metrics and the Scoreboard

- **The time metrics (§8.1):** MTTA (the acknowledge clock — the T1 responsiveness), MTTD (the detection clock — the dwell-time view), MTTR (the containment clock — the effectiveness), trended monthly with defined clocks and reviewed against the on-call response targets (✅ 5/30-minute anchors) and the regulator's notification deadline as the hard outer bound. The incident timeline records the *event* timestamps (the attacker's first action, discovered in the hunt) not just the alert timestamps — the honest MTTD.
- **The quality metrics (§8.2–§8.3):** the detection-coverage matrix per estate (the crown-jewel paths at full coverage), the false-positive rate per use case with the noise budget (a use case over budget is disabled until fixed), the alert-per-analyst load reviewed against the SRE sustainability caps, and the intel-to-detection conversion rate (§6.3). The scoreboard's consumer is the steering committee ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §5.3) — the monthly report is the §8 table rendered as the governance briefing, with the dwell-time trend as the headline.
- **The evidence design (§9.2–§9.3):** the case files, the metrics trend, the tabletop records and the KEV/EPSS/CVSS prioritization log are the **examination artefacts** — structured for the FSM-N05/DORA sample from day one, not retrofitted at the audit.

### 10.6 The Lessons

1. **The operating model is the product, not the tools.** The Cymbal Bank SOC's differentiator is the §10.2–10.4 machine — the tiers, the shifts, the playbooks, the metrics, the evidence — not the brand of the SIEM; the tools are the machine's parts (§10.3), and every §3–§7 verification in this guide is a part-spec, not the design.
2. **The 24-7 reality is solved by the composite, not by heroics.** Follow-the-sun plus MDR plus on-call (the sibling's §3.7 hybrid, the SRE staffing math ✅) delivers the always-on watch with sustainable humans — the single-site night-rotation is the burnout trap ([operational_support_frameworks_guide.md](operational_support_frameworks_guide.md) §4.3).
3. **The regulator reads the evidence, not the ambition.** The FSM-N05/DORA examination samples the case files, the timelines, the tabletop records and the prioritization log (§9.2–§9.3) — which is why the §10.4 evidence design is a first-class deliverable, not an audit afterthought.
4. **The metrics are the operating system.** The MTTA/MTTR trend, the coverage matrix and the FP-rate loop are what make the SOC *steerable* (§8) — a SOC without the scoreboard is a team, not an operation.
5. **The crown jewels set the priority, everywhere.** The coverage targets, the use-case backlog, the hunt calendar and the patching SLA all trace back to the five crown jewels ([cybersecurity_guide.md](cybersecurity_guide.md) §9.1) — the estate-driven weighting of §9.1 is the design's spine. The investment case for the whole machine is owned by [../management/business_case_development_guide.md](../management/business_case_development_guide.md) (the cost-of-breach vs cost-of-SOC framing) and the team design by [../management/high_performance_team_guide.md](../management/high_performance_team_guide.md).

## 11. The One-Page Summary — the Final Word: the Always-On Watch

**SecOps — security operations — is the discipline of running the defence as a continuous, measured, 24-7 operation: the runtime of the security program, distinct from the DevSecOps build-time culture and executed by the SOC organisation.** It has five workstreams, each verified and cross-ref'd in this guide. **First, the operating model** — the Tier-1 triage / Tier-2 investigation / Tier-3 hunting-and-engineering ladder (⚠-structural but universally practised), the 24-7 shift patterns (follow-the-sun and the SRE on-call math ✅), and the maturity ladder from reactive to predictive (§2). **Second, the detection stack** — the SIEM correlation plane with use-case engineering as the real discipline (Sigma ✅, the ATT&CK-tagged backlog), the EDR/XDR platforms (CrowdStrike Falcon, SentinelOne, Microsoft Defender XDR — the platform facts ✅), and the NDR network plane for the blind spots (§3). **Third, the response stack** — the SOAR playbooks (Cortex XSOAR and Splunk SOAR ✅), the case-management spine that is simultaneously the work queue, the audit trail and the regulator artefact, and the NIST SP 800-61 IR orchestration (Rev. 2 → Rev. 3 ✅) that the standing SOC plugs into on declaration (§4). **Fourth, the surrounding machinery** — the managed services (the MDR model ✅, the co-managed SOC ⚠) that solve the 24-7 staffing economics, the threat intelligence (the tactical/operational/strategic levels ✅, the TIPs ⚠, the feed-to-rule loop) that makes detection targeted, and the vulnerability management (the scanning layer, the KEV → EPSS → CVSS prioritization stack — all three ✅ — and the patching SLA ⚠) that removes the attacker's front door (§5–§7). **Fifth, the metrics and the bank** — the MTTA/MTTR scoreboard (MTTR ✅), the detection-coverage matrix and the false-positive/alert-fatigue controls that keep the watch honest (§8), and the banking reality: the Cymbal Bank 24-7 SOC across the federation of estates, the MAS TRM expectations (FSM-N05, the Notice-655 cancellation ✅ via the sibling), and the tabletop exercises that rehearse the evidence (§9). The worked example assembles the whole machine for the house (§10). **The final word is the always-on watch**: the SOC is not a tool you buy or a team you staff — it is an operating model that turns the estate's raw noise into decided, timed, evidenced actions, every hour of every day, compressing the dwell time the adversary lives on; and it is only as good as the loop that feeds it — the tiers, the tools, the intelligence, the remediation and the metrics, all verified, all cross-ref'd, all running.
