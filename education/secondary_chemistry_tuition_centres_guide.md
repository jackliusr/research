## 1. Overview, the Honest Framing and the Decoder

### 1.1 The problem this guide starts with

If you search for "best Chemistry tuition Singapore", you will find ranked lists. Read them carefully and you will notice something: none of them is a quality assessment. They are commercial pages. A home-tuition agency publishes a "Top 12 Chemistry Tuition Centres" list; a directory publishes a "238 chemistry tuition centres" page whose own text states that "Listings are ranked using Google rating, review volume and website popularity, and are refreshed regularly" (SG DSA, *The Best Chemistry Tuition Centres in Singapore (2026)*, page states "Updated September 2026"; verified 2026-09-25). Both are transparent about being commercial artefacts. Neither claims to have examined teaching.

There is no independent, non-commercial assessment of tuition centres in Singapore. There is no inspection body that visits centres and publishes findings on teaching quality. There is no regulator that publishes a comparative quality metric for this service type. School results — the thing every centre advertises — are self-published, from self-selected cohorts, with unauditable denominators. No third party checks them.

Given that, **a defensible league table of Chemistry tuition centres cannot be produced.** Anyone who publishes one is either ranking something other than quality (price, review volume, Google stars, willingness to pay for placement) or inventing the quality component.

### 1.2 What this guide gives you instead

Two things, and only two:

1. **A named evaluation framework you apply yourself** (§9). The criteria, what good looks like, and how to test each one — including questions to put to the centre and things to look at during a trial lesson.
2. **A dated, verified factual profile set of centres** (§7). For each centre: existence, subjects and levels, delivery format, published locations, published fees, published class sizes — each attribute taken from the centre's own published material, with the date this guide read it. Where the centre publishes nothing on an attribute, this guide writes "not published" rather than guessing.

The profiles are ordered **alphabetically by centre name** — not by size, price, popularity or merit. The alphabetic order is the only ordering rule, and it exists precisely to avoid implying one.

### 1.3 The thesis, in one line

**The right fit beats the best name.** The question is not which centre is best; it is which arrangement of syllabus match, class size, teacher continuity, materials, progression feedback and schedule actually fits this child at this level in this subject form. A centre that is wrong for your child is wrong regardless of how many people recommend it.

### 1.4 The decoder — the vocabulary you need before you compare anything

These terms are used inconsistently by centres and by list articles. Fix their meanings now, or you will compare unlike things.

| Term | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Tuition centre** | A business operating fixed premises, running scheduled classes at published times, usually by academic year or term. The subject of this guide. | You are buying a *place in a class on a timetable*, not a person's calendar. |
| **1-to-1 tutor** | One student, one tutor, arranged privately or through an agency. Owned by the sibling guide, `sg_secondary_tutors_guide.md`. | A different product with different economics and a different failure mode. |
| **Class / small-group model** | Several students taught together for a fixed period per week. "Small group" is marketing language with no standard definition — the number is what counts, and it is often unpublished. | Class size is the single largest determinant of whether you are buying teaching or supervision. |
| **Class-size tiers** | Centres operate at roughly: 4–8 (small-group specialist), 8–15 (boutique), 15–30+ (lecture-style/chain). Tiers are not published consistently; count heads at a trial. | A "capped" class is a claim; a head-count is evidence. |
| **Pure subject** | Chemistry as a subject in its own right: GCE O-Level Chemistry, subject code **6092** (SEAB, 2026 O-Level syllabuses; page states "Last updated 29 April 2026"). | A full-depth syllabus with its own practical paper. |
| **Combined-science Chemistry component** | Chemistry as one half of a two-science subject: Science (Physics, Chemistry) **5086**, Science (Physics, Biology) **5087**, Science (Chemistry, Biology) **5088** (same SEAB source). | A shorter, differently assessed syllabus. Confusing the two is the most expensive common mistake in this market. |
| **The levels** | Lower secondary (Sec 1–2 general science), upper secondary (Sec 3–4 by subject), O-Level, N(A)-Level, Integrated Programme (IP), IB/IGCSE for international schools. | Ask which one the class is actually built for; "secondary" on a website can mean any of them. |

| **Practical assessment** | The laboratory-based paper in the science subjects. Currently a formal, centrally set practical examination paper (Paper 3 for 6092; Paper 5 for the combined sciences — see §4). | Class-based centres can prepare for it; they do not conduct it. |
| **Enrichment vs remedial** | Enrichment: ahead-of-school content, breadth, competition-style work. Remedial: closing gaps, re-teaching, confidence. | A centre built for one is frequently a poor fit for the other, and vice versa. |
| **Franchise vs independent** | Franchise: multiple outlets under one brand, a shared curriculum and fee schedule, locally operated. Independent: one or a few outlets, owner-operated, variable by site. | With a franchise you are buying the brand's system plus one site's delivery quality. Verify the site, not just the brand. |

## 2. The Market

### 2.1 What can and cannot be stated

This guide could not verify a current market-size figure for private tuition at any primary source. A web query for a published Singapore tuition-market size on 2026-09-25 returned an empty result set (recorded as a tool limitation in §14, not as evidence that no figure exists).

One figure circulates in this repository. The sibling guide `education/sg_secondary_tutors_guide.md` (generated 2026-06-08) opens with: "Singapore's private tutoring market is estimated at **$1.4 billion annually**", and reports "Over 60% of Singaporean secondary students receive some form of private tuition".

**Treat both as follows:** they are figures reported in the sibling guide, and **no source is cited there for either**. They are carried here only as a report of what that guide says, they are labelled as reported estimates with no traceable origin, and this guide does not use them as the basis for any conclusion. If you need a market figure for a decision, that is a signal that the decision is the wrong shape — no parent chooses a Chemistry class on the basis of a national aggregate.

### 2.2 The shape of the market, described structurally

Rather than size, describe it by structure — this is what actually affects your choice.

**Three delivery segments.**

| Segment | What is bought | Who owns the guide | Structural characteristic |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Class-based centre** | A seat in a scheduled class at fixed premises | This guide | Fixed timetable, shared materials, brand-level curriculum, site-level delivery |
| **1-to-1 tutoring** | A person's time, privately | `sg_secondary_tutors_guide.md` | Flexible schedule, per-hour pricing, quality tied to the individual |
| **Online course / platform** | Access to recorded or live-online lessons | Partly covered in the sibling guide §4 | Scale without premises; interaction is the variable |

These segments compete at the margin but are not substitutes. A centre sells a system that includes a timetable, a peer group and materials; a tutor sells attention. A child who needs attention is not buying the same thing as a child who needs a system.

**Franchise versus independent, from the parent's side.**

- **Franchise chains** exist here: a brand, a shared curriculum, a fee schedule and multiple outlets, each operated as a local business. The brand's consistency is a claim about the *system*; it is not a claim about the teacher in the room at your chosen branch. When a chain publishes results, they are chain-wide, and the branch you enrol in contributes an undisclosed fraction of them.
- **Independent centres** are typically one or a few sites, owner-operated, with the curriculum built around the teachers present. Their risk is key-person dependency; their advantage is that the person who designed the programme is often the person teaching it.
- Practical consequence: for a chain, verify the branch and the specific class; for an independent, verify what happens if the key teacher leaves.

**Seasonality.** The examination calendar is verifiable and the tuition calendar follows it. SEAB states that non-written components (science practical, oral, listening comprehension) are scheduled **before** the written examinations, and that results are released in **one common release in January** of the following year (SEAB, *Secondary Education Certificate (SEC)*; page states "Last updated 14 May 2026"). The practical paper therefore sits at a particular, early point in the examination season, not after it. Any inference that demand rises ahead of these dates is an inference about behaviour, not a verified statistic — but the calendar itself is what a class timetable has to fit, and it is worth checking a centre's schedule against it before you enrol.

**What the market does not provide.** No shared reporting standard, no accreditation of teaching, no published cohort data. This is the structural fact that shapes every other section of this guide.

## 3. The Syllabus — What Secondary Chemistry Actually Is

This is the structural section. Everything a parent should look for follows from it.

### 3.1 Chemistry is two different subjects with one name

At upper secondary level in Singapore, "Chemistry" means one of two distinct syllabi with distinct examination structures. Both are set and published by the Singapore Examinations and Assessment Board (SEAB) with Cambridge.

| | **Pure Chemistry** | **Chemistry inside a combined science** |
|---|---|---|
| **O-Level subject code** | **6092** — "Chemistry" | **5086** Science (Physics, Chemistry) · **5087** Science (Physics, Biology) · **5088** Science (Chemistry, Biology) |
| **Published title form** | The subject is named directly | The subject is named **"Science (X, Y)"**. The published syllabus does **not** call it "Combined Science" |
| **Papers** | Papers 1, 2 and 3 | Paper 1, Paper 5, plus two of Papers 2/3/4 |
| **Chemistry's weight in the subject** | The whole subject | One section of a two-science subject; Paper 3 is the Chemistry paper |
| **Practical** | Paper 3 Practical | Paper 5 Practical Test (Physics + Chemistry for 5086; Chemistry + Biology for 5088) |
| **Core reference** | SEAB/Cambridge, *Chemistry (Syllabus 6092)*, https://isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg/334/19dfb9f9-b3ce-4609-a3cf-5d30052317f5/6092_y26_sy.pdf | SEAB/Cambridge, *Science, Syllabus codes 5086 / 5087 / 5088*, https://isomer-user-content.by.gov.sg/334/a67fa90c-d3a3-4cb4-8c92-4f88346b5051/5086_y26_sy.pdf |

Both documents were read for this guide on **2026-09-25** (SEAB's O-Level syllabus listing page states "Last updated 29 April 2026").

**Why this governs everything.** A class built for 6092 and a class built for 5086 are not the same product. They differ in topic depth, in the number of papers, in the duration and weighting of each paper, and in the practical paper. A child enrolled in the wrong one spends a year being taught content that is not examined at the depth being taught, or not taught content that is. **Matching the class to the exact subject code is the first question to ask a centre, and the first thing to check on a published timetable.** §11 treats this as the most expensive common error in the market.

### 3.2 The topic map for the pure subject (6092)

The published content structure is three strands and twelve topics. Quoted exactly as published (Source B, *Chemistry (Syllabus 6092)*, "Content Structure"):

| Section | Topics |
|---|---|
| **I. Matter – Structures and Properties** | 1. Experimental Chemistry · 2. The Particulate Nature of Matter · 3. Chemical Bonding and Structure |
| **II. Chemical Reactions** | 4. Chemical Calculations · 5. Acid-Base Chemistry · 6. Qualitative Analysis · 7. Redox Chemistry · 8. Patterns in the Periodic Table · 9. Chemical Energetics · 10. Rate of Reactions |
| **III. Chemistry in a Sustainable World** | 11. Organic Chemistry · 12. Maintaining Air Quality |

This is the skeleton. A centre's published curriculum, its term plan and its materials should map onto it visibly. If a centre cannot show you a topic-to-term map, you have learned something useful.

### 3.3 The topic map for the combined science

The Chemistry Section of the combined syllabus (Source C) is published with the **same three strand headings and the same twelve topic headings (1–12)** as the pure subject. Verified at Source C, "Chemistry Section" → "Content Structure", read 2026-09-25. The subject content under each topic is condensed relative to 6092 — the document publishes a shorter treatment for each heading, not the full-depth one.

Do not generalise beyond this. The published document's *own* structure is the same twelve headings; how much content each one carries differs, and the paper it is examined in differs (see §4). If a centre tells you the two syllabi are "the same content", that is demonstrably not what the published documents say.

### 3.4 The levels and the exam path

**Sec 1–2 (lower secondary).** Students take general Science, not separate chemistry. Any Chemistry class at this level is either enrichment ahead of the syllabus or general-science support in all but name. Ask which.

**Sec 3–4 (upper secondary).** The subject choice is made at or before Sec 3. From this point a student is in either the pure subject or a combined science, and the tuition must match.

**O-Level / N(A)-Level.** The 2026 examinations are GCE O-Level (codes above) and GCE N(A)-Level. At N(A)-Level the combined-science pairings that contain Chemistry carry the pre-2027 reference codes 5105 (Science (Physics, Chemistry)) and 5107 (Science (Chemistry, Biology)); the third pairing, 5106, is Science (Physics, Biology) and contains no Chemistry. The corresponding 2027 SEC codes are in the table below. A Sec 4 N(A) student and a Sec 4 Express student are preparing for different papers — and an N(A) student taking 5105 or 5107 sits a different combined-science paper from an Express student taking 5086 or 5088.

**Integrated Programme (IP).** IP students do not sit O-Level. Their Chemistry is school-designed, progresses faster, and leads to A-Level or IB. A centre's O-Level class is not an IP class; ask specifically and look for a distinct IP timetable, as several centres publish (§7).

**From 2027: the SEC changeover.** SEAB states that in 2027 the Singapore-Cambridge GCE N(T)-, N(A)- and O-Level examinations are combined and renamed the **Singapore-Cambridge Secondary Education Certificate (SEC)**, in line with Full Subject-Based Banding, with students sitting subjects at G1/G2/G3. The SEC is jointly examined and awarded by SEAB, MOE Singapore and Cambridge International Education, UK. SEAB states: "The mode of assessment and overall examination standards for the SEC will be the same as the GCE N(A), N(T) and O-Level examinations." Grades: G1 A–E; G2 1–6; G3 A1–E8/9 (SEAB, *Secondary Education Certificate (SEC)*; page states "Last updated 14 May 2026"; verified 2026-09-25).

The new and old codes, as published in the same SEAB tables (pages state "Last updated 29 April 2026"):

| Level | Subject (2027 title) | 2027 code | Pre-2027 code |
|---|---|---|---|
| **G3** | Chemistry | **K324** | 6092 |
| **G3** | Science (Physics, Chemistry) | **K326** | 5086 |
| **G3** | Science (Physics, Biology) | **K327** | 5087 |
| **G3** | Science (Chemistry, Biology) | **K328** | 5088 |
| **G3** | Physics | K323 | 6091 |
| **G3** | Biology | K325 | 6093 |
| **G2** | Science (Physics, Chemistry) | **K223** | 5105 |
| **G2** | Science (Physics, Biology) | K224 | 5106 |
| **G2** | Science (Chemistry, Biology) | K225 | 5107 |

*(G3 from "(2027 SEC) G3 Syllabuses for School Candidates"; G2 from "(2027 SEC) G2 Syllabuses for School Candidates".)*

**On G1:** the G1 syllabus page was read directly for this guide on 2026-09-25 (page states "Last updated 29 April 2026"). It lists, among Local Subjects, a single subject titled **Science** with 2027 code **K123** (reference code for 2026 and earlier: 5148). Its Cambridge Subjects table lists no Chemistry. **There is therefore no separate G1 Chemistry code in the published G1 tables, and none should be assumed.** A G1 student at that level is taking a single Science subject.

**Practical consequence for 2026 enrolments.** A Sec 1 or Sec 2 child in 2026 will face the SEC in 2028/2029; a Sec 3 child in 2026 will face it in 2027. SEAB states the mode of assessment and overall standards will be the same, so the content and skills a good class teaches do not change — but the *name and level structure* on the exam entry change, and a centre that cannot explain what its programme maps to after the change has not thought about it. This is a question to ask, not a reason to panic.

### 3.5 The practical-assessment dimension

Both subject forms contain a hands-on assessment, and both are examined as a formal, centrally set practical paper:

- **6092:** Paper 3, Practical, 1 h 50 min, 40 marks, weighting 20%.
- **5086 / 5087 / 5088:** Paper 5, Practical Test, 1 h 30 min, 30 marks, weighting 15.0%. The syllabus states plainly: this assessment "is provided in Paper 5, as a formal practical test".

Verified at Sources B and C, read 2026-09-25. The practical is therefore a real, marked, timed examination component with a published weighting — not a school-based exercise a centre can run on the school's behalf. §4 sets out what follows from that for tuition.

**A history note, flagged.** Singapore previously used a **School-based Science Practical Assessment (SPA)** for O-Level science subjects. Secondary sources associate SPA with O-Level science subject codes 5059 (Physics), 5073 (Chemistry) and 5158 (Biology), date its introduction to 2006, and date its phase-out to the 2018 syllabus revision, when it was replaced by a one-time practical examination. **This history is based on secondary sources only and is flagged — it was not read off a current SEAB publication.** It is included solely so that a parent who finds an old reference to SPA knows what it is. SPA is **not** the current mechanism; the current mechanism is the formal practical paper described above and verified at Sources B and C.

## 4. The Assessment Structure

### 4.1 The pure subject — Chemistry 6092

Quoted from Source B, "Scheme of Assessment": *"Candidates are required to enter for Papers 1, 2 and 3."*

| Paper | Type of paper | Duration | Marks | Weighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Multiple Choice | 1 h | 40 | **30%** |
| **2** | Structured and Free Response | 1 h 45 min | 80 | **50%** |
| **3** | Practical | 1 h 50 min | 40 | **20%** |

**Paper 1** consists of 40 compulsory multiple choice items; a copy of the Periodic Table of Elements is printed as part of the paper.

**Paper 2** has two sections. **Section A** carries 70 marks and consists of a variable number of compulsory structured questions; the last two questions carry 20 marks, one of which is a data-based question requiring candidates to interpret, evaluate or solve problems using a stem of information, and that data-based question carries **8–12 marks**. **Section B** carries 10 marks and consists of two questions, of which candidates answer **only one**. A copy of the Periodic Table is printed as part of the paper.

**Paper 3** consists of a variable number of compulsory practical questions. One or more may incorporate assessment of Planning and require candidates to apply and integrate knowledge from different sections of the syllabus. The assessment of PDO and ACE may include questions on data-analysis that do not require practical equipment and apparatus. Candidates are **not allowed to refer to notebooks, textbooks or any other information** during the assessment. **A copy of the Notes for Qualitative Analysis is printed as part of this paper.**

**Practical skill weightings:** Planning (P) is weighted **15%**; the skill areas Manipulation, measurement and observation (MMO), Presentation of data and observations (PDO) and Analysis, conclusions and evaluation (ACE) are weighted **85%**.

All of the above is Source B, read 2026-09-25.

### 4.2 The combined sciences — 5086 / 5087 / 5088

Quoted from Source C, "Scheme of Assessment": *"Candidates are required to enter for Paper 1, Paper 5 and two of Papers 2, 3 and 4."*

| Paper | Type of paper | Duration | Marks | Weighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Multiple Choice | 1 h | 40 | **20.0%** |
| **2** | Structured and Free Response (Physics) | 1 h 15 min | 65 | **32.5%** |
| **3** | Structured and Free Response (Chemistry) | 1 h 15 min | 65 | **32.5%** |
| **4** | Structured and Free Response (Biology) | 1 h 15 min | 65 | **32.5%** |
| **5** | Practical Test | 1 h 30 min | 30 | **15.0%** |

**Paper assignments, exactly as published:**

- **Science (Physics, Chemistry), 5086:** Paper 1 covers the Physics and Chemistry sections; Paper 2 Physics; **Paper 3 Chemistry**; Paper 5 Physics and Chemistry.
- **Science (Physics, Biology), 5087:** Paper 1 covers Physics and Biology; Paper 2 Physics; Paper 4 Biology; Paper 5 Physics and Biology.
- **Science (Chemistry, Biology), 5088:** Paper 1 covers Chemistry and Biology; **Paper 3 Chemistry**; Paper 4 Biology; Paper 5 Chemistry and Biology.

Paper 1 consists of 40 compulsory multiple choice questions providing **approximately equal coverage of the two appropriate sections** of the syllabus.

**Practical:** the syllabus states that the assessment of practical work and experimental skills "is provided in Paper 5, as a formal practical test". Candidates are not allowed to refer to notebooks, textbooks or any other information in the practical examination, and **the Notes for Qualitative Analysis are included in the question paper** for candidates' use in the examination.

All of the above is Source C, read 2026-09-25.

### 4.3 What this means for a class-based purchase

Map the components against what a class can and cannot do.

| Component | Weight (6092) | Weight (combined, Chemistry paper) | Can a class help? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 MCQ | 30% | 20.0% (half of Paper 1) | **Yes, fully.** Drillable, markable, diagnostic. |
| Paper 2 structured / free response | 50% | 32.5% (Paper 3) | **Yes, fully.** The bulk of a good class's value. |
| Paper 2 Section B choice question | inside the 50% | n/a | Yes — but only if the class actually rehearses *choosing*. |
| Data-based question (8–12 marks) | inside the 50% | included in the structured papers | Yes — requires practised interpretation, not recall. |
| Practical paper | **20%** | **15.0% (Paper 5)** | **Partly, and structurally not entirely.** |

**The honest entry in that last row.** A class prepares for the practical; it does not sit it. Three things follow:

1. A centre can legitimately teach practical technique, qualitative-analysis reasoning, titration method, observation recording, error analysis and planning — all of it against the published skill areas (P 15%, MMO/PDO/ACE 85% for 6092). That is real, teachable value.
2. A centre **cannot** perform the examination for the student, cannot know the paper, and cannot guarantee the outcome. Any centre that presents practical preparation as a guarantee of the practical mark is overreaching.
3. Where a centre has a laboratory, the difference it makes is *exposure to equipment and procedure*. That is worth something in a syllabus whose practical is 15–20% and whose candidates are not permitted reference material in the examination. Whether it is worth a particular premium is a judgment only you can make — and §7 records which centres publish a laboratory and which do not.

**The weighting arithmetic a parent should do once.** Pure Chemistry's Paper 3 is 20% and the combined Paper 5 is 15.0%. So between four-fifths and six-sevenths of the outcome rides on the written papers, which is exactly what the majority of class time is spent on. If a centre's marketing leads with the practical and says nothing about the structured-paper work, read that as a positioning choice rather than a curriculum.

## 5. What a Class-Based Centre Actually Sells

### 5.1 The bundle

When you enrol at a centre, you are buying six things at once. Only one of them is teaching, and the other five are where most surprises live.

| Component | What it is | What to establish before paying |
|---|---|---|
| **A timetable slot** | A recurring weekly slot, tied to a term or academic-year calendar | Whether the slot survives school events, CCA seasons and exam periods |
| **A peer group** | The other students in the room | Whether the class is banded by ability or by level only — and how big it is |
| **Curriculum and materials** | Notes, worksheets, practice papers, in-house books, online portals | Whether the material maps to the *exact* subject code and is dated |
| **Teaching** | The person in the room, week after week | Who it is, what happens if they leave, and whether the same person teaches the whole year |
| **An assessment cycle** | Tests, marked work, reports, parent updates | How often, marked by whom, and whether you actually receive the marking |
| **Administrative terms** | Registration, deposit, make-up, refund, withdrawal | The written terms — not the verbal ones |

### 5.2 Class-size tiers and the ratio question

Class size is the component most often described vaguely and the one that most changes what you get. There is no industry standard and no regulator's definition, so treat published numbers as claims and verify them by attendance.

- **Small-group (roughly 4–8).** Individual work can plausibly be checked each lesson. Where a centre publishes a hard cap in this band, that cap is the most useful single number on its website.
- **Boutique (roughly 8–15).** Teacher-led, with interaction, but marking and individual diagnosis depend on the centre's out-of-class systems.
- **Large-group / lecture-style (15–30+).** Efficient content delivery; individual diagnosis is minimal. This can still be the right purchase for a confident student who needs syllabus coverage and exam technique rather than attention.

**The ratio you should reason about is not students-per-teacher, it is minutes-of-individual-feedback-per-student.** A class of 20 with a fortnightly marked script and detailed written comments can beat a class of 6 where nothing is ever collected and marked. Ask what is marked, how often, and what comes back to you.

### 5.3 Curriculum and materials

An O-Level Chemistry programme is a two-year sequence with a practical component. So the materials question is concrete:

- Is there a **topic-to-term map** covering all twelve published topics? (§3.2)
- Are the notes **dated**, and do they reflect the current syllabus and the 2027 SEC change? (§3.4)
- Are there **past-paper or prelim-paper sets**, and are they marked or just distributed?
- Is there a **homework load** with a marking commitment, or worksheets that are never collected?
- For the practical: is there a **laboratory**, and are practical sessions part of the fee or an add-on?

Materials volume is not quality. Two hundred pages of photocopied past papers is a photocopier, not a curriculum. Conversely, a single teacher with a coherent two-year plan and a marked weekly practice paper can outperform a branded workbook.

### 5.4 The assessment cycle, and why it is the centre's real differentiator

A class has one structural advantage over a private tutor: it can generate **cohort data**. It can set the same test to twelve students and show you where your child sits, which topics the class is weak on, and how the class's performance moves over a year. That is genuine progression evidence and it is a legitimate thing to ask for.

What a class cannot do is produce *certified* results. Any cohort statistic is published by the seller, computed by the seller, from a cohort the seller selected, with a denominator the seller chooses. §8 and §11 deal with the consequences. What you want from the assessment cycle is not a headline number but a mechanism: how often your child is assessed, what comes home, and what changes as a result.

### 5.5 Make-up classes, reporting and the terms that matter

These are the operational terms that decide whether a year at a centre is smooth or corrosive.

- **Make-up practice.** Illness and school events happen. Ask whether missed lessons can be made up, in the same week or not, in any branch or only yours, by recording, or not at all. Terms vary widely and some centres publish them in detail (see §7.15).
- **Parent reporting.** How frequently, in what form, and whether it is a generic progress note or an actual diagnostic with topic-level detail.
- **Withdrawal and refund.** Written notice period, deposit refundability, remaining-term fees. Get this in writing before the first payment.
- **Fee escalation.** Whether the rate is fixed for the year or resets at a term or level boundary.

### 5.6 The honest note about a service with variable delivery

A class-based centre sells a **service delivered by a person on a schedule**, under a brand. The brand is fixed; the delivery is not. Two families at the same centre in the same year, in two different classes taught by two different teachers, can have justified opposite opinions of the same business. That is not a scandal, it is the structure of the product — and it is why §9 exists and why §11 is about symptoms rather than villains. The correct posture is not "is this centre good?" but "is this specific class, taught by this specific teacher, on this specific timetable, right for this child, and what happens if the answer turns out to be no?"

## 6. The Chemistry-Specialist Question

### 6.1 What specialisation structurally buys

A single-subject chemistry centre concentrates everything on one syllabus. Structurally, that tends to produce:

- **Deeper subject expertise concentration.** The people teaching are there because of the subject; the materials, question banks and error analysis are subject-specific rather than shared across six subjects.
- **Practical facilities as a design premise.** Several centres profiled in §7 publish an in-house laboratory as their distinguishing facility, which is a direct response to the 15–20% practical weighting in §4. A general-purpose centre that also runs English classes is less likely to have built one.
- **Syllabus-form granularity.** A chemistry specialist is more likely to run *separate* classes for the pure subject and for the combined-science chemistry component, because for them the distinction is the business. §3.1 is the distinction that matters most.
- **Concentration of cohort data.** A chemistry-only centre sees more chemistry students per year than a general centre of the same size, so its own tracking can be more finely sliced (by topic, by paper, by school).

### 6.2 What a general centre structurally has instead

The counterweight is real, and it is not a consolation prize:

- **A strong chemistry teacher inside a general centre may be equivalent or better than a specialist's teacher.** Specialisation is a property of the *business*, not of the individual teaching your child. Nothing about a chemistry-only brand guarantees that the person in the room is better than the chemistry teacher two MRT stops away in a multi-subject centre.
- **Multi-subject convenience.** If the child also needs mathematics or another science, one centre with one timetable and one set of terms is materially easier on a family than three. That convenience is a legitimate benefit with a real time cost saved.
- **Larger pooling of teaching staff.** A larger centre can absorb a teacher's departure by moving a class to another qualified colleague; a one-teacher specialist may not be able to.
- **Broader level coverage.** General centres frequently run lower-secondary science and the full range of upper-secondary sciences, which suits a Sec 2 student who has not yet chosen subjects.

### 6.3 How to use this section without misusing it

This is a section about **structures, not centres**. It would be a mistake to read §6.1 as an argument for specialists and §6.2 as an argument for generalists. The two lists are the same shape: concentration versus breadth. Which one your child needs depends on the diagnosed need (§9.1, §12), not on either list being superior.

Three practical tests apply to both kinds of centre, and neither kind is exempt:

1. Ask to be told the **exact subject form** of the class you would be joining, and check it against §3.1.
2. Ask **who** teaches it and what happens if that person leaves — a question that hurts specialists and generalists alike.
3. Ask to see **marked work** from a student at your child's level. That request is a better filter than any claim about specialisation. A centre that cannot put marked scripts in front of you has told you what its marking looks like.

**No centre named in §7 is assessed here, favourably or unfavourably, on whether it is a specialist.** §7 records only what each publishes.

## 8. The Evidence Problem

### 8.1 What cannot be known, and why

| Cannot be known | Why |
|---|---|
| **Which centre is "better"** | No independent assessment exists for this service type. No inspection, no published findings, no comparative metric. |
| **Whether a results claim is true** | Claims are self-published from self-selected cohorts. No denominator, no method, no external audit. A claim of "90% A/B" could mean 90% of students who finished, 90% of those who took the paper the centre counts, or 90% of nine students. |
| **Whether a teacher is good** | Teaching quality is not certified by anyone for tuition. "Ex-MOE" describes a former employer, not a standard of instruction. |
| **Whether a class is the size claimed** | Class-size statements are marketing until counted. Some centres publish a hard cap; most publish nothing. |
| **How a given class this year will compare to last year** | The teacher, the cohort and the materials change. Past classes are not a sample of future ones. |
| **Whether a centre's materials match the current syllabus** | You can check whether materials are *dated* and whether they map onto §3.2's twelve topics, but you cannot audit what a class actually uses week to week without sitting in. |

### 8.2 An important distinction: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence

Because the assessment layer does not exist, a centre cannot be *blamed* for the absence of independent verification — no centre has it. The result is not "these centres are bad". The result is that **comparison between centres on quality is impossible, and comparison must therefore be done on attributes that are actually observable.** That is a change in method, not a verdict.

### 8.3 What CAN be verified — and why these are the right attributes to compare

| Verifiable attribute | How to verify it | Why it is worth comparing |
|---|---|---|
| **Existence and location** | Visit; the centre's own published address | You can check it exists and reach it. Every profile in §7 was verified for existence. |
| **Subject coverage and form** | The centre's own timetable and programme list | Whether it teaches **6092**, a combined science, or both, and at which levels. This is the §3.1 match, and it is fully checkable. |
| **Level and stream** | Its published timetable | O-Level, IP, N(A), lower secondary, G1/G2/G3. A class for the wrong stream is useless regardless of quality. |
| **Delivery format and lesson length** | Its published schedule | Whether you are buying a weekly class of a stated length, and whether make-up exists. |
| **Published fees and charges** | Its own fee page, in writing | Four of the centres in §7 publish these; the rest do not. Where they are published you can compute an annual cost exactly. |
| **Published class-size cap** | Its own material; then count at the trial | Where a hard cap is published ("maximum 6", "Max 8"), it is checkable at the lesson. |
| **Materials, if dated and mapped** | Ask to see the current notes and a topic-to-term plan | You can check the twelve-topic map of §3.2 against a real document. |
| **Syllabus currency and the SEC changeover** | Ask directly; see §3.4 | A centre that has an answer about 2027 is a centre that reads the syllabus. |
| **Terms: refund, withdrawal, deposit, make-up** | The written terms, before payment | Enforceable, comparable and often decisive. |

### 8.4 Three specific traps in published claims

1. **Self-bestowed authority.** Rankings and superlatives on a centre's own material — "No. 1", "the only private centre in SG with…", "Singapore's only specialist offering…" — are the centre's self-description. This guide does not repeat superlatives about named centres; where it notes that one exists, it attributes it. A self-description has no evidentiary force whatever.
2. **Mutually incompatible exclusivity.** Three centres profiled here publish assertions of this kind about laboratory provision. As published, the absolute versions cannot all be true. This guide does not adjudicate and takes no position — but a parent can safely conclude that a claim of this shape tells you nothing you can use to choose between centres.
3. **Review counts and star ratings.** Numbers like "1,156+ Verified Reviews" or "5.0 (588 reviews)" are platform metrics. They measure who asks for reviews and how, not teaching. Where a commercial directory states its own method — SG DSA states it ranks "using Google rating, review volume and website popularity" (verified 2026-09-25) — you are being told, accurately, what is being measured. It is not quality, and the word "Best" in the page title is a page title.

### 8.5 The working conclusion

You will not get a quality answer. You can get a **fit** answer, and fit is decided on the attributes in §8.3, tested with the criteria and questions in §9. That is the entire basis on which a defensible choice can be made in this market.

## 9. The Evaluation Framework

This is the guide's actual answer. Nine named criteria, each with what good looks like and how to test it. Score nothing; use it to eliminate options.

### 9.1 Syllabus and level match — *the most common expensive mistake*

**What good looks like.** The centre, unprompted, states the exact subject form and level: "Sec 3 pure chemistry, 6092" or "Sec 4 combined, 5088". The class timetable shows the form as a separate class rather than folding pure and combined students together.

**How to test it.** Say your child's subject exactly as it appears on their subject list, and ask which class they would join. Then ask what happens if a pure-chemistry student and a combined-science student need the same topic: if the answer is "same class", the pace will be set by one and wasted by the other. Check the timetable against §3.1.

**Failure mode.** Enrolling a combined-science student in a pure-chemistry class "for depth", or the reverse "for exam focus". Both waste the year.

### 9.2 Class-size reality versus the class-size claim

**What good looks like.** A published hard cap in the small-group band, restated verbally, and confirmed by an actual head-count at the trial. Published caps exist — §7 records "maximum 6" and "Max 8" at two centres.

**How to test it.** Ask for the cap, ask for the *current* number in the class you would join, and count at the trial. Ask what happens when the class is full: is a new class opened, or is the cap breached?

**Failure mode.** A "small group" of eighteen, discovered in week one after the deposit is paid.

### 9.3 Teacher subject depth and continuity — the churn question

**What good looks like.** The centre names the teacher for your slot before you enrol, commits in writing to that person for the academic year or term, and has a stated plan if they leave (a named substitute with subject experience, or a pro-rata refund).

**How to test it.** Ask: "Who teaches this slot, and will it be the same person in term 4?" Ask: "What happened the last time a Chemistry teacher left?" Ask whether the teacher teaches only Chemistry or also unrelated subjects at the same level.

**Failure mode.** A brand-quality sales conversation followed by a sequence of relief teachers. Note that this risk is *not* exclusive to chains — a single-teacher independent centre has no cover at all if that teacher goes.

### 9.4 Materials and marking load

**What good looks like.** Dated, syllabus-mapped notes with the twelve §3.2 topics visible in the contents; a weekly practice paper that is **collected and marked**; a stated turnaround.

**How to test it.** Ask to see the current year's notes and one marked script with a named student redacted. Ask how many pieces of written work are marked per month and who marks them.

**Failure mode.** A beautiful workbook and no marking. Volume is not feedback.

### 9.5 Progression evidence the centre can actually show

**What good looks like.** A diagnostic on entry; a repeated assessment during the year; topic-level reporting that tells you *which* topics are weak; and the ability to show you a real trend for a real class, anonymised.

**How to test it.** Ask: "Show me the report a parent receives, and tell me what I will learn from it that I don't already know from school." Ask whether the centre tests the same paper across classes so the data means something.

**Failure mode.** A single headline percentage that cannot be traced to a method. See §8.1.

### 9.6 The practical-assessment position

**What good looks like.** A clear statement of what the centre does for the 20%/15.0% practical: is there a laboratory or a workshop arrangement; does it cover qualitative analysis, titration and planning; is it included in the fee or charged separately; and how many practical sessions a year.

**How to test it.** Ask the question in exactly those terms. Compare the answer to §4.1–4.2: a good answer mentions observation recording and error analysis, not just "we do experiments".

**Failure mode.** Practical marketing with no practical access, or practical access priced as an unattached extra.

### 9.7 Make-up and refund terms

**What good looks like.** Written terms covering: notice required for a make-up; whether make-up is same-week only; whether it can be done at another branch or by recording; the notice period for withdrawal; and whether the deposit is refundable. §7.15 records one centre's published make-up policy in full as an example of the level of detail to ask for from any centre.

**How to test it.** Ask for the terms in writing before paying anything, and read the deposit clause specifically.

**Failure mode.** Non-refundable lump-sum payment with no make-up provision — the structure that traps families who want to leave.

### 9.8 Site and schedule fit

**What good looks like.** A slot that survives your family's actual week: travel time, CCA days, and the stretch before the practical and written papers. The published calendar matters — SEAB states non-written components are scheduled before the written examinations (§2.2).

**How to test it.** Ask the centre for its term dates and its exam-season timetable; ask how many lessons will be lost to public holidays and school events and whether they are replaced.

**Failure mode.** A "perfect" class at an impossible time on the far side of the island, attended for six weeks.

### 9.9 What you can inspect at a trial lesson

Trials and open lessons exist in this market, including free ones published by some centres (§7) and paid ones by others. If you attend one, inspect rather than watch:

| Inspect | What you are looking for |
|---|---|
| **The lesson plan or materials for that day** | Topic mapped to the syllabus; dated; ahead of the class's actual school pace |
| **The marked work of neighbours** | Whether the class's work is being collected and returned with comments |
| **The head-count** | Whether the published cap is real |
| **The board and the questioning** | Whether students are asked to explain reasoning, or only given answers |
| **The other students** | Whether the class is banded to your child's level, or a mixed-ability group where your child will either coast or drown |
| **The pace and the note-taking load** | Whether a full lesson yields notes plus practice, or dictation |
| **A question you already know the answer to** | Whether the teacher handles "why" and "what if" competently |

### 9.10 The questions to put to the centre

Ask these verbatim and write down the answers. Inconsistency between the sales answer and the written answer is itself information.

1. Which exact class would my child join — subject code, level, form (pure or combined), and stream (O-Level or IP)?
2. How many students are in that class right now, and what is the maximum?
3. Who teaches it, will it be the same person all year, and what happens if they leave?
4. What is the fee, per term and per month, and what extra charges exist — registration, deposit, materials, lab, exam-season intensives?
5. What is included in the fee and what is billed separately?
6. How many pieces of written work are marked per month, by whom, and what reaches me?
7. What do you do for the practical paper, how many sessions, and is it included?
8. What are the make-up, withdrawal and refund terms, in writing?
9. What does my child's progression report look like, and how is it produced?
10. How does your 2027 (SEC) programme differ from your 2026 programme, and where does my child's level map to after the change?
11. What happens if the class turns out to be the wrong level for my child after four weeks?
12. Can I see a marked script from a student at my child's level?

### 9.11 How to use the framework

Apply §9.1 and §9.7 as pass/fail — a wrong subject form or a non-refundable lock-in eliminates a centre outright, whatever else is good. §9.2–9.6 and §9.8 are comparative; use them to choose between the survivors. §9.9–9.10 are the mechanism. Never let a headline result claim move an option up the list.

### 10.2 Cost by model — and why the sibling guide owns half of this

The 1-to-1 baseline is **not** re-derived here. `sg_secondary_tutors_guide.md` §5 owns it, including hourly bands by tutor qualification tier (graduate $40–80/hr, full-time professional $50–100/hr, MOE/NIE teacher $80–150/hr at the time that guide was written, mid-2026, on its own stated basis of no cited source) and monthly estimates for one to three subjects. Read that section for the tutor comparison; this guide will not restate figures it cannot verify and does not own.

What can be said structurally:

| Model | Unit of purchase | What the money buys | Structural cost behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Class-based centre** | Seat, per month or term | Shared teaching time plus materials, timetable, peer group and marking systems | Cost per hour falls with class size; the discount is bought with individual attention |
| **1-to-1 tutoring** | Tutor's hour | Exclusive attention, flexible schedule, no materials system | Cost per hour is highest; the premium buys diagnosis and pace control |
| **Online course / platform** | Subscription or per-module | Recorded or live-online teaching at scale | Lowest cost per hour; the discount is bought with interaction and accountability |

### 10.3 Why cost per hour is not the right comparison unit

Cost per hour compares the *price of a seat* across products that deliver different amounts of attention. The correct question is:

> **What does a month of this arrangement cost, and what specifically does my child get that they are not getting now?**

That reframing changes decisions. A $40/hour class of twenty that never collects homework and a $40/hour class of six that marks weekly are not the same product at the same price, and the arithmetic above cannot tell them apart. The things that separate them — marking load, class cap, teacher continuity, progression reporting — are in §9, not in §10.

**Three cost rules worth writing down.**

1. **Compare annual cost, all in:** monthly fee × months, plus registration, deposit, materials and any compulsory intensives. Ask for the total.
2. **Never buy on the discount.** Bundle, sibling and referral discounts are real money, but the decision order is fit first, price second. A 20% discount on the wrong class is a 100% loss on the fee.
3. **Price the recovery.** If you leave in week six, what is recoverable? That number belongs in the comparison.

## 11. The Red Flags

Seven patterns. Each is presented as a **what to look for**, with why it is a problem and what to ask instead. None of these is an accusation against any named centre in this guide.

### 11.1 The guarantee claim

**What to look for.** A published "guarantee" attached to a grade, a distinction, or an improvement — for example a "Grade Improvement Guarantee", a "fast grade boosts guaranteed", or a claim that a course "gets 100% of students A1–B3". **Why it is a problem.** Nobody can guarantee an examination outcome: the candidate sits the paper, the paper changes, the cohort changes. A guarantee claim is either marketing with no mechanism, or a promise with conditions buried in terms that make it unenforceable — and it signals something about how the centre thinks about evidence. **What to ask instead.** "What exactly is guaranteed, in writing, over what period, and what happens if it does not happen?" If the answer is "a refund of fees subject to attendance conditions", read the conditions. If the answer is a disclaimer, you have your answer.

### 11.2 The grade-improvement claim with no method

**What to look for.** A percentage or grade jump with no stated denominator: "90.3% scored A & B", "average grade jump 4.0 grades", "more than 90% ... or jump 2 grades or more yearly". **Why it is a problem.** Without a method the number is unfalsifiable and non-comparable — and §7 shows every centre in this market publishes a figure of this shape. The issue is not dishonesty; it is that the claim cannot inform a decision. **What to ask instead.** "Of how many students in the cohort, over what period, measured against what baseline, and which students are excluded from the figure?" A centre that can answer precisely is telling you something genuinely useful; a centre that cannot is telling you what the number is for.

### 11.3 Ranked-list placement presented as evidence

**What to look for.** "Top 10", "Voted best", a directory badge, a "No. 1" claim, a placement in an agency's or directory's list. **Why it is a problem.** In this market those lists are commercial products. One directory states outright that it ranks "using Google rating, review volume and website popularity"; the "Top 12" list published by a home-tuition agency is published by a business that also sells tuition matches. Paying for placement, or being ranked by review volume, is not a quality finding (§1.1, §8.4). **What to ask instead.** Nothing of the centre — the question is unanswerable. Instead, use the badge to decide what *not* to rely on, and put the §9 questions to the centre.

### 11.4 Uncapped class size

**What to look for.** An enrolment offer with no published cap, no answer when asked, or "we'll see how many sign up". Also a cap that is published but breached: "we opened one more class" is fine; "we added four seats" is not. **Why it is a problem.** Class size is the main determinant of individual attention, and it can be increased by the seller at any time after you commit. **What to ask instead.** "What is the maximum number in this class, and what happens if demand exceeds it — a new class, or more seats?" Then count at the trial (§9.2).

### 11.5 Tutor churn behind a brand

**What to look for.** A sales process about the brand rather than the teacher; no named teacher for your slot; no continuity commitment; a general answer to "what happens if the teacher leaves". **Why it is a problem.** Teaching is delivered by a person. Brand guarantees about "qualified teachers" do not survive the departure of the one your child clicked with, and chemistry teachers with strong subject depth are the scarcest input in this market. **What to ask instead.** §9.3, verbatim, and ask for it in the enrolment agreement rather than in conversation.

### 11.6 Non-refundable lock-in

**What to look for.** A large upfront payment, a non-refundable deposit with no make-up provision, withdrawal only at term boundaries, or terms supplied only after payment. **Why it is a problem.** It transfers the entire risk of a bad fit to you. Attrition in this market is normal — some classes are simply wrong for some children — and the terms determine what that costs you. **What to ask instead.** "Give me the withdrawal, refund and deposit terms in writing, and tell me exactly what I lose if we stop after six weeks." (§9.7)

### 11.7 Pressure to commit at a trial

**What to look for.** A same-day-only discount, "two seats left in this class", enrolling you for a term at the end of a trial lesson, or a fee that depends on signing before you leave the room. **Why it is a problem.** A fit decision worth a year of a child's time deserves a night's sleep and a comparison. Urgency is a sales mechanism; it is never a fit signal. Some centres publish genuine early-registration discounts with published deadlines — that is a schedule, not pressure; the difference is whether you can walk away and return. **What to ask instead.** "May I take the terms and the fee schedule home and confirm tomorrow?" A centre confident in its fit will say yes.

### 11.8 The one-line statement about adverse material

This guide found **no** regulator's finding, and no court judgment, published by the regulator or the court, concerning any centre profiled in §7. Nothing of that kind is reported here because nothing of that kind was found, and this guide does not report complaints, allegations or disputes about named centres from any other source.

## 12. The Decision Guide

Routes through the decision. **Every route ends in attributes to prioritise, never in a named centre.** The named material lives in §7 as facts; the choice is yours, and it should be made on the attributes.

### 12.1 Route by diagnosed need

| Diagnosis | What it looks like | Prioritise | Deprioritise |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Remedial** (foundation gaps) | Fails or barely passes; cannot do mole calculations; has stopped trying | §9.1 exact form match · §9.2 a small published cap · §9.3 teacher continuity · §9.4 marking (diagnosis requires marked work) · §9.5 entry diagnostic | Lecture-scale classes; enrichment framing; volume of notes |
| **Consolidation** (C/B, wants the A) | Understands content, loses marks on structure and phrasing | §9.4 marked weekly practice on structured questions · §9.5 topic-level reporting · §9.9 whether the class rehearses answer *wording* | Pure content coverage; "revision package" marketing |
| **Stretch** (A, wants distinction or IP/IB depth) | Already strong; wants harder problems and a faster pace | §9.1 but for the *right* syllabus (IP ≠ 6092) · §9.2 banding by ability · §9.3 subject depth · §9.4 harder question sets | Remedial framing; classes banded for the weakest member |

### 12.2 Route by subject combination

- **Pure Chemistry (6092).** Full-depth syllabus, Paper 3 practical at 20% (§4.1). Prioritise §9.1, §9.6 and §9.4. Look for a centre that publishes a separate pure class.
- **Combined science with chemistry (5086/5087/5088).** Chemistry is one section, with a shorter content treatment examined as Paper 3 alongside a Paper 5 practical that shares time with the other science (§4.2). Prioritise a centre that (a) publishes a **separate combined class** and (b) recognises that the chemistry is additionally examined inside Paper 1. A pure-chemistry class is a mismatch, not a bonus.
- **N(A)-Level.** Different codes today and a different examination path (§3.4). Ask which class, at which level, and whether the class is shared with Express students. Shared classes are a warning: the papers differ.
- **IP / IB / international.** Confirm a distinct IP timetable exists before assuming it. IP moves faster and is school-designed; O-Level-tuned material is not a substitute (§3.4).
- **Double or triple science.** If your child takes two or three sciences, weigh buying one class at a centre that teaches both sciences in one place and one timetable (§6.2) against buying two separate classes. The convenience is real; so is the dilution risk if one science is taught better than the other.
- **Post-2027 (SEC).** Ask what the programme maps to at G3/G2 (§3.4). The standards are stated to be unchanged; the entry structure is not.

### 12.3 Route by level

| Level in 2026 | Examination ahead | What to prioritise |
|---|---|---|
| Sec 1 | General science; SEC path begins | Broad science support or enrichment only; do not buy a "chemistry" class that is really general science |
| Sec 2 | Subject choice approaching | Confirm chemistry is a realistic choice; use the year to check whether a class fixes the foundation before Sec 3 |
| Sec 3 | SEC G3/G2 in 2027 (for chemistry as a subject) | §9.1 exact form · §9.2 cap · §9.4 weekly marked practice · §9.7 exit terms |
| Sec 4 (2026 exam) | 2026 O-Level / N(A) | §9.6 practical position is urgent · §9.4 exam-technique marking · §9.8 timetable fit around the practical |
| Sec 5 N(A) | O-Level after N(A) | Confirm the class is built for the O-Level paper, not the N(A) paper |

### 12.4 Route by budget

| Budget posture | Structural approach |
|---|---|
| **Tightest** | One subject, one class, small-group or lecture-scale; prioritise §9.4 (marking) over §9.2 (size), because unmarked work in a tiny class is worth less than marked work in a bigger one |
| **Middle** | One class plus the centre's own materials and reporting; compare all-in annual cost (§10.1) rather than the headline rate |
| **Highest** | Small-group cap in the 4–8 band with a published cap, plus lab access for the practical; the premium buys attention and practical access, and both should be verified (§9.2, §9.6) |

### 12.5 Route by location and schedule

1. Shortlist by travel time first. A weekly class is a year-long commitment of a family's week.
2. Check the published timetable against CCA days and against the examination calendar (§9.8) — the practical is scheduled before the written papers (§2.2).
3. Ask whether the same class runs at a second branch, so a move or a clash does not end the enrolment.
4. Ask what happens in the June and September holiday weeks.

## 13. The Alternatives to a Centre

### 13.1 The 1-to-1 route

Owned by `education/sg_secondary_tutors_guide.md` — read it for the agency landscape, tutor-qualification tiers, hourly rate bands and per-hour baselines. **Nothing in that market is re-derived here.** Structurally: 1-to-1 buys attention and schedule flexibility; it does not buy a published timetable, a peer cohort, a materials system or lab access. For a child whose problem is pace and confidence, it can be the correct purchase; for a child whose problem is exam-technique volume, a class that marks weekly may do more.

### 13.2 The online course model

Partly covered in the sibling guide §4. Structurally: cheapest per hour, no premises, no travel; the variable is interaction and accountability. For practical preparation (15–20% of the grade) an online course cannot supply apparatus. Use it for content and revision speed, and check whether anything is marked.

### 13.3 Self-study with school materials

Genuinely viable for a strong, self-directed student with a good school chemistry teacher. The school supplies notes, past papers and lab access; what self-study lacks is external scheduling, marking pressure and diagnosis. If your child already does past papers unprompted and asks their teacher questions after class, consider whether the money buys anything they do not already have.

### 13.4 When a centre is the wrong purchase

- **When the diagnosis is motivation, not understanding.** A class cannot fix a disengaged student; it compounds the problem by adding a weekly obligation.
- **When the practical is the actual gap and the centre has no lab, workshop or practical sessions** — you are paying for the 80–85% they already have help with.
- **When the child's school pace and the centre's pace diverge badly**, so every week is either revision of schoolwork or a preview the school never reaches.
- **When the timetable cannot survive the family's week.** Attending for six weeks then stopping costs more than not starting.
- **When you are buying a name.** §1.3.

## 16. Glossary and Closing Summary

### 16.1 Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **6092** | The SEAB/Cambridge O-Level subject code for **Chemistry** as a single subject (the "pure" form). |
| **5086 / 5087 / 5088** | SEAB/Cambridge O-Level codes for the combined-science subjects, published as "Science (X, Y)": (Physics, Chemistry), (Physics, Biology), (Chemistry, Biology). |
| **ACE** | Analysis, conclusions and evaluation — practical skill areas weighted with MMO and PDO at 85% of the 6092 practical assessment (P is 15%). |
| **Class-based centre** | A business delivering scheduled, fixed-premises classes; the subject of this guide. |
| **Combined science** | A two-science O-Level subject ("Science (X, Y)") in which Chemistry is one section, examined as Paper 3 and inside Paper 1. |
| **FSBB (Full Subject-Based Banding)** | The MOE policy under which students take subjects at G1/G2/G3 levels; the reason the 2027 examinations are combined and renamed. |
| **G1 / G2 / G3** | The three subject levels in the 2027 SEC. G1 grades A–E; G2 grades 1–6; G3 grades A1–E8/9. G1 has no separate Chemistry subject. |
| **IP (Integrated Programme)** | A secondary pathway that does not sit O-Level and progresses to A-Level or IB. |
| **MMO / PDO / P** | Manipulation, measurement and observation / Presentation of data and observations / Planning — the published practical skill areas for 6092. |
| **N(A) / N(T)** | Normal (Academic) and Normal (Technical) streams; their examinations merge into the SEC in 2027. |
| **Paper 3 / Paper 5** | The Chemistry practical paper for 6092 / the combined sciences' Practical Test. |
| **Pure chemistry** | The single-subject form, code 6092. |
| **SEC** | Singapore-Cambridge Secondary Education Certificate — the combined examination replacing the N(T)-, N(A)- and O-Levels from 2027. |
| **SEAB** | Singapore Examinations and Assessment Board, which sets and publishes the syllabuses with Cambridge. |
| **SPA** | School-based Science Practical Assessment — a historical practical-assessment mechanism, **not** the current one; see §3.5. |

### 16.2 Closing summary

There is no ranking in this guide, and there cannot be. No independent assessment of tuition centres exists in Singapore; the lists you will find are commercial products, and the results claims are unaudited self-publications. What is knowable is real, though: the exact syllabus your child sits and how it is examined (§3, §4); which centres publish a class for that exact subject form, at what price, at which branches, and — where they do — with what class cap and what terms (§7); and a framework for eliminating the ones that do not fit (§9).

The method is: match the syllabus form exactly, verify what the centre actually publishes, count the heads, read a marked script, read the terms, and refuse to be moved by a badge or a percentage. Then choose the arrangement that fits this child, in this subject form, at this level, on a timetable your family can keep.

**The fit, not the ranking.**
