## 1. The Overview

### 1.1 The Short Answer

**Which Crane Capital?** Several unrelated businesses share the "Crane Capital" name — US SEC-registered advisers and funds (including a Miami-based "Crane Capital Value Fund, LP"), US and UK property and investment firms, and the unknown earlier occupants of the cranecapital.com domain — so the identity guardrail comes first. This guide profiles exactly one of them: **the Hong Kong-headquartered, Singapore-linked real-estate investment firm that operates the single-page marketing site cranecapital.com** ("© 2022 Crane Capital"), states on that site that it is "a long-term real estate investment company" that "build[s] real estate operating companies" with entrepreneur partners, and is anchored in the public registries by **three legal entities**: **Crane Capital (HK) Limited** (永瑞資本有限公司, Hong Kong, incorporated 11 March 2019, Live), **CRANE CAPITAL PTE. LTD.** (Singapore, UEN 202111353G, registered with ACRA 31 March 2021, Live), and the same-day **CRANE CAPITAL PARTNERS LLC** foreign registration (UEN T21UF2823K) — the last an overseas LLC merely registered in Singapore, with no home jurisdiction disclosed in the public snapshot. ✅ (site extraction September 2026; HK and SG registry records; §2 walks the evidence.)

The firm's self-description (cranecapital.com, extracted September 2026) is a **permanent-capital real-estate investment thesis**: with entrepreneur partners it "build[s] and grow[s] real estate businesses across Asia's leading cities", acquires "high-quality assets on a value basis", drives performance "through active operational improvements", and structures "real estate investment models that align the interests of all stakeholders" to "provide risk-adjusted returns, generate stable and growing distributions for our investor while protecting them against downside risk". It reports **"US$1.9B EQUITY UNDER MANAGEMENT"** and **"8 GLOBAL CITIES ACROSS ASIA"** — ⚠ both self-reported, with the cities not enumerated publicly — and lists offices in **Hong Kong** (22nd Floor, 33 Des Voeux Road Central, T +852 2133 8008) and **Singapore** (8 Cross Street, #24-05 Manulife Tower, T +65 6335 5200). ✅ (addresses match the registry records exactly.)

In one line each, the answers a banker most often wants: **Who are they?** A private real-estate investment firm, founded in 2019 by two Kellogg-HKUST EMBA alumni, running permanent capital in necessity real estate across Asian cities. **Are they regulated?** Not by MAS and not by the SFC, as of 03 September 2026 — verified negatives, with the caveat that "no licence found" is not "no licence needed" (§7.3). **How big?** US$1.9 billion of equity under management, self-reported, unverified by any third party, up from US$650 million in 2019 (§6.1). **Where is the money from?** Unpublished — the best public hint is a 2019 alumni-magazine statement that the firm invests "exclusively for a US pension fund" ⚠ (§6.2). **Why does the name keep colliding with other firms?** Because "Crane Capital" is used by unrelated US advisers and funds, UK property firms, and crane-services companies — the disambiguation is §2's job and §9.2's procedure.

For a bank like Cymbal Bank, Crane Capital matters on four fronts: as a **corporate-client archetype** (an Asia real-estate investment group with a Hong Kong holding entity and a Singapore group/regional entity that needs operating accounts, subscription and distribution flows, FX, and cross-border HK–SG banking — the worked example in §9), as an **identity and screening case study** (the "Crane Capital" name produces multiple false-positive screening hits, so a bank's CDD file must document the disambiguation — a distinctive and realistic KYB exercise), as a **regulatory-boundary reference** (an investment company that holds no MAS licence and no SFC licence — both verified negatives as of 03 September 2026 — showing a bank how to assess an unregulated-but-professional real-estate investor without assuming exemption), and as a **worked-example generator** for this repository's Cymbal Bank persona conventions (§9).

Two caveats keep this chain honest. **First**, the *group* structure — which entity owns which, where the ultimate beneficial owners sit, and whether the Hong Kong company or the Singapore company is the head — is **not in the public record** ⚠; the guide treats the three registrations as one economic group because brand, addresses, leadership, and the SSIC 70101 "head/regional head office" classification all point the same way, and flags that ownership itself is unverified. **Second**, the HK record is read through two registry aggregators rather than the Companies Registry's paid search, so it is marked ⚠ secondary-sourced; it is internally consistent with the website address and with Wai Tang's LinkedIn employer entry ("Crane Capital (HK) Limited").

A word on what SSIC 70101 actually denotes, since a bank's classification instincts will be triggered by it: the code covers "activities of head and regional head offices; centralised administrative offices and subsidiary management offices" — the activities of a corporate centre that oversees subsidiaries or regional operations, *not* the activities of a fund manager, an investment dealer, or a property company. That the SG entity carries this code is consistent with it being the group's regional head office and administrative centre for Asia (the address the website lists for its Singapore office), while the investing itself happens through the Hong Kong entity and the operating companies below it ⚠ (reading, not registry fact — the registry tells us the *code*, not the *activities*).

### 4.2 The Business Model — Real-Estate Operating Companies and Permanent Capital

Stripping the marketing language, the model works like this (analysis in the guide's own words, anchored to the site's claims ✅/⚠): Crane Capital sits at the top of a **two-layer structure**. In the *lower layer* sit **real estate operating companies** — operating businesses created or scaled "together with" entrepreneur partners, each running a portfolio of real-estate assets (the 2019 capture's "65 assets under management" and the photography of residential/community properties on the site — ⚠ no asset names are public). In the *upper layer*, Crane Capital acts as the **capital provider and builder**: it identifies the partners, structures the investment model, provides the equity, and then works "at every stage" of the partner business — sourcing, executing, asset-managing, and reporting. The revenue and value logic is that of an **operating-platform investor**: instead of buying buildings and flipping them, the firm builds *companies* that own and operate buildings, so value is created both by the assets (acquired "on a value basis") and by the operating improvements the platform drives — and the "stable and growing distributions" are paid from operating cash flow, not from a forced sale cycle.

Three structural features distinguish the model from a textbook closed-end real-estate fund, and all three are stated or strongly implied by the firm's own language:

- **Permanent capital.** The site's phrase is literal: capital is "permanent", provided "for the long term" and "in times of volatility" — the opposite of a 7–10 year closed-end fund with a divestment deadline (contrast: the closed-end model analysed in the [Private Equity Guide](private_equity_guide.md)).
- **Company-building, not deal-stacking.** The unit of investment is the operating company and its entrepreneur partners, not a series of discrete asset deals; distributions are "stable and growing" from operations.
- **Investor-protection framing.** "Protecting them against downside risk" and "aligning the interests of all stakeholders" imply a principal-aligned, conservatively levered posture — but these are marketing commitments, not covenant-backed promises ⚠.

**The founder's own articulation of the model.** The clearest primary statement of how the two layers relate comes from Wai Tang in the October 2019 alumni profile, and is worth holding next to the site's marketing prose: "we're not just buying real estate – in each city we're first finding the people to build a company which will specialise in one real estate sector". ✅/⚠ (verbatim from the Kellogg-HKUST EMBA alumni publication; the principals' own account, launch-period.) Read with §3.2, that sentence is the whole model in miniature: the unit of investment is the city-level operating company and its people — the sector-specialist company is built first, and the real estate is what that company then owns and runs. The 2019 "65 assets under management" were the asset layer beneath those city companies, and the site's seven-year-stable wording suggests the model itself has not changed since launch — only the self-reported equity figure has grown ⚠.

**What the model does not say.** For all its strategy prose, the firm publishes nothing about the capital stack — no debt policy, leverage ratio, target return, hold-period, fee, or carried-interest term appears anywhere on the site ❌ (not found). A bank reading the model must therefore hold two truths at once: the *income logic* (operating cash flows from necessity real estate, paying stable distributions) is clear and consistent, while the *financial engineering* (how much debt sits in the operating companies, how the group is compensated, how downside protection is actually structured) is entirely undisclosed ⚠. The worked example's account structure (§9.3) is designed around that asymmetry: the bank sees the cash flows and the collateral it holds, and never pretends to see the capital stack underneath.

### 5.2 The Strategy Style — Value-Add Real Estate With an Operating-Company Angle

Read against the repository's strategy conventions, Crane Capital's disclosed approach maps to a **value-add/opportunistic real-estate strategy executed through operating companies** (analysis in the guide's own words; site claims ✅/⚠ as flagged):

- **Value-basis acquisition.** "Acquire high-quality assets on a value basis" — buying quality assets below replacement or below their stabilised value, the classic value-add entry point, rather than core assets at market pricing.
- **Active operational improvements.** "Drive investment performance through active operational improvements" — the value-add engine: repositioning, re-leasing, densification, or operating-efficiency gains at the property or company level. This is the "add" in value-add: returns come from the operator's actions, not from market beta.
- **The operating-company layer.** The improvements are run *inside* the partner operating companies, so Crane Capital's role is part capital, part builder/board — a hybrid of financial sponsor and strategic operator. (Taxonomy cross-ref: the strategy categories in the [Hedge Fund Guide](hedge_fund_guide.md); the sponsor/operator distinction is developed in the [Private Equity Guide](private_equity_guide.md).)
- **Necessity-real-estate sector focus.** "Necessity real estate for people to live and communities to grow" — residential and community-serving assets (homes, and the retail/services that anchor communities), which are structurally less cyclical than discretionary real estate (office, luxury hospitality). ⚠ sector claim is the firm's own; no asset-level evidence is public.
- **Long-hold, permanent-capital horizon.** The firm "take[s] a long term view" and provides "stable capital in times of volatility" — an evergreen, hold-through-the-cycle posture that differs from the 7–10-year closed-end fund convention (cross-ref the [Private Equity Guide](private_equity_guide.md) on closed-end structure; the [Cayman BVI Master-Feeder](cayman_bvi_master_feeder_guide.md) on the typical fund-vehicle layer).

The style label that best fits the disclosed mix is therefore **value-add/opportunistic real estate with an operating-company and long-hold tilt** — with the honest caveat that without named assets, city exposure, leverage policy, or return targets, the strategy can only be characterised at the level of the firm's own description ⚠.

The absence is not unusual for a private mandate-driven firm — most such firms disclose performance only under NDA to their single principal — but it does mean that every strategy statement in this section is a characterisation of *intent as published*, never a claim about *outcomes as demonstrated* ⚠. The claims audit (§10) and the what-could-not-be-verified list (§11) carry that distinction through the whole guide.

### 5.3 The Fund-Structure Question — Permanent Capital Versus Closed-End

A bank analysing Crane Capital will eventually ask the structural question: is this a **fund manager** or an **investment company**? The public record supports only the second, weakly. There are no public fund vehicles under the Crane Capital name in either registry jurisdiction; no MAS-regulated fund or fund manager (§7.1); no SFC-licensed corporation (§7.2); and no Form D or other SEC fund filing for the SG foreign LLC (0 EDGAR full-text hits for "Crane Capital Partners", 03 Sep 2026 ✅). The firm's own language — permanent capital, an investment *company*, "our investor", no use of the word "fund" anywhere on the site — points to a **principal/evergreen model**: capital that is not raised into finite-life pooled vehicles with public footprints, but held and deployed on the company's own balance sheet and through its operating companies, with investor money (if third-party at all, §6) committed on a permanent or long-dated basis. ⚠ This is inference from silence plus the firm's wording; no constitutive documents are public to prove how investor capital is held. For the structural conventions a reader might expect — master-feeder, VCC, or limited-partnership wrappers, offshore or onshore — the repository's fund-structures reference is the [Cayman BVI Master-Feeder](cayman_bvi_master_feeder_guide.md) guide and, for the Singapore variable-capital-company wrapper, the [Hedge Funds Singapore](hedge_funds_singapore_guide.md) guide; neither is re-derived here because no Crane Capital vehicle exists in the public record to attach them to ❌.

Why the absence of vehicles matters to a bank: if the group one day *does* launch a pooled vehicle — a VCC in Singapore, a Cayman feeder, or an HK fund — that event changes its regulatory and banking profile overnight (the re-onboarding trigger of §9.5). The bank's relationship design should therefore treat the current "no vehicles" state as a *baseline to monitor*, not a permanent feature: the same registers that returned negatives in §7 are the tripwires, and the client's own disclosures (a new entity name, a new MAS or SFC appearance, a prospectus) are the event signals ⚠ (recommendation).

If the group ever launches a pooled vehicle, opens to new investors, or appears on a regulator register, that is a **re-onboarding event**, not a note.

### 9.6 The Lessons

Five lessons generalise from this worked example to any bank meeting a Crane-Capital-style client. **First, identity is the product:** for a name as crowded as "Crane Capital", the CDD file's disambiguation evidence (registry extracts, register negatives, dispositioned lookalikes) *is* the KYB deliverable — §2's logic made operational. **Second, unlicensed is not simple:** the verified absence of MAS/SFC licences makes the client *harder* to monitor, not easier — the bank's periodic review substitutes for a regulator's. **Third, map the money to the model:** capital-in/subscription, deployment to operating companies, distribution-out — the bank that understands the permanent-capital operating-company model can distinguish legitimate periodic distributions from red-flag-shaped ones. **Fourth, custody is scoped, not assumed:** hold the cash and collateral, never pretend to custody the buildings, and say so in the product terms. **Fifth, watch the perimeter:** a mandate-driven principal group can become a fund manager without warning — the registers and the client's own disclosures are the tripwires, and the repo's honesty convention applies to banks too: document what is verified, flag what is not, and never write "exempt" where the record only shows "absent".

Crane Capital is a study in how much — and how little — a determined researcher can verify about a private real-estate investment firm from the public record alone. The verified core is solid: a Hong Kong company (Crane Capital (HK) Limited) incorporated on 11 March 2019 and a Singapore pair of registrations (CRANE CAPITAL PTE. LTD., UEN 202111353G, and the CRANE CAPITAL PARTNERS LLC foreign registration, UEN T21UF2823K) dated 31 March 2021, whose registered addresses match the two offices on the firm's own site; a founding pair — Wai Tang and Ricky Lau, Managing Partner & CEO and Managing Partner — attested by the firm's site, Tang's LinkedIn, and the Kellogg-HKUST EMBA alumni magazine of October 2019; a marketing thesis ("We build real estate operating companies", "Permanent capital for a dynamic Asia", "necessity real estate") that has not changed a word in seven years of captures; a self-reported equity-under-management curve from US$650 million (2019) to US$1.9 billion (2026) that no third party has confirmed; and two clean regulatory negatives dated 03 September 2026 — no MAS-regulated entity under the Crane Capital name in Singapore and no SFC-licensed Crane Capital corporation or representative in Hong Kong, the only register match being an unrelated company whose name merely contains the word "crane". What could not be verified is equally clear and equally important: the current investors (the 2019 record says a single US pension fund, "our investor" in the singular), the ownership chain and UBOs, the cities and assets behind "8 global cities across Asia", the home jurisdiction of the foreign LLC, and the legal reasoning that would place the firm's activities outside any licensing perimeter — all flagged ⚠ in this guide rather than smoothed, per the repository's honesty convention.

For a bank, Crane Capital is not an exotic client — it is a *representative* one: a professionally staffed Asia real-estate investment group, unregulated but not unsophisticated, whose name collides with unrelated funds and firms in every screening database, whose money moves in the recognisable rhythms of capital calls and distributions, whose real assets will never sit in the bank's custody, and whose regulatory story is an absence rather than a licence. The worked example showed what that means in practice: the CDD file that proves *which* Crane Capital by registry extract rather than by brand, the account structure that separates group cash from project cash and investor capital, the monitoring that reads periodic distribution runs as mandate mechanics while still testing them, the periodic review that stands in for the regulator the client does not have, and the re-onboarding trigger the day the client's perimeter changes. In an industry where the giants of the last cycle were felled by identities that were never truly verified and books that were never truly read, the bank that documents before it trusts, flags what it cannot prove, and watches the registers for the moment the story changes is the bank that earns the crane's lift.
