## 1. The Overview — the Decoder, the Thesis, and the Two Boundaries

**The decoder.** Five statutes and instruments set the frame. The 个人信息保护法 (Personal Information Protection Law, PIPL) governs personal information — its definition turns on an "identified or identifiable natural person" (Article 4) and it names 敏感个人信息 (sensitive personal information) as a distinct, higher tier that includes financial accounts (Article 28). The 网络安全法 (Cybersecurity Law, CSL) is the network-layer statute: it establishes 等级保护, the multi-level protection scheme (MLPS), and the localisation and security-review duties that attach to 关键信息基础设施 (critical information infrastructure, CII) and its operators, 关键信息基础设施运营者 (CIIOs). The 数据安全法 (Data Security Law, DSL) is the data-classification statute, the source of 数据分类分级 (data classification and grading) and of the "important data" (重要数据) concept. The 网信办 (Cyberspace Administration of China, CAC) is the authority that administers 数据出境 (outbound data transfer) and issues the cross-border provisions. Around those sit the transfer mechanisms — 安全评估 (the security assessment), 标准合同 (the standard contract), and the personal-information protection certification, 认证 — and, for mail specifically, one instrument: the 互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 (Measures for the Administration of Internet Email Services), the only email-SPECIFIC rule retrieved this pass.

**The thesis.** The directory service that routes mail — 邮件路由 — is a jurisdiction-agnostic protocol, but the *mailbox behind it is not*. Once an institution operates inside China, the place where a mailbox sits, the place a message transits, and the place a message is read stop being engineering defaults and become compliance decisions, because the regime treats a 数据出境 (outbound transfer) as a regulated event rather than a background operation. The email channel is therefore not merely a mailbox a bank happens to run; it is a records system and a transfer surface at the same time, and the two roles pull in opposite directions under this regime. A programme built for a jurisdiction-neutral mail estate will optimise for availability, deliverability and search; a programme built for China will additionally have to answer where the mailbox lives, whether a message that arrives from abroad is a transfer, and what the institution has documented about both.

## 2. The Regime in Outline, Cross-Referenced Not Re-Derived

This section exists for one reason: to make the email case in §3 to §5 intelligible. It states the three instruments, the authority, and the **structure** of the transfer regime — and then stops, referring the reader to [China's Data Governance Frameworks](../technology/data/china_data_governance_frameworks.md) for the depth. No penalty figure, no threshold and no retention period is printed here; those are handled in §15.

**The three instruments, in one sentence each.** The 网络安全法 (Cybersecurity Law, CSL) was adopted on 7 November 2016 and took effect on 1 June 2017 ✅ (text read on 中国人大网); it is the network and infrastructure statute, and it is **amended**: the amended text published by CAC on 2025-12-29 states that it was revised by the decision of the 14th NPC Standing Committee, 18th session, of 28 October 2025, and the amended law is **in force from 1 January 2026** ✅ — meaning that today, in October 2026, the amended CSL is the operative version, not the 2017 original. The 数据安全法 (Data Security Law, DSL) was adopted on 10 June 2021 and took effect on 1 September 2021 ✅ (identity and dates via 中国人大网 and gov.cn President's Order No. 84; the DSL's full text was **not** opened this pass, so no DSL article is cited here — see §16). The 个人信息保护法 (PIPL) was adopted on 20 August 2021 and took effect on 1 November 2021 ✅ (NPC English translation, Article 74, read in full).

The instruments, as retrieved this pass, are these — the reader should note that two are cited with an article and one (the DSL) only by identity, for the reason given:

**The authority.** The 网信办 (Cyberspace Administration of China, CAC) is the body that coordinates cybersecurity and personal-information protection and administers 数据出境 (outbound data transfer) ✅ (PIPL Article 60 assigns overall coordination to "the national cyberspace department"; the cross-border provisions are a CAC order — CAC Order No. 16 ✅). Sector regulators operate alongside it: for banking and insurance the 国家金融监督管理总局 (National Financial Regulatory Administration, NFRA) issues the sector data rules ✅, and the People's Bank of China (中国人民银行, PBOC) issues financial-industry standards ✅. A mail estate can therefore be in front of more than one regulator at once: CAC for the transfer and the network layer, NFRA for the financial-sector data-security duties.

**The structure of the transfer regime.** PIPL Article 38 sets the framework: before a processor provides personal information to a party outside China, it must meet **one of** — (1) passing the security assessment organised by the national cyberspace department under PIPL Article 40; (2) obtaining personal-information protection **certification** from a specialised institution; (3) concluding a **standard contract** with the overseas recipient; or (4) meeting other conditions set by law or by the cyberspace department ✅. PIPL Article 39 adds the notice-and-**separate-consent** duty: the processor must inform the individual of the overseas recipient's identity, the purposes and means, the categories of data, and the methods for exercising rights *over the overseas recipient*, and must obtain the individual's separate consent ✅. PIPL Article 40 states the localisation duty for CIIOs and for processors that reach the volume set by the cyberspace department, with the security assessment as the route to transfer ✅. Articles 41 to 43 close the loop on foreign-lawyer requests, restricted-recipient lists, and countermeasures ✅. That is the shape; the three mechanisms, the 2024 reforms and the PIPIA requirement are set out in depth in the framework guide's §5.

**The 2024 instrument that reorganised the structure.** The 促进和规范数据跨境流动规定 (*Provisions on Promoting and Regulating the Cross-Border Flow of Data*, CAC Order No. 16), promulgated and effective on 22 March 2024 ✅, is the instrument that routes an exporter among the three mechanisms and carves out exemptions. Read at its own text, its structure is: **Article 3** (data with no personal information or important data is exempt), **Article 4** (overseas-collected personal information processed in China and re-exported, where no domestic personal information was introduced, is exempt), **Article 5** (four specific exemptions, including a *de minimis* volume exemption and one for cross-border human-resources management of employees' personal information under lawfully made labour rules and collective contracts), **Article 6** (free-trade-zone negative lists), **Article 7** (the cases requiring the **security assessment**), **Article 8** (the middle band requiring the **standard contract** or the **certification**), **Article 9** (the validity period of an assessment result), **Article 10** (the notice, separate-consent and PIPIA duties applying to any outbound personal information), and **Article 13** (which supersedes the earlier CAC measures where inconsistent, naming the 数据出境安全评估办法 — Measures for the Security Assessment of Data Export, CAC Order No. 11 — and the 个人信息出境标准合同办法 — Measures for the Standard Contract for Outbound Transfer of Personal Information, CAC Order No. 13) ✅. The volumes that separate those bands are **cited by article and not printed** (see §5 and §15).

**One mail-specific instrument.** Alongside the general regime sits the 互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 (*Measures for the Administration of Internet Email Services*), 信息产业部 (Ministry of Information Industry) Order No. 38, promulgated 20 February 2006 and in force 30 March 2006 ✅ (Article 27, text read on gov.cn). It is the only email-specific Chinese instrument retrieved this pass. §7 and §8 rely on it; §16 flags the limits of what could be established about its current amendment status.

**What this guide takes from the outline and what it leaves.** Taken: that there are three instruments and one coordinating authority; that outbound transfer has three mechanisms and a set of exemptions; that sector regulators add a layer; that there is an email-specific instrument on top. Left to the framework guide: the statutory history, the classification ladder, the levels of MLPS 2.0, the enforcement and penalty regime, and the comparative frame. Taken from this outline, and carried into the rest of the guide:

- The **three instruments** and the one coordinating authority (§2), which frame every later section.
- The **three transfer mechanisms** and their exemptions (§5.4), which decide what a mail estate must do.
- The **sector layer** — NFRA for banking and insurance (§9, §12) — which adds duties on top of the general regime.
- The **email-specific instrument** (MII Order No. 38), which supplies the only mail-specific duties retrieved (§7, §8).

- The statutory history of the three pillars, and their relationship to one another.

- The **enforcement and penalty regime** — cited here by article only (§15).

## 3. Why Email Is a Distinctive Case Under This Regime

This is the analytical core of the guide. Four properties of email make the China data-governance regime bite differently on a mail estate than on a database, and each one turns a technical fact into a compliance question.

**Property one — mail is unstructured.** A database column has a name, a type and a documented purpose; a mailbox does not. The personal information in an inbox sits inside prose, inside a signature block, inside a scanned attachment, inside a subject line. The classification duties that the regime imposes — 数据分类分级 (data classification and grading), and for the financial sector the three-level core/important/general scheme of the NFRA measures ✅ (Article 18, read at the instrument's own text) — assume that a data asset can be identified and labelled. A mailbox is the place where that assumption is weakest. The consequence is structural, not procedural: an institution cannot truthfully assert that its mail estate contains no personal information, and it cannot label at rest what it has not read. What it *can* do is decide where the mailbox sits and what is documented about the flow, which is why §5 and §10 carry the weight in this guide that a data-catalogue section would carry elsewhere.

**Property two — mail is interpersonal, and it carries third parties' personal information.** A message from a relationship manager contains a client's name; a forwarded chain contains everyone who was ever on it; a contact list attached to a migration contains people who have no relationship with the bank at all. The consent architecture of PIPL is built for data the processor *chose* to collect: consent, or one of the Article 13 statutory grounds, is required before processing ✅ (Article 13, read at the instrument's own text). A mailbox receives data the institution did not choose to collect, from senders who are not the institution's data subjects, about data subjects who may never have heard of the institution. The result is that the mail estate is full of personal information for which no consent artefact exists and no Article 13 ground was ever recorded — and the regime's honest answer to that is not "delete", because the same estate is a records system (see Boundary A, §1) that must be able to produce what it received. The tension is the point.

**Property three — mail is received as well as collected.** A bank's outbound communications are its own; its inbound mail is its counterparties'. The regime's transfer rules were drafted with *provision* — 向境外提供, "providing to overseas" — in view: PIPL Article 38 speaks of a processor that "provides personal information for a party outside the territory"; CAC Order No. 16 speaks of "向境外提供个人信息", providing personal information to overseas ✅. A message *arriving* from a foreign sender is not, on the face of that language, the bank "providing" anything — the foreign party initiated it. That distinction matters enormously for mail, because the mail estate is the one system where the dominant flow is inbound, and it is the reason §5 treats "is a received message a transfer?" as a structured question rather than a settled rule.

**Property four — mail is routed internationally by default, so the location of a mailbox is itself a regulated decision.** SMTP is store-and-forward across relays and MX records ✅ (the universal guide's §2 owns this and cites RFC 5321; this guide states it and moves on). That means a message between two people in the same city may traverse a third country's relay, and a message between two countries is the ordinary case, not the exception. Once the mailbox — the durable store of the message — sits in a region outside China, every message that lands in it has been transferred to that region in a form the institution controls, and every message read from it has been made accessible outside China. The regime cares about the durable, controlled copy; that is precisely what a mailbox is. So the architecture question "which region does the tenant live in?" is not a latency question in China — it is the transfer question, asked early. That framing — a mailbox's location is a regulated decision — is the thesis of this guide and the thing a jurisdiction-neutral mail programme (universal guide, §4, §7) is not built to see.

A structured-data view and a mailbox view diverge on every one of the four properties, and the table is the shortest way to show why a programme built for the first does not carry to the second:

| Property | The structured-data view | The mailbox view (and why this guide exists) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Named columns, types, documented purposes; classification is tractable | Prose, signature blocks, attachments, subjects; classification at content granularity is not tractable (§3, property one) |
| Consent | The processor chose to collect, so a consent artefact or an Article 13 ground exists | Third parties' data arrives without the institution's choice and often without any consent channel (§3, property two) |
| Direction | Outbound provision is the paradigm the transfer rules were drafted for | The dominant flow is INBOUND; receipt is not provision on the instruments' face (§3, property three) |
| Location | A record's location is written down in a catalogue | A mailbox's location is a configuration setting, and it is the transfer decision (§3, property four) |

**Two derived consequences.** First, the mail estate is a *transfer surface* and a *records obligation* simultaneously, and the two pull opposite ways: localising the estate satisfies one and can frustrate the other if the local store cannot be produced to a foreign supervisor or a foreign litigant. Second, the unit of analysis changes. A database transfer is a row count; a mail transfer is a *relationship* — "who is this mailbox's operator, where does the copy rest, and what was documented". The rest of this guide works at that unit.

**Where this sits relative to the boundaries.** None of the four properties is about SMTP mechanics — the universal guide owns those. None is about the three laws in the abstract — the framework guide owns those. Each is about what the laws do *to a mailbox*, which is this guide's subject. Stated one line each, so that the four can be carried into the rest of the guide:

- **Unstructured** → a mailbox cannot be catalogued at content granularity, so control moves to the platform and the flow (§9.3).
- **Interpersonal** → the personal information in mail is often a third party's, for whom no consent artefact exists (§4).
- **Received** → the dominant flow is inbound, and receipt is not "provision" on the instruments' face (§5.3).
- **Routed internationally by default** → the durable copy's location is the transfer decision (§5.1, §5.2).

## 4. The Personal-Information Question in Mail

**The baseline definition.** 个人信息 (personal information) under PIPL is "various information related to an identified or identifiable natural person recorded electronically or by other means", excluding anonymised information, and "processing" expressly includes collection, storage, use, transmission, provision and disclosure ✅ (Article 4, read at the NPC English translation). Everyday correspondence is squarely inside that definition: a signature block, a salutation, a mobile number, a job title attached to a name are all "related to an identified or identifiable natural person". The honest starting position for any bank mailbox is therefore that it processes personal information continuously and by nature, not incidentally. The categories that make that concrete, all ordinary in a bank inbox:

- A **signature block** — a name, a title, a mobile number, an address.
- A **salutation** with a named individual, even where the substance is operational.
- An **attachment** — a spreadsheet of contacts, a scanned identity document, a signed form.
- A **forwarded chain**, which accumulates the personal information of everyone who ever replied.
- A **distribution list's membership**, which is the personal information of people who never sent a message at all.
- A **calendar invitation or a delivery receipt**, which records a person's movements and contacts.

None of those is exceptional, and none is the institution's own collection decision in the sense a database is.

**Sensitive personal information (敏感个人信息) and separate consent.** PIPL Article 28 defines 敏感个人信息 as information that, once leaked or illegally used, may easily infringe personal dignity or endanger personal safety or property, expressly listing biometrics, religious belief, specific identity, medical health status, **financial accounts** and whereabouts, and the personal information of a minor under 14 ✅. The same article permits processing of sensitive personal information only for a specific purpose, where necessary, and with strict protective measures; Article 29 requires **separate consent** for it, and written consent where another law requires it ✅. This is not an abstraction for a bank mailbox: a client's account number pasted into a message, a medical certificate attached to a leave request, a passport scan in a KYC thread, and the personal information of a minor are all plausible mail content. The regime does not carve mail out, so a mailbox that routinely carries those categories is a processor of sensitive personal information that acquired it through the mail channel.

**Separate consent, and why it is hard in mail.** PIPL requires separate consent in several places beyond sensitive data — Article 23 (providing personal information to another processor), Article 25 (disclosing personal information), and Article 39 (providing personal information outside China each require separate consent, with Article 39 adding the notice of the overseas recipient) ✅. Article 14 makes each consent voluntary, explicit and fully informed, and requires a fresh consent on a change of purpose, means or category ✅. A mail-driven flow makes "separate consent" structurally awkward: the data subject may be a third party who never interacted with the institution (Property two, §3), so there is no consent channel to the individual at all. Where no consent can be obtained, the institution's lawful basis would have to be one of the Article 13 non-consent grounds — including "necessary for the conclusion or performance of a contract", "necessary for the performance of statutory duties or obligations", or, for employees, "human resources management in accordance with lawfully formulated labour rules and collective contracts" ✅ (Article 13(2), (3)). Whether any of those grounds reaches a *received* message from a third party is not resolved by the statute's text read this pass; §16 records that as unverified. The point for a compliance function is not that consent is always required — it is that mail routinely carries personal information for which **no consent artefact and no recorded Article 13 ground exists**, and that gap has to be documented, not ignored.

**The employee-mailbox dimension.** An employee mailbox is where business-contact data, client personal information and the employee's own personal information are entangled. The human-resources ground in Article 13(2) is expressly tied to "labour rules and collective contracts signed in accordance with law" ✅, so an institution relying on it for employee data — including employee personal information moved across borders for HR administration (CAC Order No. 16, Article 5, carries a parallel exemption ✅) — needs those rules and contracts to exist and to say so. Separately, the mailbox is a monitored workplace artefact and a private communication channel at once; §7 states the institution's documentation and control duties, and stops there.

**Business-contact data versus personal data.** The distinction a compliance function needs is not "work versus personal" — PIPL draws no such line — but *whether the information relates to an identified or identifiable natural person*. A generic role address ("operations@") with no natural person behind it is not personal information; "Li Wei, Head of Operations, +86 …" is, whether or not it was given for business purposes. The practical consequence is that a bank's global address book and its mail directories are personal-information stores in their own right, not just infrastructure.

**Who is the data subject in a mail flow?** A question that has no analogue in a database review, and that a compliance function has to answer before it can honestly claim a lawful basis. In a mail flow the parties are not two — processor and subject — but at least four: the **sender** (who may be a foreign counterparty), the **recipient** (the institution, or a person at it), the **named third parties** in the content (clients, counterparties, minors), and the **bystanders** on a forwarded chain or an attached contact list, who never chose to be in the message at all. The institution is a processor for the personal information in its own mailbox, but it is not the collector of most of it, and it has no direct relationship with several of the people whose data the message carries. That four-party structure is what makes "obtain consent" an incomplete answer (see §4 above and §5.4), and it is why the guide treats documentation of the flow, rather than consent alone, as the tractable control.

**Structural where the statute is silent.** Where the PIPL text read this pass does not reach a mail-specific question — the lawful basis for inbound third-party mail, the treatment of a message chain that accumulates parties, the retention of a mailbox after an employee leaves — this guide states the rule structurally and marks it ⚠ rather than inventing an article. Section 16 lists those gaps.

## 5. The Cross-Border Transfer Question, Applied to Mail

The cross-border rules are set out in the framework guide's §5; this section applies them to a mail estate. It is written as a sequence of **structured questions**, because the regime's own text is drafted for "providing" data to an overseas party and a mail estate does not map onto that verb cleanly. No volume threshold is printed — the band separations are cited by article and left as figures to §15.

**5.1 Question one — where does the mail system sit?** The first question is architectural, not legal: for each mailbox and each durable copy, in which jurisdiction does it rest, and who operates the store? A mailbox is a controlled, durable copy of the message that the institution can read and produce. Under the transfer regime, what matters is where that durable copy is provided to, or held by, an overseas recipient. So the correct first move is to draw the mail estate on a map: the regional tenant, the primary and archive stores, the journal or capture store, the backup and disaster-recovery replicas, and any eDiscovery or third-party archive appliance. Each of those is a place personal information rests; the regime is cued by the ones outside China.

**5.2 Question two — what does a "global tenant" mean?** A single worldwide mail tenant presents the regime with a complication rather than a convenience. If the tenant's mailbox data is provisioned in a region outside mainland China, then the institution's own mail store is offshore, and the transfer question is settled in the wrong direction before any message is sent: the durable copy sits abroad by configuration. If the tenant is operated as a mainland-China instance — physically isolated, with its own endpoints — the durable copy sits in China and the direction of questioning reverses. The vendor documentation retrieved this pass describes exactly this split for one major provider: *Microsoft 365 operated by 21Vianet* is "specific to China", is **not** operated by Microsoft itself but by 21Vianet, and carries its own endpoints (for example `partner.outlook.cn`) ✅ (vendor documentation, current). The compliance reading is that choosing the global tenant versus the China-operated instance is **not** a performance choice — it is the transfer decision, and it should be taken by the compliance function jointly with IT, documented, and revisited when the tenant is reconfigured. A generic mail programme (universal guide, §7 capture, §14 anti-patterns) has no reason to treat tenant selection as a legal act; under this regime it is one.

**5.3 Question three — is a message received from abroad a "transfer"?** This is the question the regime's text does not answer for mail, and this guide analyses it as two distinct flows rather than asserting a rule. The drafters' verb is *provision*: PIPL Article 38 addresses a processor that "provides personal information for a party outside the territory of the People's Republic of China" ✅, and CAC Order No. 16 speaks of 向境外提供个人信息 — providing personal information to overseas ✅. On that language, the flows resolve differently:

- **Inbound-only (channel of collection).** A message sent *to* a China-based mailbox by an overseas sender is data the institution *received*, not data it *provided abroad*. The sender chose the recipient and the route; the institution did not transfer anything outward. On the face of the instruments, a purely inbound message is **not** the institution's 数据出境 — it is the institution's collection. That does not make it unregulated: it is personal information the institution now processes, and it engages the collection, notice and security duties, but it is not, by that reading, a provision of data overseas. This guide marks the conclusion ⚠ because it is an inference from the drafting, not a retrieved rule specific to mail.
- **Onward transfer.** The moment the institution does something with the received message that places it abroad — replicates it to an offshore archive, syncs it to a global tenant, sends it to a foreign archive vendor for review, or forwards it to an overseas recipient — the institution has *provided* that data to a party outside China, and the transfer regime is engaged on its own terms. The inbound message's origin is irrelevant at that point; what counts is the institution's act.
- **The channel-of-collection question.** The harder version, left open here, is whether a mailbox that is *itself* hosted abroad means the institution "collected" the personal information outside China (so the receipt itself is a domestic processing event) or "transferred" it (because the durable copy is offshore). The architecture in §5.1 and §5.2 determines which of those two characterisations applies. The safest compliance posture is to treat an offshore durable copy as a transfer and document it as such, even where the argument that it is merely a collection channel is available.

**5.4 Question four — which transfer mechanism would apply to a mail estate?** Assuming some mail flow *is* a transfer (because the durable copy, or an onward disclosure, is offshore), the three mechanisms of PIPL Article 38 apply ✅, and CAC Order No. 16 routes among them. Read at its own text, the routing works by **category and by volume**: Article 7 identifies the cases that must go to the **security assessment** (安全评估) — and one of those cases is defined by a volume of personal information or of sensitive personal information ✅; Article 8 identifies a **middle band** of volumes for which the exporter may instead rely on the **standard contract** (标准合同) or the **certification** ✅; Article 3, Article 4 and Article 5 provide exemptions, including a *de minimis* volume exemption and an exemption for cross-border human-resources management of employees' personal information ✅; and Article 9 sets the validity of an assessment result ✅. Applied to a mail estate, the practical reading is:

- A mail estate hosted offshore will almost always be a large, continuous flow, because it carries every message, every attachment and every contact. The volume-based routing in Articles 7 and 8 is therefore likely to point at the security assessment rather than at the standard contract, but **the figures are deliberately not printed here** — §15 records the articles, the finding that the instrument does set such bands, and the reason no number is reproduced.
- Exemptions are narrow and factual. Article 5's human-resources exemption ✅ matters for employee mailboxes whose data is administered across borders; it does not reach client personal information that happens to be in the same mailbox. Article 3's exemption for data containing no personal information or important data ✅ matters for purely operational mail (system alerts, monitoring notifications) and nothing else.
- Whichever mechanism applies, Article 10 imposes obligations that ride along with any outbound personal information: notice, the individual's **separate consent**, and a **personal-information protection impact assessment** (PIPIA, 个人信息保护影响评估) ✅. Those three duties are the ones a mail programme most often cannot evidence, because the data subject may be an inbound third party (§3, Property two) with no consent channel. That evidential gap is the compliance finding, and it is recorded, not argued away.

Set out as a table, the three mechanisms and their mail-specific consequence read as follows — the routing is by category and volume, cited by article and not reproduced:

| Mechanism (PIPL Article 38) | Where CAC Order No. 16 routes it | What it means for a mail estate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security assessment (安全评估) | Article 7 — including the cases defined by a volume of personal information or of sensitive personal information ✅ | The likely landing place for an offshore mail estate, because an offshore durable copy carries every message; the figures are left to source (§15) |
| Standard contract (标准合同) | Article 8 — the middle band of volumes ✅ | Available only where the volume falls in that band; the contract must be concluded with the overseas recipient, which for a mail estate includes the mail platform operator and any offshore archive vendor |
| Certification (认证) | Article 8 — the same middle band ✅ | An alternative to the standard contract for the same band; the certification's implementing rules were not retrieved (§16) |
| Exemptions | Articles 3, 4, 5 and 6 ✅ | Article 5's HR-administration exemption covers cross-border administration of EMPLOYEES' personal information under lawful labour rules — not client data that happens to share the mailbox; Article 3 covers mail with no personal information at all |

**5.5 Question five — the CII overlay.** If any part of the institution's operations is designated 关键信息基础设施 (critical information infrastructure) — and 金融 (finance) is one of the sectors the CSL names ✅ (amended CSL Article 33, read at the CAC-published text) — then the operator's duties are stricter: localisation of personal information and important data collected or generated in domestic operations, with a security assessment for any outbound provision ✅ (amended CSL Article 39; PIPL Article 40 confirms the same for CIIOs and high-volume processors). A mail estate that belongs to, or serves, a designated part of the institution inherits that overlay. Whether a given bank is a CIIO is a designation question this guide does not resolve; §16 flags it.

**5.6 The transfer-documentation habit.** The single operational takeaway from this section is that a China mail programme should hold, for each offshore durable copy and each onward disclosure, a written record of the following — and that this record is what a supervisor asks for, and the artefact that fails first when tenant selection is treated as an IT default:

- **What** personal information leaves — by category, and whether sensitive personal information is involved.
- **To whom** it goes — the recipient's identity and its jurisdiction.
- **On which mechanism** — the security assessment, the standard contract, the certification, or an exemption, cited to the article.
- **With what notice and consent** — the Article 39 notice and the individual's separate consent, or the ground relied on where neither exists.
- **With what assessment** — the PIPIA record for the outbound processing.
- **How it is evidenced** — the assessment result, the concluded contract, or the certification on file.

Section 10 turns this into a checklist and a set of vendor questions.

## 6. Localisation — What an In-Country Obligation Would Mean for Mail

**What the requirement is, structurally.** The localisation duty in the regime is not a general "all data must stay in China" rule; it attaches to defined data and defined operators. The CSL requires a 关键信息基础设施运营者 (CIIO) to store, within China, the personal information and important data it collects and generates in domestic operations, and to route any genuinely necessary outbound provision through the security assessment ✅ (amended CSL Article 39). PIPL states the same duty for CIIOs and for processors that reach the volume set by the cyberspace department ✅ (Article 40). The framework guide owns the general localisation regime and its industry variations (**§7 Data Localisation Requirements by Industry**), so this section does not restate it.

**What PIPL's general chapter adds.** Beyond the CII case, PIPL's cross-border chapter conditions *any* provision abroad on one of the Article 38 routes ✅ and, before that, on the Article 39 notice-and-separate-consent duty ✅. That is not localisation in the strict "store in China" sense, but it is localisation's practical twin: if the mailbox or its durable copy is abroad, the institution is running an outbound flow for which it must hold a mechanism and an evidence trail. Storing in China avoids the outbound characterisation for the stored copy; the price is that the in-China store must then satisfy the security, 等级保护 and sector duties that apply to it (§9), and must be able to produce to a foreign party when lawfully required (§3, §7).

**What an in-country mail obligation would mean for the architecture.** Framed structurally, an in-country obligation for the mail estate means four concrete things: (i) the **durable store** — mailbox, archive, journal — sits on infrastructure located in mainland China, not merely on a tenant whose contractual home region is elsewhere; (ii) the **operator** of that store is a Chinese-licensed provider or the institution's own in-China infrastructure, because providing internet email services in China is a licensed activity (§7.2); (iii) the **backup and disaster-recovery copies** follow the primary, because a replica abroad is an outbound copy in the regime's terms even if it is "just a backup"; and (iv) **access paths** — mobile clients, administrative consoles, foreign-parented support staff reading the mailbox — are analysed as outbound access, because a message made visible outside China to a person outside China is, at minimum, a disclosure event to be documented. Those four items are the whole of the localisation problem for mail, and each is a decision a bank makes rather than a switch a vendor flips. Stated as an architecture checklist:

- **The durable store in China** — mailbox, archive and journal on infrastructure located in mainland China, not merely a tenant whose contractual home region is elsewhere.
- **A Chinese-licensed or in-China operator** — the provider of the mail service holds the licence or filing; the institution knows which entity that is (§7.2, §10.2).
- **Backups and DR following the primary** — a replica abroad is an outbound copy in the regime's terms even if it is described as "just a backup" (§11.6).
- **Access paths analysed as outbound** — mobile clients, admin consoles and foreign support reading a China mailbox are disclosure events to be documented, not invisibly free.

**The tension to name honestly.** Localising the mail store satisfies the transfer duty, but the same estate is a records system that may have to be produced to a foreign regulator or a foreign court. The localisation requirement and the production obligation are both real, and they can point in opposite directions. That is not a defect to be argued away; it is a structural tension a China programme must document and plan for, and it is the reason the operational section (§10) treats production paths as part of the hosting decision rather than as an afterthought.

## 7. Content, Supervision and Lawful Access — Stated Narrowly

This section states, narrowly and factually, what the instruments and published guidance require **of an institution, as documentation and control duties**. It records the obligations a bank's compliance function would write down; it does not characterise any obligation beyond its legal content, and where an obligation cannot be described without going past its legal content, this section describes it as a legal requirement and stops.

**7.1 The email-specific instrument's control duties.** The 互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 sets a set of concrete duties on providers of internet email services and on parties that send internet email within China ✅ (Article 2 sets that scope; all articles below read at the instrument's own text on gov.cn). They are, as written: a **licence or filing** requirement before providing the service (Article 4), and a bar on access providers serving unlicensed or unfiled providers (Article 5); a duty to **register the email server's IP address** with the authority before the server is opened, and to register a change in advance (Article 6); a duty to build the service to the ministry's technical standards and to **close the server's anonymous-relay function** (Article 7); a duty to tell users the service content and rules (Article 8); a **confidentiality duty** over users' registration information and email addresses, with a bar on unlawful use or, without consent, disclosure (Article 9); a duty to **record the send or receive time, the sender's and recipient's email addresses and IP addresses**, and to provide those records when lawfully queried (Article 10); a set of **prohibited-content** duties by reference to the Telecommunications Regulations (Article 11); bars on unauthorised use of another's computer system to send mail and on trading in harvested addresses (Article 12); and rules on **commercial advertising** by email — no concealed or forged envelope information (信封信息), no advertising without the recipient's explicit consent, and required marking of advertising in the header information (标题信息) (Article 13), with an opt-out right (Article 14). A complaints and reporting machinery is imposed on providers (Articles 15 to 18). Read plainly, Article 26's definitions matter for this guide: the instrument defines the email address, the **envelope information** (信封信息) and the **header/title information** (标题信息) separately — the same envelope-versus-header distinction the universal guide's §3 owns for the protocol generally, here given a specific legal name in the Chinese instrument.

The duties read as a table — each an obligation an in-scope institution or provider must be able to show it meets, cited by article:

| Article | Duty (as the instrument's own text states it) | On whom it binds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | Obtain a value-added telecom services licence or complete non-commercial ICP filing before providing internet email services | Service providers |
| 6 | Register the email server's IP address with the authority before the server is opened; register a change in advance | Service providers |
| 7 | Build to the ministry's technical standards; **close the anonymous-relay function**; harden the system and act on discovered vulnerabilities | Service providers |
| 9 | Keep users' registration information and email addresses confidential; no unlawful use or, without consent, disclosure | Service providers |
| 10 | Record send or receive time, sender's and recipient's addresses and IP addresses; provide them on lawful query | Service providers |
| 11 | Do not create, copy, publish or transmit prohibited content (by reference to the Telecommunications Regulations) | Any person or organisation |
| 12 | No unauthorised use of another's computer system to send mail; no trading in harvested addresses | Any person or organisation |
| 13-14 | No concealed or forged envelope information; no commercial advertising without the recipient's explicit consent; required advertising marking; honour an opt-out | Senders |
| 15-18 | Operate a complaints-and-reporting channel; cooperate with investigations | Providers and access providers |

**7.2 Who those duties fall on.** Article 2 scopes the instrument to providing internet email *service*, providing access to it, and sending internet email, *within the People's Republic of China* ✅. A bank that runs its own in-China mail infrastructure or contracts an in-China provider is, on the face of it, within that scope as a service provider; a bank that merely *uses* mail is within the sending and receiving scope. The distinction matters for which articles bind it directly — the licensing, IP-registration, anonymous-relay and log duties plainly target service providers, not every corporate mailbox user. This guide records that allocation as it reads it and does not overstate it.

**7.3 The network-layer content and record duties.** The amended CSL imposes content-management duties on network operators: to strengthen management of information published by users, to stop transmitting information that law or regulation prohibits, to take elimination measures, to prevent diffusion, to **preserve relevant records**, and to report to the competent department ✅ (amended CSL Article 49, read at the CAC-published text; the pre-2026 numbering was Article 47). The same law places corresponding duties on providers of electronic information sending services and application-download services ✅ (amended CSL Article 50). And it requires network operators to **provide technical support and assistance** to public-security and state-security organs acting lawfully to safeguard national security and investigate crime ✅ (amended CSL Article 30). Recorded plainly: these are statutory duties of an operator — to act on prohibited content, to keep records, and to assist lawful investigation — and this guide states them as such.

**7.4 The protection of communication secrets, and its limit.** The email-specific instrument states the general protection and, in the same breath, its limit: citizens' communication secrets via internet email services are protected by law, and **no** organisation or individual may infringe a citizen's communication secrets on any ground — **except** where inspection of communication content is carried out by a public-security organ or a procuratorial organ, in accordance with the procedures set by law, for the needs of national security or the investigation of criminal offences ✅ (Article 3, read at the instrument's own text). A bank's lawful-access policy should be written to that text: inspection of mail content is a matter for the named authorities acting under statutory procedure, and the institution's role is to have a documented, auditable process for receiving and answering a lawful request — not a generalised content-inspection programme of its own.

**7.5 The limit on foreign requests.** PIPL Article 41 addresses the outward direction: competent Chinese authorities handle foreign judicial or law-enforcement requests for personal information stored within China in accordance with Chinese law and the treaties China has concluded or acceded to, or on the principle of equality and reciprocity, and — absent the approval of the competent Chinese authorities — **no** organisation or individual may provide data stored within China to a foreign judicial or law-enforcement authority ✅ (read at the NPC English translation). Article 42 allows the cyberspace department to place overseas recipients on restricted or prohibited lists where they infringe Chinese citizens' rights or endanger national security or public interests, and Article 43 provides for countermeasures ✅. For a mail estate this is the production-side mirror of §5: the same offshore archive that raises a transfer question raises an Article 41 question when a foreign authority asks for the messages inside it. Section 16 records that the *interaction* between a China production duty and a foreign production order is not settled by the texts read this pass.

**7.6 The institution's own documentation duties.** Gathered from the texts above, the duties a compliance function should be able to *demonstrate* — as opposed to merely assert — are these:

- **A licence/filing position** for whoever operates the in-China mail service, and the identity of that operator (Article 4).
- **An IP-address registration record** for the mail server, and the change procedure (Article 6).
- **Evidence that anonymous relaying is disabled** on the mail server (Article 7).
- **A confidentiality control** over users' registration information and addresses (Article 9).
- **A log configuration** covering send/receive time, sender's and recipient's addresses and IP addresses, with the ability to produce them on a lawful query (Article 10).
- **An acceptable-use and advertising rule** covering the sender-side prohibitions (Articles 11-14).
- **A complaints-and-reporting channel**, and a cooperation process for investigations (Articles 15-18).
- **A lawful-access procedure** that routes content inspection through the named authorities per the instrument's own limit (Article 3, §7.4).
- **An approval gate** before any data stored in China is provided to a foreign authority (PIPL Article 41, §7.5).

**7.7 What this section deliberately does not do.** It does not characterise the legal system that produces these duties, does not discuss motivations, and does not go past the instruments' own text. Where an obligation touches content, this section records only what the text requires of the institution — a duty to act on prohibited content, a duty to keep records, a duty to assist lawful process, a duty to route content inspection through the named authorities — and stops.

## 8. The Record and Retention, China-Specific Only

**What is not repeated here.** The universal guide owns capture and the record — [§7 The Record — Envelope, Message, Journal, WORM, and Completeness](email_compliance_guide.md) — and it owns retention, legal hold and disposition — [§8 Retention, Legal Hold and Disposition](email_compliance_guide.md). This section does not restate envelope-versus-message capture, journaling as a mail-server feature, what a WORM store does and does not guarantee, or how an institution would know the archive is missing something; it assumes the reader has them and adds only what China changes.

**What China changes — the log duty is a law, not a design choice.** The distinctive China feature is that the *records duty on the mail infrastructure* is statutory and specific, not merely a records-management convention. The email-specific instrument requires the service provider to record the send or receive time, the sender's and recipient's email addresses and IP addresses, and to provide those records when lawfully queried ✅ (互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 Article 10, read at the instrument's own text). The instrument fixes a retention period for those records; **the period is not printed in this guide** — see §15 for the reason and for what was retrieved. The practical reading is that an in-China mail estate is expected to keep **routing and addressing metadata** — the envelope-level facts — as a matter of law, quite apart from whether the institution also archives message bodies for its own records programme.

**The network-layer log duty.** Separately, the amended CSL requires a network operator to take technical measures to monitor and record network operation status and cybersecurity events, and to retain the relevant network logs for not less than a period fixed by the law ✅ (amended CSL Article 23(3), read at the CAC-published text; pre-2026 numbering Article 21(3)). Again the figure is cited by article and not printed (§15). This duty sits on the network, not specifically on the mailbox, but a mail estate is network infrastructure, and the two duties — the email-service log duty and the network-log duty — together mean an in-China estate is under a positive obligation to keep logs it might otherwise have turned off.

**The financial-sector overlay.** For a bank, the NFRA's 银行保险机构数据安全管理办法 adds a sector duty that reaches the mail estate through the data it carries: it requires operation logs on sensitive-and-above data to be retained for a fixed minimum period and audited at a fixed maximum interval, both set in the instrument's own text ✅ (Article 43), and it fixes a retention period for personal-information protection impact assessment reports and processing records (Article 58) ✅. The figures are cited by article and not printed (§15). Read together, the sector rule means the mailbox's *log* trail, not only the mailbox's *messages*, is a regulated record for a Chinese bank.

The record duties, gathered in one view — note that each is cited by article and that the guide prints no period:

| Record duty | Instrument and article | What the estate must keep | Period printed here? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Email-service log duty | 互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 Article 10 ✅ | send/receive time, sender's and recipient's addresses, IP addresses | No — figure in the instrument, §15 |
| Network-log duty | amended CSL Article 23(3) ✅ | network operation and security-event logs | No — figure in the instrument, §15 |
| Sector operation-log duty | NFRA measures Article 43 ✅ | logs on operations touching sensitive-and-above data, plus an audit interval | No — figure in the instrument, §15 |
| Sector PIPIA record | NFRA measures Article 58 ✅ | the PIPIA report and the processing record | No — figure in the instrument, §15 |
| Message capture | universal guide §7 (envelope AND message, journal, WORM) | the correspondence itself | n/a — jurisdiction-neutral (universal guide §8) |
| Message retention | **not sourced** | — | **No — no institution mail-retention period was retrieved** |

**The message-retention question, and why no period is printed.** Nothing retrieved this pass sets a **message-body retention period for an institution's own mailbox** — no "keep client mail for N years" rule was found at source. The statutory periods that *were* retrieved concern logs (the email-service duty, the CSL network-log duty, the NFRA log duty) and the PIPIA record, not the retention of correspondence as such. That distinction is the honest finding: **the guide prints no retention period as a rule for the institution**, because none was sourced, and it prints no period for the log duties either, because the guide cites the instrument and article and leaves the figure in §15. A mail programme that needs a message-retention period should therefore source it from the institution's own records-retention schedule and its supervisor's expectations (universal guide, §8), and record the China-specific *log* duties beside it — not infer an institution period from a service-provider log duty, which binds a different party.

**Disposition, legal hold and the China overlay.** The universal guide's §8 shape — that retention is a schedule, that legal hold suspends disposition, and that disposition is evidentiary — holds in China unchanged. The China overlay is only that (i) the log duties above are non-discretionary and cannot be dispositioned away on the institution's own schedule, and (ii) an outbound copy of a message is itself a transfer event (§5), so disposition of an offshore replica is not purely a records decision but also a transfer-documentation decision. Neither changes the shape; both add rows to the schedule. Concretely, a China mail retention schedule should carry:

- The **institution's message-retention period**, sourced from its own records schedule and its supervisor — not from the mail platform's default and not inferred from a log duty.
- The **statutory log rows** — the email-service log duty, the CSL network-log duty, and the NFRA operating-log and audit rows — each cited to its article and each with an owner, because they are non-discretionary (§8, §15.3).
- The **PIPIA-record row**, cited to the NFRA article (Article 58).
- A **transfer-event row**, recording that an outbound copy's disposition is also a transfer-documentation act (§5).
- A **legal-hold suspension** that overrides the schedule where litigation or an investigation is foreseeable (universal guide, §8).

## 9. The Security Obligations — 等级保护, Security Review, Encryption

**9.1 等级保护 is the codified control framework, and it is statutory.** The multi-level protection scheme, 等级保护 (MLPS, widely called "MLPS 2.0"), is not a voluntary best-practice catalogue in China; it is a statutory system. The amended CSL states that the state implements a cybersecurity multi-level protection system and that a network operator must, per that system, fulfil a listed set of security duties ✅ (amended CSL Article 23, read at the CAC-published text; pre-2026 numbering Article 21). The listed duties are ordinary security controls given legal force: internal security management systems and operating procedures with a designated person responsible; technical measures against viruses, attacks and intrusion; technical measures to monitor and record network operation and security events with a minimum log-retention duty; and measures for classification, backup of important data, and encryption ✅ (same article). The baseline standard beneath the system is **GB/T 22239-2019** *信息安全技术 网络安全等级保护基本要求* (*Baseline for classified protection of cybersecurity*), published 2019-05-10 and effective 2019-12-01, superseding GB/T 22239-2008 ✅ (National Public Service Platform for Standards Information). The framework guide owns the MLPS 2.0 level ladder (**§6 Data Classification and Grading System (MLPS 2.0 Levels 1-5)**); this guide does not restate the levels.

**9.2 The financial-sector rules put data *into* MLPS.** The NFRA measures make the linkage explicit for a bank. They require an institution to fulfil data-security protection duties **on the basis of the cybersecurity multi-level protection system** ✅ (Article 5), to bring its data **into** the multi-level protection system, to divide network security domains by data-security level, and to establish per-domain baselines including content filtering, access control and security monitoring ✅ (Article 41), and, where it uses internet and other information networks for data processing, to implement the requirements of 等级保护, critical-information-infrastructure security protection, and cryptography protection ✅ (Article 28). The plain consequence for a mail estate is that the mail platform is not outside MLPS: on the face of these texts, an in-China mail service that carries sensitive-and-above data is expected to be graded and controlled under the scheme, with a control baseline set for the highest-classified data it processes or stores.

The framework guide owns the MLPS 2.0 level ladder (**§6 Data Classification and Grading System (MLPS 2.0 Levels 1-5)**); this guide does not restate the levels. What matters for mail is which statutory control duties a mail estate has to demonstrate — and the CSL lists them in the same article that establishes the system:

| Statutory MLPS control duty (amended CSL Article 23) | What it means for a mail estate |
| --- | --- |
| Internal security management systems and operating procedures, with a designated person responsible ✅ | Documented mail-platform administration, ownership and responsibility |
| Technical measures against viruses, attacks and intrusion ✅ | The gateway and platform controls the universal guide's §6 owns, here legally required |
| Technical measures to monitor and record network operation and security events, with a minimum log retention ✅ | The log duties of §8; email is network infrastructure |
| Measures for classification, backup of important data, and encryption ✅ | Data classification (§9.3) and the encryption duties of §9.4 |

**9.3 数据分类分级 feeding the security baseline.** MLPS grading is not the same exercise as data classification, and a bank runs both. The NFRA measures require a data-classification and grading regime with a data catalogue and graded protection measures ✅ (Article 16), and they set a three-level scheme — 核心数据 (core), 重要数据 (important) and 一般数据 (general), with the general tier subdivided into 敏感数据 (sensitive) and other general data ✅ (Article 18, read at the instrument's own text). The mail estate's compliance problem (§3, Property one) is that it cannot be catalogued at message-content granularity; the workable answer is to classify at the *platform* and *flow* level — where the mailbox rests, what categories it is credibly capable of carrying, and which controls therefore attach — and to document that reasoning.

**9.4 Transmission and storage controls.** On encryption, the retrieved texts are directions rather than algorithms: the amended CSL includes encryption among the required measures of the multi-level protection system ✅ (Article 23(4)); the NFRA measures require that transmission of sensitive-and-above data use a secure transmission method to protect integrity, confidentiality and availability ✅ (Article 44), that security storage measures protect sensitive-and-above data against ransomware and trojans, and that **identity-authentication data shall not be stored, transmitted or displayed in plaintext** ✅ (Article 45, read at the instrument's own text). For mail specifically, the universal guide's §6 owns the transport-security controls (STARTTLS, MTA-STS, RFC 8314) as protocol mechanics; the China addition is only that the regime *requires* encryption rather than recommending it, so the transport controls are compliance-relevant, not merely hygiene.

**9.5 The security-review dimension.** Beyond MLPS sits the security review (网络安全审查) régime, which the CSL attaches to CII operators procuring network products and services that may affect national security ✅ (amended CSL Article 37, read at the CAC-published text; pre-2026 numbering Article 35), requiring the review to be passed through the national cybersecurity department together with the relevant State Council departments. The 关键信息基础设施安全保护条例 (State Council Order No. 745, adopted 27 April 2021, promulgated 30 July 2021, in force 1 September 2021) ✅ is the regulation beneath that duty; its full text was not read this pass, and the separate network-security review measures were not retrieved, so this guide states the security-review dimension structurally only and marks it ⚠. For a mail estate the practical point is confined: if the institution is a CIIO, the procurement of the mail platform and its supporting network products may itself be in scope for a security review — a procurement-timing and documentation consideration rather than a design one.

**9.6 What is verifiable at source and what is second-hand.** Verified this pass: that MLPS is a statutory system (CSL, CAC-published text ✅); the identity, publisher, publication date and effective date of GB/T 22239-2019 ✅; the NFRA measures' articles placing data into MLPS and setting classification, transmission, storage and plaintext-identity duties ✅; and the identity and dates of State Council Order No. 745 ✅. Not verified this pass: the internal content of GB/T 22239-2019 (the specific requirements at each level) — cited by identity and date only; the content of the PBOC financial-industry standard JR/T 0171-2020, whose title, publisher, publication date and effective date were retrieved from the standards platform ✅ but whose body was not read; the content of the related financial data-security grading standard JR/T 0197-2020, which was **not** retrieved at all; and the network-security review measures, not retrieved. Section 16 records each of these as an honest gap, on the principle that a reader should be able to tell which claims rest on an instrument's own text and which rest on an identity-and-date match.

**9.7 What a mail programme reads from the standard, and what it must not.** The workable reading of the standards material for a mail estate is a short list:

- 等级保护 is a **statutory** system (CSL ✅) with a **standards baseline** (GB/T 22239-2019 ✅) — so an in-China mail platform is expected to be graded, and a control baseline set for the highest-classified data it processes.
- The NFRA measures **place the data into** that system (Articles 5, 28, 41 ✅), so a bank cannot treat the mail platform as outside grading on the ground that it is "just infrastructure".
- The **controls to expect** are the ordinary ones the CSL lists (management systems, technical protections, logging, classification, backup, encryption), not a mail-specific control set — the mail-specific duties live in the 2006 instrument (§7, §8), not in the security standard.
- What must **not** be read from the material is any claim about the *content* of GB/T 22239-2019, JR/T 0171-2020 or the security-review measures: those were not read this pass, and this guide cites their identity and dates only.

## 10. The Operational Reality — What a Bank Actually Decides

This is the practical section. Everything above reduces, for a bank operating in China, to four decisions and one checklist of documentation.

**10.1 Decision one — where the durable copy rests.** The hosting question is the transfer question (§5.2, §6). A bank chooses between (a) a mainland-China **isolated tenant or on-premises/private mail infrastructure**, where the durable copy is in China and the transfer characterisation is avoided for stored mail but the 等级保护, licensing and log duties land squarely on the institution (§7, §8, §9); and (b) the **global tenant**, where the durable copy is offshore, the transfer regime is engaged by configuration, and the institution carries a continuous outbound-flow documentation burden. Both are lawful; neither is free. The decision should be taken against a written inventory of every place the mail data rests — primary mailbox store, archive, journal, backup, DR replica, eDiscovery appliance, mobile-device cache — because a single offshore replica re-creates the outbound characterisation even if the primary is in China (§6).

**10.2 Decision two — who operates the store.** Providing internet email services in China is a licensed or filed activity ✅ (互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 Article 4). So the operator of an in-China mail service is either the institution itself (holding the appropriate registration) or a Chinese-licensed provider. The vendor documentation retrieved this pass illustrates how the market is structured: a major global provider serves China through a physically isolated instance operated by a domestic partner rather than by itself, with its own endpoints ✅ (Microsoft 365 operated by 21Vianet — kind: vendor documentation, current). The compliance reading is that "who is the operator" determines which entity holds the licence, which entity holds the log duty, and which regulator is competent — so the operator question is a legal question, not a sourcing preference.

**10.3 Decision three — the tenant and region configuration, documented as a legal act.** A global tenant's region settings, its data-residency options, and any later "harmonisation" or tenant-consolidation project are, under this regime, changes to the transfer posture. They should be raised, approved and recorded by the compliance function, not applied as an administrative change. The failure mode this prevents is the tenant misconfiguration in §11 — a region change made for performance or licensing reasons that silently converts a domestic mail estate into an outbound flow.

**10.4 Decision four — the production and lawful-access path.** Because the same estate that is localised may have to produce mail to a foreign regulator, a foreign court, or a lawful Chinese request (§7.4, §7.5), the institution must decide in advance how each of those is handled and document it: which entity may lawfully answer, which law governs, and what approval is required before data stored in China is provided to a foreign authority (PIPL Article 41 — no provision without the approval of the competent Chinese authorities ✅). The universal guide's §10 (Discovery and Production) owns the mechanics; the China addition is the approval gate.

The three realistic hosting postures, contrasted so that the trade-off is visible in one view:

| Posture | Where the durable copy rests | Transfer posture | What it costs the institution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mainland-China isolated instance or own in-China infrastructure | In China | Stored mail is not an outbound provision; onward disclosures still are | Licensing/filing duty, 等级保护 grading, the log duties, and the production problem (§6) |
| Global tenant, China mailboxes on it | Outside China | Every message in the estate is an outbound flow by configuration | The full Article 38/39/10 burden — mechanism, notice-and-separate-consent, PIPIA — for the whole estate, including domestic-to-domestic mail |
| Hybrid — China instance for the domestic estate, group archive and support fenced from it | In China, with residual registered flows | Localised for the primary; registered for each residual flow | The engineering and governance cost of the fence, plus the register for what crosses it |

**10.5 The documentation a compliance function must hold.** A China mail programme that cannot produce the following on request is not compliant in any meaningful sense, regardless of where its mailboxes sit: (1) a **mail data map** — every durable copy and its jurisdiction; (2) a **transfer register** — for each outbound flow, the recipient, the jurisdiction, the Article 38 mechanism relied on, and the notices given; (3) the **notice-and-separate-consent evidence** required by PIPL Article 39 and CAC Order No. 16 Article 10 for outbound personal information ✅; (4) the **PIPIA records** for outbound processing ✅ (same articles; PIPL Article 55 requires an assessment in the listed circumstances including outbound provision, and Article 56 governs its content); (5) the **mechanism artefacts** — the security assessment result, the concluded standard contract, or the certification, as applicable ✅ (PIPL Article 38); (6) the **log-retention configuration** evidence for the email-service, CSL network-log and NFRA log duties (§8); and (7) the **classification and MLPS grading** documentation for the platform (§9).

**10.6 The questions to ask a global mail vendor.** The following are the questions a China programme should put to any global mail provider, framed as vendor documentation requests (the answers are vendor representations, not law, and should be recorded as such — see §11, failure mode five):

1. In which jurisdiction is the **mailbox content at rest** for our tenant, and is there a separate China-operated instance with its own endpoints and its own operator?
2. Who is the **legal operator** of the China instance, and which entity holds the Chinese licence or filing for internet email services?
3. Where do the **archive, journal, backup and DR replicas** rest, and can any of them be pinned to China independently of the primary?
4. What **administrative and support access paths** exist from outside China, and can they be disabled or logged for our tenant?
5. What **log fields** does the service retain (routing envelopes, sender/recipient addresses, IP addresses, timestamps), for how long, and can the retention be configured to meet a Chinese statutory log duty?
6. What **encryption** is applied at rest and in transit, and is identity-authentication data held in plaintext anywhere?
7. What **data-residency commitments** are contractual, and what notice is given before a region or residency change?
8. What does the vendor provide to support a **security assessment, standard contract or certification** filing, and a **PIPIA**?
9. How does the vendor handle a **lawful request** from a Chinese authority and from a foreign authority, and what is disclosed to the customer?

The honest framing is that these answers are vendor claims to be verified and recorded, not compliance facts in themselves — which is exactly the failure mode the next section names. What each answer should become, in the record:

- A **vendor assertion with a date and a scope**, filed as vendor documentation, never cited as law.
- A **verification task** — a configuration check, a contractual clause, or a test — before the assertion is relied on.
- A **row in the transfer register** if the answer discloses a durable copy or an access path outside China.
- A **gap note** if the vendor cannot or will not answer, with the interim control the institution applies instead.

## 11. The Failure Modes — Symptom, Cause, Guardrail

The format follows the universal guide's §12 (Failure Modes — Symptom, Cause, Guardrail), applied to the China case. Each failure is one a mail programme actually hits; each guardrail is a control, not a hope.

**11.1 The global tenant misconfiguration.**

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Symptom** | The mail estate is believed to be "in China" because a mailbox was created on a China endpoint, but a tenant-wide setting — a default region, a harmonised archive, a migrated backup — has placed a durable copy offshore, and the institution's transfer register does not mention it. The outbound flow exists and is undocumented. |
| **Cause** | Tenant and residency settings are treated as IT configuration rather than as a legal act (§10.3); the durable-copy inventory (§10.1) was never written, so no one can tell that a copy moved. |
| **Guardrail** | Treat every tenant, region, residency and archive change as a compliance change requiring approval and a register update; maintain the mail data map as a living artefact; test it by asking "for mailbox X, list every jurisdiction where a readable copy exists". |

**11.2 The transfers nobody documented.**

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Symptom** | A supervisor asks how outbound personal information in mail is routed among the Article 38 mechanisms, and the institution cannot answer: it has mailboxes abroad, an archive vendor abroad, and support staff abroad, but no per-flow record of the mechanism, the recipient or the notice given. |
| **Cause** | The transfer regime was read as a data-export project (databases, catalogues) rather than as a mail-flow question (§3); the mail estate's outbound flows are continuous and ordinary, so they were never enumerated. |
| **Guardrail** | Build the transfer register (§10.5) flow-by-flow — durable copies, onward disclosures, vendor access — and attach a mechanism, a recipient and a notice record to each; re-run it whenever the estate changes. |

**11.3 The employee who forwards to a personal account.**

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Symptom** | Business mail containing client personal information is forwarded to a personal webmail or a personal device, moving it outside the institution's controlled estate and, if the personal account is offshore, across a border; the institution has neither the record nor the control. |
| **Cause** | The estate's controls (§6 gateway controls, universal guide) meet a human convenience; the transfer regime's obligation attaches to the institution, but the act is an individual's. |
| **Guardrail** | Technical blocking of external auto-forwarding and personal-account destinations where the platform allows it; a documented acceptable-use rule tied to the institution's transfer duties, not merely to confidentiality; and monitoring of forwarding rules as a control (recorded as a control, §7). |

**11.4 The mailbox in the wrong jurisdiction.**

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Symptom** | A mailbox intended to be a China mailbox is provisioned in the wrong region — because a user's account defaulted elsewhere, or a migration restored it to the wrong store — so one mailbox's durable copy sits abroad while the rest are local; the boundary of the localised estate is quietly broken. |
| **Cause** | Provisioning defaults and migration tooling do not carry the compliance intent (§10.1); there is no check that each mailbox's durable copy is where it is supposed to be. |
| **Guardrail** | A post-provisioning and post-migration reconciliation that reports the jurisdiction of every mailbox's durable copy against the intended map; treat any out-of-place mailbox as an incident with a documented disposition. |

**11.5 The vendor assurance taken at face value.**

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Symptom** | The compliance file records "the vendor is compliant" or "data stays in China" on the strength of a marketing statement, a sales deck, or an inherited assurance, without a source, a date or a scope; when tested, the assurance does not cover the specific durable copy or the specific access path in question. |
| **Cause** | Vendor documentation is treated as law (§10.6); the distinction between a vendor representation and a regulatory obligation is not maintained. |
| **Guardrail** | Record every vendor claim with its source, its date and its kind — documentation, not law — and verify the specific claim against configuration and contract before relying on it; keep the vendor claims in the same register as the transfers they support (§10.5). |

**11.6 The restore that reintroduces a jurisdiction.**

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Symptom** | A disaster-recovery restore, a mailbox-level recovery, or a vendor-side backup restoration brings mailboxes back on infrastructure in a different region from the primary, so the "China" estate silently re-acquires an offshore copy — often during an incident, when attention is on availability, not on jurisdiction. |
| **Cause** | DR and backup infrastructure was never in the mail data map (§10.1), so the restore target's jurisdiction is not a designed constraint. |
| **Guardrail** | Pin backup and DR targets to the same jurisdiction as the primary where the obligation requires it; make the restore runbook assert the jurisdiction of the restored copy; re-run the data-map reconciliation (§11.4) after any recovery. |

**11.7 The log duty that was never configured.**

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Symptom** | The mailbox is localised correctly, the transfers are registered, but the routing/addressing log duty and the network-log duty are unmet — the platform's default retention is shorter than the statutory duty, or a setting was left at a default, so the institution cannot produce the records the law requires it to keep and produce on a lawful query. |
| **Cause** | The log duties (§8) were read as records-management preferences rather than as non-discretionary statutory duties, and no one checked the platform's retention against the duty. |
| **Guardrail** | Treat the email-service, CSL and NFRA log duties as configuration requirements with an owner; verify the platform's log retention against each duty; and record the verification as part of the log-retention evidence in §10.5. |

**11.8 The common thread.** Four of the seven failures above (11.1, 11.4, 11.6, and the vendor-claim failure 11.5) are the same failure wearing different clothes: a fact about the estate — where a copy rests, who can read it, how long a log is kept — was never written down, so no one could tell that it had changed. The single durable control against all of them is the living mail data map and the register it feeds (§10.5). The other three (11.2, 11.3, 11.7) are failures of *obligation awareness*: the transfer duty, the forwarding duty and the log duty were each real, and each was unowned.

## 12. The Banking Angle — Domestic versus Foreign Institution

This section is framed structurally and asserts only as far as the sources retrieved this pass support.

**12.1 The domestic institution.** The NFRA measures apply directly and by name to banking and insurance institutions **established within the territory** of the People's Republic of China — the definition enumerates policy banks, commercial banks, rural cooperative banks, credit unions, asset-management companies, finance companies, financial-leasing companies, auto-finance companies, consumer-finance companies, money brokers, trust companies, wealth-management companies, insurance companies and their holding structures ✅ (Article 2, read at the instrument's own text). So for a domestic bank the sector data rules are not guidance to be weighed; they are the rule set, and the mail estate is in scope through the data it carries. The NFRA measures' incident-reporting and annual-reporting duties (Articles 69, 73 and 74; see §15.3, row 4) and their administrative-penalty provisions for banking and for insurance (Article 77) attach to that institution ✅ — the article is cited; no penalty amount is printed (§15).

**12.2 The foreign institution operating in China.** The same NFRA notice is addressed to, among others, 外资银行 (foreign-funded banks) ✅ (addressee list, read at the gov.cn text), so a foreign-funded bank established in China is within the measures' reach on the same terms as a domestic one. What differs for a foreign institution is not the existence of the duty but its **direction of pull**: the institution is embedded in a group whose mail estate, archive, directory and support functions typically sit outside China and serve the parent and its other entities. That group architecture is exactly what converts ordinary mail administration into a series of outbound flows (§5), and it is where a foreign institution's compliance burden concentrates. The NFRA measures also require, for a bank that shares sensitive-and-above data with its parent bank, group or subsidiaries, an authorisation-and-consent basis and data-security isolation between parent and subsidiary ✅ (Article 29) — a family-relationship provision that bites directly on a foreign group's shared mailbox directory and shared archive.

A comparison of the two institution types, framed structurally and asserted only as far as the sources retrieved this pass support:

| Dimension | Domestic institution | Foreign institution operating in China |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which rule applies | The NFRA measures apply directly and by name (Article 2) ✅ | The same, since 外资银行 (foreign-funded banks) are among the addressees ✅ |
| Direction of pull | The mail estate is more likely already in China | The group mail estate, archive and support typically sit outside China, so ordinary administration becomes outbound flows |
| Group sharing | Parent/subsidiary sharing is still governed by the isolation and consent rule (Article 29) ✅ | The same rule bites directly on a foreign group's shared directory and shared archive |
| Extraterritorial edge | Limited (the institution is already inside) | PIPL Article 3 and the Article 53 representative duty can reach offshore mail processing of Chinese clients' data ✅ |
| Where the burden concentrates | The log duties, MLPS grading and the log/production interplay | The transfer register — every group-configured flow has to be enumerated and mechanised |

**12.3 The extraterritorial edge.** PIPL Article 3 applies the law extraterritorially where a processor outside China processes the personal information of natural persons inside China for the purpose of providing them products or services, or to analyse or evaluate their behaviour ✅, and Article 53 requires such an offshore processor to set up a specialised agency or designate a representative **within** China ✅. A foreign group's offshore mail-processing — a foreign archive, a foreign eDiscovery platform, a foreign support desk reading Chinese clients' messages — can therefore engage PIPL directly, and the representative duty is a concrete obligation rather than a principle. The framework guide owns extraterritorial reach generally (**§11 Extraterritorial Reach**); the mail point is only that a mail estate is a place where extraterritoriality becomes operational, because the offshore processing of Chinese clients' messages is exactly the fact pattern the article describes.

**12.4 What this section does not assert.** It does not assert whether any given bank is a 关键信息基础设施运营者 (CIIO) — that is a designation question (§5.5, §16). It does not assert a specific filing obligation, deadline or threshold for any institution, because no figure is printed (§15). It does not assert an interaction rule between a Chinese production duty and a foreign one (§7.5, §16). And it keeps every worked example on the one fictional institution, Cymbal Bank (§13).

The four assertions a compliance function should **not** make on the strength of this guide alone, stated so that they are easy to check against the record:

- That the institution is, or is not, a 关键信息基础设施运营者 (CIIO) — a designation question (§16.1, entry 3).
- That a specific filing obligation, threshold, deadline or penalty amount applies — no figure is printed (§15.3).
- That a Chinese production duty and a foreign production order can both be satisfied — the interaction is unresolved (§7.5, §16.1, entry 3's neighbourhood).
- That an offshore copy held for "just backup" is outside the transfer regime — it is not, on this guide's reading (§6, §11.6).

## 13. The Cymbal Bank Worked Example

**This example is explicitly fictional and illustrative.** Cymbal Bank is the only institution in any worked example in this guide; the scenario below is constructed to make the regime's decisions concrete and is not a description of any real institution's systems.

**The situation.** Cymbal Bank is a bank with an established operating presence in China — a China-licensed entity staffed in Shanghai and Beijing, serving domestic clients — and a parent group elsewhere. Its mail estate grew by acquisition and habit: the group runs a single worldwide mail tenant for staff collaboration, with some China staff on that tenant and some on an older on-premises server that predates the global rollout. There is a group-wide archive appliance in the parent's region. A support desk in the parent's region can open any mailbox to troubleshoot. Cymbal Bank has never drawn its mail estate on a map.

**Step one — the map.** Cymbal Bank's compliance function begins by listing every place a readable copy of a China mailbox's content can exist: the mailbox store (split between the global tenant and the legacy server), the tenant's archive, the parent's archive appliance, the journal/capture store, the backup and disaster-recovery replicas, the eDiscovery appliance, and the mobile-device cache on every phone that synced a China mailbox. Twelve of those turn out to rest outside China. That single exercise answers the transfer question in §5.1 before any legal analysis: Cymbal Bank is running outbound flows continuously, by configuration.

**Step two — the decision the regime forces (hosting).** Cymbal Bank now must choose between migrating the whole China mail estate to a mainland-China isolated instance (or its own in-China infrastructure) and retaining the global tenant with a documented outbound-flow programme. Working the choice structurally:

- The **in-China instance** removes the outbound characterisation for the stored mail and places the estate inside 等级保护 and the Chinese log duties — but it requires an operator that holds the Chinese licence or filing for internet email services (互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 Article 4 ✅), and it forces Cymbal Bank to confront the production problem in §6: an in-China store that a foreign regulator or court may one day need.
- The **global tenant** keeps the estate where the group's tooling, archive and support already are, but it makes the transfer regime Cymbal Bank's permanent operating condition: every durable copy offshore, every support access offshore, a per-flow register and an evidence trail for the Article 38 mechanism, the Article 39 notice-and-separate-consent, and the PIPIA.
- The **hybrid** Cymbal Bank actually reaches — a China instance for the domestic estate, with the group archive and support desk fenced from it — is the realistic middle, but only once the fence is enforced and the residual flows are registered, which is the point of §10 and §11.

**Step three — the documentation.** Cymbal Bank builds the seven-item compliance file of §10.5: the mail data map; the transfer register, per offshore durable copy and per onward disclosure, each with its Article 38 mechanism; the notice-and-separate-consent evidence for outbound personal information; the PIPIA records; the mechanism artefacts; the log-retention configuration for the email-service, CSL and NFRA log duties; and the classification and MLPS grading documentation for the platform. The file is the deliverable; the migration is only the vehicle.

The hosting decision, worked as a table the way Cymbal Bank's compliance function actually put it to the board:

| Option | Durable copy | What it fixes | What it costs | Compliance consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Keep every China mailbox on the group's global tenant | Offshore | Latency, tooling, one help desk, one archive | Nothing up front | The whole estate becomes an outbound flow; a mechanism, a notice-consent artefact and a PIPIA are owed for it, and the estate's mail becomes subject to the transfer regime in its entirety |
| Move the whole China estate to a mainland-China instance | In China | The transfer characterisation for stored mail | The licence/filing operator, MLPS grading, the log duties, and a production problem for foreign requests | Localised, but the institution owns the Chinese log duties and must plan the production path |
| Hybrid — China instance for the domestic estate, group archive and support fenced | In China, residual registered | The transfer characterisation for stored mail, with the group's tooling retained behind a fence | The engineering and governance of the fence | Localised primary plus a register for the residual flows — the posture Cymbal Bank adopted |

The sequence Cymbal Bank actually worked, in order, so that the dependence between the steps is visible:

1. **Draw the map** — every durable copy, before any decision (§13, step one).
2. **Characterise the flows** — which of them are outbound provision, which are inbound collection, which are onward disclosure (§5.3).
3. **Choose the posture** — China instance, global tenant, or hybrid, against the table above.
4. **Name the operator** — the licensed entity, and therefore the entity that holds the log duty (§10.2).
5. **Set the log retention** against the email-service, CSL and NFRA duties (§8).
6. **Build the register and the evidence file** (§10.5).
7. **Re-run the reconciliation on every change** — tenant change, migration, restore, vendor change (§11.1, §11.4, §11.6, §14.14).

**The one decision the regime changed.** A jurisdiction-neutral mail programme would have answered the hosting question on the same grounds a global IT programme always does — **latency, licensing cost, and the support model** — and would have chosen to keep every China mailbox on the group's global tenant, because that is where the archive, the eDiscovery tooling and the help desk already sit, and because no mail protocol distinguishes a Shanghai mailbox from a Singapore one. Cymbal Bank's jurisdiction-neutral programme had, in fact, already made that choice silently: the mailboxes were on the global tenant by default. **Under the China regime, that default became a regulated act.** The decision the regime changed was not "where should the mailboxes be for performance" but "which jurisdiction's law applies to a message the moment it comes to rest" — and because the durable copy sat offshore, the answer was that every message in the estate, including purely domestic correspondence between two Shanghai employees, had been made subject to the outbound-transfer regime (PIPL Articles 38 and 39; CAC Order No. 16 Articles 7, 8 and 10 ✅). A programme that never asks the question gets the wrong answer by default, and the wrong answer here is not a latency defect — it is an undocumented, unmechanised, unconsented export of the institution's entire mail archive. Cymbal Bank consequently moved the domestic estate into a China instance and registered the residual flows, not because the global tenant was unlawful, but because the regime required the *decision* to be made, evidenced and maintained.

**The thesis, restated.** Cymbal Bank's mailboxes were never merely mailboxes. The moment an institution operates in China, the mailbox's location stops being an engineering default and becomes the compliance decision on which every message in the estate depends — which is why the map came before the architecture, and the register came before the migration.

## 14. The Anti-Patterns

Each anti-pattern below is a real way a China mail programme goes wrong, stated as the mistake and the correction. They are the China-channel analogues of the universal guide's §14.

**14.1 "We are hosted in China, so we are fine."** Treating a China region setting on the primary store as the whole of localisation, while the archive, the journal, the backups and the DR replica rest elsewhere. *Correction:* the durable-copy inventory (§10.1); localised means every readable copy, not the primary.

**14.2 "Our vendor says data stays in China."** Recording a vendor representation as a compliance fact, with no source, date or scope (§11.5). *Correction:* record vendor claims as vendor documentation, dated and scoped, and verify against configuration and contract.

**14.3 "Transfers are a data-export project, not a mail problem."** Building the transfer register for databases and interfaces and leaving the mail estate — the one system that moves personal information by default — off it (§11.2). *Correction:* the mail estate is a transfer surface by construction; it belongs on the register.

**14.4 "Consent is someone else's problem, because the data came inbound."** Assuming that because a message arrived from a foreign sender, no consent or Article 39 notice obligation attaches to anything done with it thereafter. *Correction:* the moment the institution places the received content abroad, it is doing the providing (§5.3) and the Article 38 and Article 39 duties attach to the institution.

**14.5 "The mailbox is just a tool; region is an IT setting."** Letting tenant, region and archive changes be applied as administrative changes (§10.3, §11.1). *Correction:* every such change is a compliance change and goes through the register.

**14.6 "The employee's forwarding rule is a user-behaviour issue."** Handling personal-account forwarding as a confidentiality breach only, with no reference to the transfer duty it can trigger (§11.3). *Correction:* tie the acceptable-use rule and the technical blocking to the institution's transfer obligations.

**14.7 "We'll document it after the migration."** Retrospectively writing the transfer register to match an architecture already changed. *Correction:* the register is the deliverable; the architecture change is approved against it, not after it.

**14.8 "Retention is a records-management choice."** Assuming the mailbox's retention is entirely the institution's to set and disposition, ignoring the non-discretionary log duties (§8). *Correction:* the log duties sit outside the disposition schedule; the message-retention period is sourced from the institution's own schedule and its supervisor (§8, §15).

**14.9 "A foreign production order can be answered directly."** Producing mail stored in China in response to a foreign authority's request without the approval the regime requires (PIPL Article 41, §7.5). *Correction:* document the approval gate before the request arrives.

**14.10 "One jurisdiction-neutral programme covers every market."** Assuming the group's global mail compliance programme, which was built for latency, availability and search (universal guide, §7 and §8), answers the China question. *Correction:* the China question is *where the durable copy rests and what was documented about it*, and it requires a China-specific decision and register.

**14.11 "The mail platform is infrastructure, so it is IT's problem."** Delegating tenant, region and archive decisions to the infrastructure function because they look like configuration. *Correction:* those decisions *are* the compliance decision under this regime (§10.3); the compliance function approves and records them.

**14.12 "Consent covers it, because employees can be told."** Treating a policy acknowledgement by staff as the notice-and-separate-consent the regime requires for outbound personal information. *Correction:* the notice and separate consent are owed to the *individual whose data it is* (PIPL Article 39), not to the employee who happens to send the mail; employee acknowledgement is not the Article 39 artefact.

**14.13 "The China instance and the global tenant are just replicas of each other."** Assuming the two can be kept in sync without each sync being a registered flow. *Correction:* replication across the boundary is an onward disclosure (§5.3); each direction of sync is a flow on the register.

**14.14 "The register can be a spreadsheet we fill in annually."** Treating the transfer register as a periodic filing rather than a living artefact. *Correction:* the estate changes with every tenant change, migration, restore and vendor change (§11.1, §11.4, §11.6); the register is maintained on change, not on a calendar.

The anti-patterns share a root with the failure modes: a China mail programme fails when it treats a *placement* fact as a *configuration* fact. Every correction above therefore resolves to the same two artefacts — a map of where every readable copy rests, and a register of every flow across the boundary — maintained on change rather than on a calendar. If a programme holds those two and nothing else, it holds the two things a supervisor can test.

3. **The CII Regulations' content and the network-security review measures.** State Council Order No. 745 was verified by identity and dates; its body was not read, and the separate 网络安全审查 (network security review) measures were **not retrieved at all**. The security-review dimension in §9.5 is therefore stated structurally and marked ⚠. Whether and how a mail platform's procurement is caught by security review is unresolved here.
4. **The three transfer-mechanism instruments in their own texts.** The 数据出境安全评估办法 (Measures for the Security Assessment of Data Export, CAC Order No. 11) and the 个人信息出境标准合同办法 (Measures for the Standard Contract for Outbound Transfer of Personal Information, CAC Order No. 13) were identified **only** through Article 13 of the Cross-Border Provisions ✅; their own texts, and the standard-contract annex, were not retrieved. The personal-information protection certification's implementing rules were not retrieved either. So the mechanism descriptions in §5.4 rest on PIPL Article 38 and CAC Order No. 16, not on the mechanism instruments themselves.
5. **The email-specific instrument's current amendment status, and the real-name question.** The 互联网电子邮件服务管理办法 (2006) is the only email-specific instrument retrieved; its issuer, the Ministry of Information Industry, was reorganised into MIIT in 2008, and no amendment or repeal record was retrieved this pass, so whether it stands unamended is left as a flagged open question (§15.4, row 5). Separately, whether email *accounts* fall within the CSL's real-name registration duty for network-access, domain-registration, information-publication and instant-messaging services (amended CSL Article 26) is not stated in the article and could not be verified (§15.4, row 6). The anti-telecom-and-online-fraud law's possible email-related duties were not retrieved.
6. **Vendor documentation beyond one provider, and a tool limitation.** Vendor documentation was retrieved for **one** provider — Microsoft's *Microsoft 365 operated by 21Vianet* ✅ — and the market structure of other China mail providers (Alibaba Mail 阿里云邮箱, Tencent Exmail 腾讯企业邮, Coremail, Google Workspace's China posture) was **not** retrieved; vendor and government pages are frequently JavaScript-rendered and several candidate URLs would not extract this pass. **Tool limitation, recorded honestly:** the Library of Congress Global Legal Monitor URL supplied as a dated government source for the Cybersecurity Law amendment **failed to extract** (the scraper tried only pdf/document engines and returned an internal error), and the CAC's own publication URL for the Cross-Border Provisions failed to extract similarly (JavaScript-rendered). The amendment fact was therefore **not** verified through that LOC page; it was verified instead at the amended instrument's own text as published by CAC on 2025-12-29 (source text 中国人大网) ✅, and corroborated by dated press. Where a search returned empty or a page would not extract, that is recorded as a tool limitation and not as absence of material.

7. **No institution-level mail message-retention period.** Nothing retrieved this pass sets a retention period for the correspondence in a bank's own mailbox — no "keep client mail for N years" rule was found at source. The statutory periods that *were* retrieved concern logs (the email-service log duty, the CSL network-log duty, the NFRA operating-log and audit duties) and the PIPIA record, none of which is a message-body period. This is recorded as a finding, not a gap to fill: the guide prints no message-retention period, and a programme that needs one must source it from the institution's own records schedule and its supervisor.

### 16.4 A reading order for a China mail programme

For a reader who arrives with a real estate and needs a sequence rather than a survey, the order that follows the dependencies is:

1. **Inventory** every place a readable copy of the estate's content rests, and its jurisdiction (§10.1).
2. **Characterise** each flow — inbound collection, stored copy, onward disclosure — against the instruments' verb "provide to overseas" (§5.3).
3. **Choose the hosting posture** and name the operator (§10.1, §10.2).
4. **Select and file the Article 38 mechanism** for each outbound flow, with its Article 39 notice and separate consent and its PIPIA (§5.4, §10.5).
5. **Set the log retention** against the email-service, CSL and NFRA duties (§8).
6. **Grade the platform** under 等级保护 and classify the data (§9).
7. **Write the lawful-access and production procedure**, including the approval gate for foreign requests (PIPL Article 41) (§7.4, §7.5, §10.4).
8. **Maintain** the map and the register on change, and re-run the reconciliation after any migration or restore (§11, §14.14).

### 16.5 The closing summary

The China email-and-SMTP compliance problem is not a protocol problem — SMTP is jurisdiction-agnostic, and the universal guide owns its mechanics. It is a *placement* problem: the mail estate is a records system and a transfer surface at once, and under the China data-governance regime the location of a mailbox, the location of every durable copy of what it holds, and the documentation of every place that content can be read are regulated decisions rather than engineering defaults. The regime is administered by 网信办 (the CAC) and applied through 个人信息保护法, 网络安全法 and 数据安全法, with 数据出境 (outbound transfer) routed among 安全评估, 标准合同 and 认证, 等级保护 (MLPS 2.0) codifying the controls, and a sector layer from the NFRA for banking and insurance. What email adds — unstructured content, third parties' personal information, inbound receipt, and international routing by default — is why the honest answer to a bank's mail-hosting question is not "where is it fastest" but "which law governs a message the moment it comes to rest, and what have we written down about it". This guide has printed no threshold, no retention period and no penalty figure, citing each instrument and article instead, and has recorded its unverified claims rather than dressing them as rules. The thesis holds at every layer: an email crossing a border in China is not a transfer of information; it is a regulated event.
