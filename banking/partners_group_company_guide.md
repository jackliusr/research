## 1. Company Overview

### 1.1 The Founding — 1996, Zug (Verified)

**Partners Group** was founded in **1996** in **Zug, Switzerland** — the low-tax canton on Lake Zug that has become Europe's private-markets and commodity-trading hub. The corporate origin story is unusually well-documented because the firm tells it itself on its "Our Heritage" page: three partners set up the firm "with the intention of building a different kind of firm" — a private-markets investment house built around a *single integrated platform* rather than siloed, deal-team-led partnerships. The founding year is confirmed by the corporate site, Wikipedia, and the firm's own press materials; the 1996 date is **Verified**.

The founding insight deserves attention because it explains everything that follows in this guide. In the mid-1990s, private equity was still a niche, largely US-centric business run by boutique general partnerships. The founders' bet was that (a) institutional investors worldwide would need *access* to private markets — hence a fund-of-funds and advisory engine — and (b) the asset class would professionalise and scale, which would reward firms that built **platform** capabilities — sourcing, underwriting, portfolio management, technology — rather than star dealmakers. Partners Group's first major milestone, in year two, was a **US$150 million small/mid-cap private equity fund-of-funds** — a fund-of-funds start, which is why the firm's DNA still carries the "primaries/secondaries" legacy even though it is now overwhelmingly a direct investor (§4).

Key founding-period facts (Verified unless flagged):

- **1996** — Founded in Zug by three partners (see §1.2), each contributing an equal share of the initial capital pool.
- **1997/98** — First fund-of-funds (US$150M small/mid-cap program); the firm began as an access provider to private markets.
- **2000s** — Expanded into direct private equity, private debt, private real estate and private infrastructure; opened its global office network, including Singapore in 2004 (§8).
- **2006** — IPO on the Swiss Exchange (then SWX, now SIX) — a landmark: one of the first private-markets managers to list (§6).
- **2010s** — The direct-investing pivot matured; private wealth business launched; AUM crossed US$50B (2016) and US$100B (2021) (trajectory flagged, reported).
- **2020s** — Crossed US$150B AUM (end-2024, Verified); ~US$185B by mid-2026 (flagged; see §3); newer strategies (royalties, special opportunities) scaled.

### 1.7 The Early Years in More Depth (Reported, Directional)

The first decade deserves a little more texture than the timeline allows, because it explains the firm's structural quirks. Partners Group began not as a deal shop but as an **access business**: the US$150M small/mid-cap fund-of-funds of 1997/98 was a vehicle for institutions that wanted private-equity exposure without picking GPs themselves. Over the late 1990s and early 2000s the firm layered on three things in parallel: (1) **advisory mandates** — helping large institutions design private-markets portfolios (the ancestor of today's customised mandates, §5.1); (2) **secondaries** — buying LP stakes, which became a signature capability (the firm was among the earliest systematic secondaries buyers in Europe); and (3) **direct investing** — its own buyouts and growth deals, initially in European mid-market industrials and later healthcare and technology. By the 2006 IPO the firm had ~USD 10–15B of AuM and a genuinely global client list — the IPO (CHF 2.24B market cap) was a milestone both for the firm and for the asset class, as one of the first major private-markets listings in Europe.

Two features of this early history are worth carrying forward: **vintage diversification** (because it ran primaries/secondaries/direct across many funds and years, Partners Group's books were always diversified across vintages — a risk property allocators value), and **the advisory-to-platform arc** (the firm kept the allocator's mindset — thinking about portfolio construction and diversification rather than single deals — even as it became a direct investor). Both are recurring themes in the firm's own investor communications (historical narrative **Reported**; the 1997/98 fund-of-funds milestone is from the firm's heritage materials).

## 2. The Business Model

### 2.1 Private Markets: The Core Definition

Partners Group is a **private markets** firm: it invests in assets that are **not traded on public exchanges** — companies, loans, buildings and infrastructure — typically through long-dated, illiquid funds in which investors commit capital for 8–12+ years. The distinction that matters for a banking audience: public markets are price-discovered continuously and traded daily; private markets are **negotiated, valued periodically (quarterly marks), and exited via sale, IPO or refinancing**. The economic reward for the illiquidity and opacity is the **illiquidity premium** — the extra return private assets have historically delivered over comparable public assets — and the skill reward is **active ownership**: private investors can change management, restructure balance sheets, or reposition assets in ways a public shareholder cannot.

Partners Group's own framing is that it built the firm "around a uniquely integrated investment platform, forged to leverage the new age of private markets" (corporate site). Concretely, the model has three structural layers:

1. **Capital raising** — the firm raises long-dated commitments from institutional investors (§5), either into commingled funds or into **customised mandates** (tailored portfolios built for a single large client — a segment Partners Group pioneered at scale).
2. **Investing** — the firm deploys capital across its four asset-class pillars (§2.2) through primaries, secondaries and direct investments (§4).
3. **Portfolio management and monetisation** — the firm actively manages portfolio companies/assets and exits through sales, IPOs, refinancings and dividends.

### 2.2 The Four Pillars — Private Equity, Private Debt, Private Real Estate, Private Infrastructure

The task brief asked to verify the four asset classes. **Verified** — Partners Group operates across exactly these four pillars, and the Annual Results 2024 presentation confirms direct deployment in all four: **USD 3.7 billion in direct private equity, USD 1.4 billion in direct real estate, USD 3.6 billion in direct infrastructure and USD 4.2 billion in direct debt** invested during 2024 (PG Annual Results 2024 presentation — **Verified**). Two additional/emerging sleeves — a **royalties** strategy (US$1.5B AUM per finews, 2026 — **Reported**) and **special opportunities** — sit alongside the four core pillars.

### 2.4 How the Firm Earns: Fees, Carry, and the Balance Sheet

Partners Group's revenue model is the standard private-markets manager model, with a listed-company twist:

- **Management fees** — recurring fees (typically 1–1.5% per annum of committed/invested capital for commingled funds; lower for large customised mandates) charged to the funds and mandates it manages. This is the recurring, annuity-like revenue that makes listed private-markets managers trade like asset managers.
- **Performance fees / carried interest** — the firm's share (typically ~20% of profits above a hurdle rate, e.g. 8%) of the returns it generates for investors. This is the volatile, upside-heavy component.
- **Other income** — transaction, monitoring and advisory fees; some firms also earn from co-investment capital deployed on their own balance sheet (Partners Group's own balance-sheet investing is **flagged** — the firm does maintain proprietary co-investment exposure, but the precise scale was not re-verified this pass).

For a banking reader, the useful mental model is: Partners Group is a **fee-earning asset manager whose revenue is a function of AUM and performance, not of a balance sheet**. Unlike a bank, it takes no deposits and runs no leverage-driven intermediation; like a bank, it earns on the spread between what it pays for capital (nothing — LPs supply it) and what it earns deploying that capital (fees + carry). The 2024 numbers illustrate the flywheel: **USD 22 billion of new client commitments in 2024** (guidance had been USD 20–25B) compounding into a **USD 152B AUM** base (PG press release, 14 Jan 2025 — **Verified**).

### 2.5 The Fee Engine vs. the Bank Model (Cross-Reference)

For a banking reader the cleanest way to hold the model is to contrast it with the bank model in [universal_banking_model_guide.md](universal_banking_model_guide.md). A bank intermediates *deposits* and *funding* on its own balance sheet and earns net interest margin plus fees; its P&L is levered to rates, credit and capital. Partners Group intermediates *commitments* — long-dated promises from LPs — and earns fees and carry; its P&L is levered to **AUM growth, deployment pace and realised performance**, not to a balance sheet. The firm does not take deposit risk, does not run a trading book in the bank sense, and does not need the capital adequacy machinery a bank does. Its closest bank analogue is actually the **asset-management arm** of a universal bank (the Crédit Agricole context: CAAM-style businesses sit inside the same group as the CIB), with the differences that a listed private-markets manager carries *carry* upside and *illiquidity* as its core risk, and reports its books at quarterly NAV rather than daily marks.

The credit overlap is real, though: Partners Group's **private debt** pillar (§2.2) competes with banks for mid-market lending, and the [asset_backed_trading_guide.md](asset_backed_trading_guide.md) and [financial_risk_compliance_systems_guide.md](financial_risk_compliance_systems_guide.md) cross-refs cover the bank-side machinery (securitisation, credit risk) that sits across the table. When an SG bank like Crédit Agricole CIB structures a financing for a mid-market company, the syndicate may well include private-credit funds run by firms like Partners Group — the private-markets firm is both a client (as an investor) and a competitor (as a lender) of the banking system.

## 4. The Investing Approach

### 4.1 Primaries, Secondaries, Direct — the Three-Lane Model

The task brief asked to verify the primaries/secondaries/direct model. **Verified as the firm's own framing**: Partners Group describes its investing through the three classic private-markets lanes, and its history maps neatly onto them:

- **Primaries** — investing as an LP into *new* private-market funds run by other managers (fund-of-funds commitments), or into first-time/early funds. This was Partners Group's *original* business (the US$150M fund-of-funds of 1997/98) and remains a source of diversification and vintage-year spread. In industry shorthand, primaries = "GP commitments into fresh funds".
- **Secondaries** — buying *existing* private-asset positions from other investors: LP stakes in funds (LP secondaries) or direct stakes in companies (direct secondaries). This lane lets the firm acquire mature, de-risked assets at a discount to NAV and achieve faster capital deployment and shorter durations than primaries. Partners Group has been a systematic secondaries buyer for two decades.
- **Direct** — the firm's own direct investments: control and growth equity in companies, direct lending, direct real estate and direct infrastructure deals, originated and underwritten by its own teams. **Direct is now the dominant lane**: the Annual Results 2024 presentation shows ~USD 12.9B of *direct* capital invested in 2024 (USD 3.7B PE, 4.2B debt, 3.6B infra, 1.4B real estate — **Verified**), versus a much smaller primaries/secondaries flow. Industry reporting captures the same shift ("Partners shifts gaze from secondaries to direct", Secondaries Investor) — **Reported**.

The strategic story: Partners Group *started* as an access/allocator business (primaries), *scaled* through secondaries (buying mature positions cheaply), and *became* a direct-investing platform (originating and owning its own assets) — while keeping all three lanes so it can express any vintage, price point or liquidity profile for its clients. The three-lane model is the operational core of its "integrated platform" claim.

### 4.3 Thematic and Entrepreneurial Investing

Partners Group's own description of its style — "we think like entrepreneurs, with a thematic investing approach that seeks to find attractive assets before they become mainstream" (corporate site) — is worth taking seriously as a *process* statement. The firm invests behind a small number of structural megatrends (digitalisation, energy transition, deglobalisation/reshoring, demographic/healthcare shifts), identifies target sub-sectors within them, and then builds positions through a mix of direct deals, secondaries and primaries across all four asset classes. The same theme can be expressed as equity in a software company (PE), a data-centre loan (debt), a logistics portfolio (real estate) or a fibre network (infrastructure) — which is precisely what the four-pillar model (§2) enables. This thematic, cross-asset-class discipline is the firm's differentiation versus single-asset-class rivals. (Process descriptions are drawn from the firm's own materials — **Reported**; the *outcomes* of the process are visible in the verified investment figures in §3.)

### 4.4 The Deal Process: Sourcing to Exit

For a reader who comes from systems thinking, the operating cycle is worth laying out as a pipeline (process **Reported** — the firm's own descriptions plus industry-standard practice; stage-level specifics are directional):

1. **Theme and sector selection** — investment teams track the structural themes (§4.3) and map sub-sectors and target companies/assets.
2. **Sourcing** — the firm's 20+ offices and portfolio-company network generate deal flow; thematic sourcing aims to find assets "before they become mainstream" (the firm's phrase).
3. **Underwriting** — cross-functional due diligence: commercial, financial, operational, ESG and legal; the three-lane structure means the same asset can be bought as a direct deal, a secondary stake, or via a primary commitment.
4. **Deal execution** — equity buyouts, growth rounds, direct loans, real-estate/infrastructure acquisitions, structured by the firm's own transaction teams.
5. **Portfolio management** — board seats, operating partners, management changes, digital/AI enablement (the Emeria agentic-AI example, §9.1), capital-structure optimisation, add-on acquisitions.
6. **Monetisation** — exits via trade sale, IPO, secondary sale or refinancing/dividend recapitalisation; realised proceeds return to LPs and trigger carried interest (§2.4).

The pipeline is also the *technology* story: a firm this diversified runs its sourcing, valuation and portfolio data through proprietary tooling (§9) — the substance of which is publicly unverifiable, but the *need* for it is implied by the scale of the books it manages.

### 10.4 The Lessons

The worked example is designed to teach five transferable lessons for a banking/technology reader:

1. **AUM is a dated figure, not a fact.** The ~US$150B brief figure was the end-2024 number (Verified); by mid-2026 it was ~US$185B (Reported). Every diligence memo must pin the as-of date — the same discipline the risk guides ([risk_management_models_guide.md](risk_management_models_guide.md)) apply to model inputs.
2. **Listed private-markets managers are uniquely auditable.** The SIX listing (§6) is what made half of this guide verifiable — press releases, ad hoc announcements and annual results are primary sources. That transparency is itself an alignment signal for allocators.
3. **The primaries/secondaries/direct mix is a strategy statement.** A firm's lane mix (fund-of-funds heritage → secondaries → direct dominance) tells you where its economics and risk profile sit — read it like a bank's balance-sheet composition ([universal_banking_model_guide.md](universal_banking_model_guide.md)).
4. **The Singapore regional-HQ model is an access story.** For an SG-based allocator, the 2004-vintage local presence, MAS licence and regional mandate reduce operational distance — a real (if soft) factor in manager selection (§8).
5. **Know the verification boundary.** The firm's internal technology is a black box (§9.3); competitors' AUM figures are directional (§3.3); some headline 2026 events (Everlight auction) were in play at writing. A disciplined assessment scores what is verifiable and flags the rest — exactly the pattern this guide's §13 makes explicit.

## 11. Summary — Partners Group in One Page

**Partners Group Holding AG** is a Swiss private-markets investment firm, founded in **1996 in Zug** by **Marcel Erni, Alfred Gantner and Urs Wietlisbach**, and **listed on the SIX Swiss Exchange since its 2006 IPO** (CHF 63 offer, CHF 84 day-one close). Headquartered in Zug (registered seat Baar, canton of Zug), the firm manages **USD 152 billion of AuM as of end-2024** (Verified) — reported at **~USD 185–186 billion by mid-2026** (flagged) — for **900+ institutional investors** (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurers, endowments, corporates, and private-wealth clients) across **~20–24 offices worldwide**, staffed by **~2,000 professionals**.

The business model is the four-pillar private-markets platform: **private equity, private debt, private real estate and private infrastructure** (Verified pillars; royalties and special opportunities as emerging sleeves), deployed through the three classic lanes of **primaries, secondaries and direct investments** — direct now dominant (USD 12.9B of direct capital invested in 2024, Verified). Revenue is the standard manager model — management fees plus performance fees/carry — on top of which the listing adds transparency, a liquid equity, and a ~45% employee/partner ownership alignment.

The **Singapore angle**: the firm opened its Singapore office in **2004 as its Asian regional headquarters** (Verified), operating today as Partners Group (Singapore) Pte. Ltd., a MAS-licensed fund manager at Asia Square Tower 1 — the natural service point for Asian institutional capital, and the hook for the worked assessment in §10.

On technology, the honest boundary: Partners Group is a major **investor** in technology and digital infrastructure (a verified theme, with AI pushed into portfolio operations), but its **internal platform is not publicly documented** — flagged, not fabricated.

**The final word:** Partners Group belongs to the small club of firms that *invented* the institutional private-markets asset class and then scaled with it — from a US$150M fund-of-funds in 1997 to a ~US$185B, four-asset-class, globally listed platform with 900+ institutional clients. In an industry where scale, track record and alignment decide who receives the world's pension and sovereign capital, Partners Group sits comfortably among **the private-markets powerhouses** — the European listed pillar of a league table whose other members (Blackstone, KKR) are larger but less transparent, less employee-owned, and far less accessible from Singapore. For allocators, the firm's enduring pitch is simple: one integrated platform, four asset classes, three investment lanes, and a 30-year head start.
