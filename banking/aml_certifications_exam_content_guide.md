## 1. The Overview, the Integrity Rule and the Decoder

### 1.1 What This Guide Is

This guide is a **factbook about examination content**. It answers one narrow question: *when a professional body says "you are certified in AML/CFT", what does that body actually require a candidate to demonstrate on paper?*

It covers, for each credential:

- **The awarding body** — who owns the credential and publishes the syllabus.
- **The exam blueprint or syllabus** — the published domain list and, where the body publishes them, the **domain weightings**.
- **Format and delivery** — number of questions, duration, question style, remote or test-centre proctoring.
- **Eligibility** — education, experience, membership and attestation requirements.
- **Cost** — application, exam and mandatory membership fees.
- **Recertification and CPD** — the maintenance cycle and credit requirement.
- **Jurisdictional scope** — where the credential is recognised in practice.

### 1.2 The Integrity Rule

> **This guide describes published syllabi, exam blueprints, domain weightings, formats, structures, eligibility, fees and recertification requirements — the material the awarding bodies themselves publish for candidates.**

It deliberately does **not**:

- reproduce, paraphrase or approximate actual examination questions;
- reproduce or approximate question banks, "practice" pools sold as the real thing, or "brain dump" content;
- direct readers to such material.

Where a section describes what a syllabus domain covers, it **summarises the published syllabus in this guide's own words, at the level of an outline** — the same granularity a candidate handbook uses in a contents page or a learning-outcomes list. That is deliberate: a summary sufficient to tell you *what a domain covers* is not a substitute for study, and it is not a leak of examination content.

Three facts follow, and they are why the rule is stated plainly rather than buried:

1. **The awarding bodies publish candidate handbooks.** ACAMS, the ICA, ACFE, ACFCS, the IIA, the IBF and the regional institutes all publish a candidate handbook, a syllabus/blueprint document, and eligibility and fee pages. Those are the authoritative sources for everything in this guide, and they are free. If a third party charges you for "the blueprint", you are paying for a copy of something the body gives away.
2. **Third-party "braindump" content is unreliable.** Questions reconstructed from memory by candidates are frequently mis-remembered, out of date against the current edition, and sometimes actively wrong. Studying them teaches you another candidate's guess, not the syllabus.
3. **Third-party "braindump" content violates the awarding bodies' terms.** The bodies' candidate agreements and certification policies prohibit disclosing, soliciting or using actual examination content, and prohibit unauthorised reproduction of their copyrighted study materials. Certification can be revoked for it. Using such material is not a shortcut; it is a way to lose the credential later.

### 1.3 The Boundary Against This Repository's AML Domain Guides

This repository already contains substantial AML, KYC and regtech material — the AML-KYC vendor stack, MAS notices and guidelines, trade-surveillance and monitoring systems, sanctions screening, fraud detection at scale, private-banking onboarding. **Those guides are about the AML subject matter itself. This guide is about what the exams test.**

The distinction matters because the two answer different questions:

| Question | Where it is answered |
| --- | --- |
| *What is a suspicious transaction report, and what does the regime require?* | The AML/regtech domain guides (see §9) |
| *Which syllabus domain of which credential covers STRs, at what weighting, and how do I route study?* | This guide, §9 |
| *How does a monitoring system generate and disposition an alert?* | `banking/regtech_guide.md`, `banking/financial_fraud_detection_at_scale_guide.md` |
| *Does the ACAMS or ICA syllabus include monitoring, and is it examinable?* | This guide, §3–§7 |

Section 9 is therefore the hinge of the document: it takes the recurring syllabus domain set that every credential shares and maps each domain to the specific existing guide and section in this repository that teaches it. **This guide does not re-teach AML.** It tells you what the exams ask about, then points at the repository's own material for the substance.

### 1.4 The Decoder — Reading a Certification's Claims

Certification marketing uses a compressed vocabulary. Decode it as follows.

| Term | What it actually means | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Awarding body** | The entity that owns the syllabus, sets the exam, and issues the credential. | Is the body a professional association, a training company, a government institute, or a vendor? A training company that also awards its own certificate is not equivalent to an independent body. |
| **Credential** | A designation, qualification or certificate. Designations are usually post-nominal letters (CAMS, CFE, CGSS); qualifications are more often level-based (ICA Diploma). | Whether the credential is a single exam or an accumulation of modules changes the study and cost model entirely. |
| **Candidate handbook** | The body's own policy document: eligibility, registration, scheduling, rules, results, retake, recertification. | The handbook, not the marketing page, is where eligibility and retake policy are actually defined. |
| **Exam blueprint** | The published specification of what the exam covers, often with domain weightings. | Some bodies publish weightings; some publish only a domain list with no percentages; a few publish neither. Never infer a distribution. |
| **Domain weighting** | The percentage of exam content allocated to each syllabus domain. | Weightings change with editions. Re-checking the current edition's weighting is one of the highest-value actions available to a candidate. |
| **Eligibility requirement** | The gate before you may sit: education, years of experience, membership, attestation signature, or nothing at all. | Ambiguity here is expensive — some bodies allow you to sit the exam *before* satisfying the experience requirement, which changes sequencing. |
| **Proctoring and delivery** | How the exam is invigilated and where it is taken: test centre, live remote proctor, or record-and-review remote proctoring. | Delivery models changed materially after 2020; several bodies now offer remote proctoring by default. |
| **Recertification and CPD** | The cycle (commonly annual or three-yearly) and credit requirement to keep the designation. | The recertification cost is a recurring line item and is frequently omitted from a budget. |
| **Knowledge credential** | Evidence that the holder has studied and been examined on a syllabus. | It does not evidence that the holder has performed the work. |
| **Specialist / advanced credential** | A narrower syllabus at greater depth (sanctions, KYC, crypto), or a level-based qualification with a higher-nominal-difficulty assessment. | "Advanced" describes syllabus depth, not a licence to practise, and not a rank above a generalist credential for all purposes. |

### 1.5 Six Standing Cautions

These cautions apply throughout, and are restated in §14 where the claims are audited.

1. **This is a point-in-time snapshot.** Everything in this guide is stated as of **2026-09-19**. Exam structures, blueprints, fees and CPD cycles change, and several credentials in this landscape were revised recently. Every fee, weighting and eligibility rule below carries its source and the date checked. Treat any figure older than the current edition as a hypothesis.
2. **The current edition is the single most likely thing to be wrong.** For each credential, §3–§7 state the edition or revision status that could be established, and flag it where it could not.
3. **Pass rates and candidate numbers appear here only where the awarding body itself publishes them.** Vendor marketing figures are excluded by policy (see §10).
4. **Recognition claims need a named source.** Where a credential is described as "required" or "recognised" by a regulator or jurisdiction, the guide either names the source or labels the statement as the awarding body's own claim. Absent a named source, these are **voluntary professional credentials**.
5. **Regional credentials are not globally portable.** Jurisdictional scope is stated per credential in §7, and each one is presented as what it is: a credential rooted in a specific market.
6. **Do not crown a winner.** §8 compares and then gives a role-keyed decision framework. There is no single best credential, and any guide that names one has substituted a preference for an analysis.

> **The credential evidences a syllabus. It does not evidence competence.** That sentence is the thesis of §11 and §12, and it is why this guide spends as much space on the anti-patterns in §13 as on the exam structures themselves.

## 2. The Certification Landscape

### 2.1 How the Landscape Is Organised

AML/CFT certification is not one market. It is at least six, and the credentials inside each are not substitutes for one another:

| Tier | What it certifies | Typical form | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Global generalist** | Breadth across the whole AML/CFT programme: standards, risk assessment, CDD, monitoring, reporting, governance. | One proctored exam, usually scenario-based. | ACAMS CAMS |
| **Specialist vertical** | Depth in one sub-domain: sanctions, KYC/CDD, crypto, trade-based laundering, correspondent banking. | Shorter single-subject exam or short course with assessment. | ACAMS CGSS; ICA Specialist Certificates |
| **Associate / entry** | Foundational knowledge for staff new to financial crime roles. | Shorter exam, no or minimal experience gate. | ACAMS associate series; AICB CAML |
| **Qualification ladder** | Progressive academic-style levels with assignment-based assessment. | Module + assignment per level, multiple levels. | ICA Award → Certificate → Advanced Certificate → Diploma → Professional Postgraduate Diploma |
| **Adjacent professional** | A different discipline that happens to touch financial crime. | Multi-part or single exam from another body. | ACFE CFE; IIA CIA and CRMA; ABA CRCM |
| **Regional / national** | Market-specific regulation, standards and supervisory expectations. | Local exam, often combined with a local training pathway. | IBF (Singapore); AICB (Malaysia); IIBF (India); HKIB (Hong Kong); ACFCS (US-rooted) |

Three structural observations drive the rest of this guide:

1. **Single-exam credentials and level-based qualifications are managed very differently.** A single-exam credential is a study-and-sit event: buy the material, book the slot, revise, recertify on a fixed cycle. A level-based qualification is a programme: you accumulate modules and assignments across months or years, and the credential is an aggregation rather than an event. Employers that budget for "a certification" often discover they have funded the second kind.
2. **"Specialist" does not mean "more advanced".** A sanctions-specialist certificate is narrower than a generalist credential, not a rank above it. A hiring manager comparing a CAMS and a CGSS is comparing breadth against depth, not senior against junior.
3. **The bodies differ in age and jurisdictional rooting.** The generalist and adjacent-professional bodies are largely US-rooted and export globally; the qualification-ladder body is UK-rooted. This is visible in *how* they assess (exam versus assignment), in the legal and regulatory examples used in their syllabi, and in where their alumni networks are densest. §5 covers this honestly.

### 2.3 What Is Deliberately Absent, and the Shared Backplane

Three categories are excluded because they are not credentials in this guide's sense: **in-house training modules with a completion certificate** (they evidence attendance, not assessment); **university degrees and postgraduate certificates** (academic awards under their own accreditation regime — and a qualification *awarded in association with* a university business school remains the professional body's, not a degree); and **membership alone**, which is a prerequisite for some credentials and a CPD source for all, but never itself an earned designation.

Underneath all of them sits one backplane: the **FATF Recommendations** and their interpretive material, **Basel Committee** guidance on customer due diligence, and industry-standard guidance such as the **Wolfsberg** principles. FATF does not award a professional certification — it sets the standard the certifications test against, which is why the normative material is free and why every credential in §2.2 examines a selected portion of it.

## 3. The Flagship Generalist Credential — ACAMS CAMS

### 3.1 The Verification Gap — Read This First

**This section is the one place in this guide where the primary source could not be read.** Every attempt to retrieve `acams.org/en/certifications/cams` and its sibling pages during this research pass (2026-09-19) failed: the site refused automated retrieval on every engine tried, and no archived snapshot of the current certification pages is available in the Wayback Machine either. **This is recorded as a tool limitation, not as evidence that ACAMS publishes nothing** — ACAMS clearly publishes candidate materials; the guide simply could not read them.

The consequence is strict and deliberate: **nothing in §3 or §4 is asserted as a verified fact about ACAMS's current exam.** What follows is organised as:

- **Established** — statements that follow from ACAMS's existence, its stated position as the flagship-generalist awarding body in this market, and definitional facts that do not depend on the blocked pages.

### 3.5 Eligibility, Delivery and Retakes — What to Ask ACAMS

Because the specifics could not be read, the guide instead gives the **question set** to put to the awarding body, phrased so that the answers are actionable. This is the same question set §14 uses to audit any credential claim.

1. **Experience:** how many years, in what roles, and does the requirement have to be met *before* sitting or only within a window after?
2. **Attestation:** does the experience have to be attested by an employer or a current designee, and what happens if the candidate is between roles?
3. **Alternative routes:** is there a qualification-credit route (degree, related credential, training hours) that substitutes for years of experience?
4. **Membership:** is membership mandatory to sit, mandatory to hold the designation, and does it carry its own renewal fee?
5. **Delivery:** test centre, live remote proctoring, or record-and-review? Are all three available in the candidate's country, and what identification is required for remote delivery?
6. **Retake:** how many attempts, in what window, at what fee, and does a failure impose a waiting period?
7. **Result reporting:** immediate or delayed, and is a scaled score or a pass/fail only reported?
8. **Recertification:** cycle length, credit requirement, whether free CPD from the body counts, the fee, and the consequence of lapsing.

A candidate who gets answers to those eight questions has, in practice, everything §3 was unable to provide from the outside.

On the evidence available, **CAMS is a voluntary professional credential**, like every other credential in §2's table. §11 develops this point.

Awarding-body-published pass-rate data would be admissible; none was retrieved this pass ⚠.
- **What a candidate should conclude:** CAMS is a reasonable default when a generalist AML credential is wanted for a broad range of roles. It is not a competence test for any specific employer's systems, procedures or jurisdiction, and it is not a substitute for experience (see §13, anti-pattern 1).

### 4.2 How the Family Layers — the Only Structural Claims Worth Making

Three structural claims survive the source problem, because they follow from the *shape* of the catalogue rather than from any single figure:

1. **The family is layered by breadth and depth, not by seniority.** The flagship is the broadest. The specialists are narrower and deeper. The associate credentials are narrower and shallower, and exist mainly to credential staff who need a defined floor of knowledge rather than a programme-level credential.
2. **The associate tier changes the *cost-per-tested-head* calculation.** If an associate credential is genuinely shorter and cheaper than the flagship, it becomes the rational choice for large cohorts of operations staff — which is exactly the decision §12's worked example has to make. The decision does not require knowing the exact figures; it requires knowing that the tier exists and is aimed at founding-level knowledge.
3. **A specialist credential is a *complement*, not an upgrade.** A sanctions specialist credential and a generalist credential test different things. Holding both is a breadth-plus-depth signal; holding only the specialist one leaves the generalist syllabus untested, and vice versa.

### 4.4 The Layering Decision — Questions Before Money

If an organisation is considering stacking credentials, the sequence that avoids waste is:

1. **Obtain ACAMS's current catalogue and candidate handbook directly.** Establish which credentials currently exist, in which edition, at what fee, with what eligibility.
2. **Map each credential to a role, not to a person.** Stacking credentials onto the same individual is usually a reward, not a control. §12 works the alternative through.
3. **Check the overlap.** If a specialist syllabus is substantially contained inside the flagship syllabus, funding both for the same person buys depth in one area and duplicated breadth everywhere else.
4. **Check the recertification arithmetic.** Two credentials means two CPD cycles. §12's illustrative figures show how quickly that compounds.
5. **Decide what the credential is *for*.** If the answer is "a hiring filter", the flagship is usually the right instrument. If the answer is "a specific capability gap in sanctions or crypto", the specialist credential is. If the answer is "a floor for 30 operations analysts", the associate tier is the plausible candidate — subject to the availability checks above.

## 5. The Association Qualification Route — the International Compliance Association

### 5.1 The Structural Difference — and Why It Matters

The ICA is the clearest example in this landscape of a **qualification ladder** rather than a single-examination credential, and the difference is not cosmetic.

| Dimension | Single-exam credential (the ACAMS model) | Qualification ladder (the ICA model) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit of achievement | One proctored examination | A level, comprising modules, taught sessions and assessments |
| Time to complete | Weeks to months of self-study | Months (a certificate) to years (a postgraduate diploma) |
| Assessment | Examination, sat once | Mixed: online multiple-choice assessments **plus written assignments**, per level |
| Failure handling | Retake the exam | Re-submit or re-sit the failed assessment component |
| Output | A designation (post-nominals) | A qualification title at a stated level, plus a designation |
| Employer budgeting | A per-head purchase | A per-head programme with a schedule |

**The assessed-by-what question is the one to get right.** The two bodies differ here, and candidates who assume "certification equals exam" mis-plan their study entirely.

- **ICA at introductory level:** the Certificate in AML is assessed by a **one-hour online multiple-choice examination**, taken at any point during the studies, with results available immediately. That is an exam-based assessment even though the qualification is study-at-your-own-pace. *(Source: int-comp.org course page for the ICA Certificate in Anti Money Laundering; checked 2026-09-19.)*
- **ICA at advanced level:** the **Diploma in AML** is assessed by a **sequence of multiple-choice assessments (one per module) plus two written assignments**, together with an "immersive scenario" session. The published timetable shows Assessment 1a, 1b and 1c (MCQ windows) and Assessments 2 and 3 (assignment submission dates). *(Source: int-comp.org course page for the ICA Diploma in Anti Money Laundering; checked 2026-09-19.)*

So the accurate statement is: **the ICA uses a blended assessment model — multiple-choice assessments and written assignments — where the ACAMS model is a single proctored examination.** Neither is "harder" in the abstract; they test different things. A written assignment tests reasoning, structure and ability to apply a framework to a described situation. A timed examination tests recall and judgement under time pressure.

### 5.2 The Ladder

The ICA publishes its full designations list, which is the cleanest available map of its ladder. *(Source: int-comp.org, "Qualification designations"; checked 2026-09-19.)*

| Level (as ICA states it) | Representative qualifications | Designations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Introductory (Level 2)** | Certificate in Anti Money Laundering; Certificate in Compliance; Certificate in Financial Crime Prevention; Certificate in KYC/CDD; Certificate in Managing Sanctions Risk; Certificate in AML (Insurance) | `Cert(AML)`, `Cert(Comp)`, `Cert(FinCrime)`, `Cert(KYC/CDD)`, `Cert(Sanctions)`, `Cert(AML Ins)` |
| **Specialist (Level 2)** | Conduct Risk; Corporate Governance; Anti Corruption; ML Risk in Betting and Gaming; ML Risk in Correspondent Banking; Trade Based Money Laundering; Managing Financial Crime in Global Banking & Markets; Combating the Financing of Terrorism; Evolving Risks in Financial Crime Technology; AML and Art; Financial Crime Risk in Cryptocurrencies; AI for Compliance Professionals; AI Threats and Risk Mitigation | `Spec.Cert(...)` in each subject |
| **Intermediate (Level 4)** | Advanced Certificate in AML; Advanced Certificate in Governance, Risk and Compliance; Advanced Certificate in Practical Customer Due Diligence; Advanced Certificate in Managing Fraud; Advanced Certificate in Business Compliance; Advanced Certificate in Managing Sanctions Risk; plus Malaysian AML/CFT and Regulatory Compliance certifications | `Adv.Cert(AML)`, `Adv.Cert(GRC)`, `Adv.Cert(CDD)`, `Adv.Cert(Fraud)`, etc. |

Two features of this table deserve attention.

- **The ladder is genuinely progressive.** A candidate can enter at introductory level, move through advanced certificate to diploma, and end at a postgraduate diploma without changing awarding body. That is a materially different value proposition from buying four unrelated credentials from three bodies.
- **There are **Certified Practitioner** designations aimed at named senior roles** — financial crime investigator, MLRO, head of compliance. These are role-designated rather than subject-designated, which is unusual in this landscape and worth noting for §8's role-keyed framework.
- **The designations list also carries "designations for former qualifications"** — an explicit retirement list. That is a useful signal in itself: it means the ladder *has* changed, and that a designation seen on a CV may belong to a retired qualification. Check the current list, not a candidate's memory.

### 5.4 Eligibility and Entry Requirements — the ICA Model

The ICA's ladder is **not primarily experience-gated**. The Certificate in AML page describes the course as suitable for "anyone wanting to learn more about AML", and the ladder is designed to be entered at introductory level without prior sector experience. The principal hard requirements are procedural rather than experiential:

- **ICA membership is required while studying** (unless already a member) — the body states plainly that "as a professional, awarding body ICA requires you to be an Associate member while you're studying for your course". A 12-month membership fee is added at checkout if you are not already a member.
- **Enrolment is by course booking**, with four intakes a year for the diploma.

This is a genuine contrast with the credit-and-experience gate seen in US-rooted credentials, and with the regional bodies' entry requirements. In practice: a career-changer can enter the ICA ladder immediately; a career-changer typically cannot satisfy the eligibility gate on a US-rooted generalist credential without accumulated experience. That difference is often the deciding factor in the role-keyed framework in §8.

**Malaysia specificity:** ICA operates a Malaysian variant of the AML/CFT certification at Intermediate and Advanced levels, awarded jointly, and ICA's own pages direct Malaysia enrolments to a separate regional route. That variant is described in §7 and is **not portable as a general ICA designation**.

### 5.5 Recognition — Honest Assessment

The ICA's own position, stated on its own pages, is that its qualifications are "internationally recognised as evidence of high levels of competence" and that "many employers all over the world specify an ICA Diploma as a pre-requisite for roles", with the organisation counting over **190,000 professionals** who have taken its qualifications and over **30,000 members**. Those are the **body's own claims**, and they are recorded here as such, in accordance with §1's sourcing rule. They are not corroborated or contradicted by any independent evidence gathered in this pass.

What can be said more carefully:

- **Rooting:** ICA is UK-rooted, and its qualifications are awarded in association with Alliance Manchester Business School, University of Manchester. Its syllabus framing is therefore more likely to lean on UK/EU regulatory vocabulary (FATF, the UN conventions, the EU AML framework) than a US-rooted credential would. The Diploma in AML's own "what you will gain" text names FATF, the UN and the EU explicitly.
- **Portability:** the qualifications are delivered globally and the course pages carry localised fee currencies and translated versions (Arabic, Chinese, French, Russian routes are listed on the certificates page). International portability is therefore supported by the body's delivery model.
- **Where it differs from a US-rooted credential:** alumni density and recruiter familiarity vary by market. In a US-headquartered institution, the flagship US generalist credential is usually the currency recruiters read; in UK, EU, Gulf, African and much of the Asia-Pacific market, the ICA ladder is at least as visible. This is a market observation about familiarity, not a quality judgement.
- **What it does not mean:** neither body's credential is a licence. Neither is a regulatory requirement on the evidence of this pass. §11 addresses this directly.

### 5.6 Practical Implication for a Study Plan

Because the ladder is assessed by a mix of multiple-choice assessments and written assignments, the study method differs from a single-exam credential's. The MCQ components reward coverage; the **written assignments reward structure and argument**, so a candidate who only drills multiple-choice material will underperform on them. The ICA timetable schedules each module's assignment *after* its MCQ assessment — a design signal about how knowledge is expected to build. And the **calendar is the constraint, not the exam slot**: nine months, four intakes, three modules. Budget calendar time, not just study hours.

For substance, §9 routes each topic to this repository's material. The Certificate in AML's published topic list maps almost exactly onto §9.2's domain set.

## 6. The Fraud, Audit and Risk-Adjacent Credentials

These credentials appear constantly on AML job specifications without being AML credentials. Each is assessed below on one question: **how much AML/CFT does it actually contain, and when is it the right instrument instead of a dedicated AML credential?**

### 6.1 The IIA — Certified Internal Auditor (CIA)

**What it actually certifies:** internal audit as a *discipline* — its standards, its engagement process, its governance and its reporting. Financial crime is not the subject; auditing it is one application among hundreds.

| Item | Verified detail |
| --- | --- |
| Awarding body | The Institute of Internal Auditors (The IIA) |
| Exam structure | **Three parts** (traditional pathway), which must all be passed within three years of applying. An entry-level **IAP** pathway gives a Part 1 waiver to holders; a **CIA Challenge Exam** single-examination pathway exists for qualified CPA/CA holders and CISA holders |
| Experience requirement | Master's degree: 1 year of internal audit experience. Bachelor's degree: 2 years. No degree: 5 years, of which 2 must be within the past 3 years |
| Qualifying experience | Explicitly includes Internal Audit, Quality Assurance, Risk Management, Audit/Assessment/Disciplines, **Compliance**, External Audit, and Internal Control |
| Testing windows | February, June, September, November. After purchasing an exam registration the candidate has 180 days to register, schedule and sit |
| Scale claim | The IIA states more than **220,000 CIAs from 170 countries** and positions the CIA as the only globally recognised internal audit certification, in place since 1974 |
| Preparation materials | The IIA publishes a **Candidate Handbook**, an exam syllabus, interactive exam questions, practice questions drawn from retired exams, and a study guide |

*(Source: theiia.org CIA certification page; checked 2026-09-19.)*

**The AML content:** modest by syllabus share, substantial by applicability. The CIA does not teach the FATF Recommendations or typologies. It teaches you to test controls and report on them — which is precisely the skill required to audit an AML programme, but it is not evidence you can run one. The IIA's practice questions being drawn from **retired** exams is a useful and honest practice worth noting: it is exactly the opposite of "braindump" material, because the questions are released by the body itself.

**When it is the right choice:** when the role is *audit of* financial crime controls — internal audit, third-line assurance, audit committee reporting. It is not a substitute for AML subject knowledge, and a hiring manager who accepts a CIA where the role requires AML typology knowledge has bought a control-testing skill for a subject-matter gap.

### 6.2 The IIA — Certification in Risk Management Assurance (CRMA)

| Item | Verified detail |
| --- | --- |
| Exam | **150-minute exam, 120 questions** |
| Completion window | Two years from approval |
| Prerequisite | **The CIA designation is no longer required** — the IIA states this explicitly |
| Experience requirement | Master's: 1 year internal audit experience. Bachelor's: 2 years. No degree: 5 years, 2 of which in the last 3 years |
| Fees | Application: US$100 (member) / US$220 (non-member). Exam: US$465 (member) / US$610 (non-member) |
| Syllabus basis | The IIA states the **CRMA syllabus is currently supported by the 2017 Standards**, that the core principles remain consistent, and that **there are currently no plans to update the CRMA exam** to the Global Internal Audit Standards |

*(Source: theiia.org CRMA certification page; checked 2026-09-19.)*

**The AML content:** low. Risk, governance and assurance concepts applied to an organisation's risk management, not AML/CFT specifically.

**Two honest observations.** First, the fee asymmetry between member and non-member pricing ($145 on the exam alone) means the membership decision is part of the cost calculation, not separate from it. Second, and more importantly for anyone using this guide: **the CRMA is explicitly positioned against a superseded standards edition, on the body's own statement, with no update planned.** That is a legitimate, transparent position by the IIA — but it is a concrete illustration of the guide's standing caution that **the current edition of a credential is the single most likely thing to be wrong.** A candidate who assumes "current exam = current standard" would be wrong here, and only the body's own page tells them so.

### 6.3 ACFE — Certified Fraud Examiner (CFE)

**What it actually certifies:** fraud prevention, detection and investigation — the orientation towards *fraud* as the offence, with investigative technique and legal framing. The ACFE describes CFEs as holding skills in complex financial transactions, investigative techniques and legal issues, resolving fraud allegations, and designing anti-fraud programmes. The ACFE states it has over **95,000 members** across sectors and industries. *(Source: acfe.com CFE credential pages; checked 2026-09-19.)*

**Exam structure — the honesty problem.** The ACFE's own exam-detail pages returned 404 errors during this pass ⚠, and the substantive structure below is therefore **third-party consensus, not verified**. The consensus structure is a **four-section examination: Fraud Prevention and Deterrence; Financial Transactions and Fraud Schemes; Investigation; and Law**, with each section comprising a number of multiple-choice questions delivered as separately scheduled components. Treat the section names as a reliable orientation and **the question counts and pass mark as unverified** — verify them with the ACFE before planning.

**How much AML/CFT is in it?** Some, and in a specific direction. Financial Transactions and Fraud Schemes covers the asset-misappropriation, corruption and financial-statement-fraud landscape; that overlaps with predicate-offence theory but is *offence-type* knowledge rather than *AML-programme* knowledge. There is no section on CDD, beneficial ownership, transaction monitoring design, STR filing regimes, or FATF standards. A CFE can therefore hold a strong grasp of what predicate offending looks like without ever having been examined on what an AML programme must contain.

**When it is the right choice:** fraud investigation roles, forensic and dispute work, internal investigative functions, and teams where fraud is the primary mandate and AML is adjacent. It is a poor proxy for an AML compliance officer and a strong proxy for an AML *investigator* with a fraud mandate — see §8's role framework.

### 6.4 ABA — Certified Regulatory Compliance Manager (CRCM)

**What it certifies:** management of regulatory compliance in a US bank — the compliance programme, the regulatory landscape, and the management responsibilities around it. It is the most explicitly *US-bank-specific* credential in this section.

**Verification status:** the ABA's certification pages could not be retrieved during this pass ⚠, so **no exam structure, question count, fee, eligibility rule or CPD cycle is asserted here.** What can be said without a source: the credential's subject matter sits closest to an AML/CFT compliance officer's actual job of any credential in §6, because it is about running a compliance programme in a bank rather than auditing one or investigating one. That makes it a natural object of interest for a bank building a compliance bench — and it makes the missing verification a real gap rather than a cosmetic one. Obtain the ABA's candidate handbook directly.

### 6.5 The Cross-Cutting Rule

| Credential | Subject | Contains AML/CFT programme content? | Best used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **CIA** | Internal audit discipline | No — it audits controls that include AML controls | Third-line audit and assurance roles |
| **CRMA** | Risk management assurance | No | Audit-committee-facing risk assurance |
| **CFE** | Fraud prevention, detection, investigation | Partially — offence types and investigation, not programme design | Fraud and financial-crime investigation |
| **CRCM** | US bank regulatory compliance management | Substantially — programme management is the subject ⚠ unverified in detail | Compliance management in US banks |
| **CFCS** (see §7) | Cross-disciplinary financial crime | Substantially, and by design | Analysts and investigators needing breadth across crime types |

**The rule:** a credential from an adjacent discipline is the right instrument when the *role* is in that discipline. It becomes an anti-pattern when used to fill a subject-matter gap — funding a CIA for someone who needs typology knowledge, or a CFE for someone who needs to run a monitoring programme. §13 formalises that as anti-pattern 1.

## 7. The Regional and National Credentials

§2's landscape table gave the shape. This section gives the **scope and portability** of each regional or nationally rooted credential — and states portability plainly, because the most common error in this market is treating a national credential as if it travelled.

### 7.1 The Portability Principle

Three distinct things are often conflated:

1. **Delivered internationally.** The body will sell you the course and let you sit the assessment anywhere. This says nothing about whether *employers* elsewhere recognise it.
2. **Recognised internationally.** Employers and recruiters in multiple markets treat the credential as a meaningful signal. This is a market fact, not a certificate term.
3. **Recognised by a specific regulator or jurisdiction.** This requires a named source. Absent one, assume it does not apply — including for credentials with impressive international delivery footprints.

The table below carries a **Portability** column judged on (2) and (3), with the basis stated.

### 7.2 ACFCS — Certified Financial Crime Specialist (CFCS)

This credential is US-rooted but deliberately cross-jurisdictional in framing, which places it between the global generalist and the national credentials.

| Item | Verified detail |
| --- | --- |
| Awarding body | Association of Certified Financial Crime Specialists (ACFCS) |
| Scope | The full financial crime spectrum — the body names AML, anti-corruption, fraud, tax evasion, cybersecurity and asset recovery, and describes the syllabus as **12 core areas of financial crime topics** |
| Eligibility | **Active ACFCS membership plus proof of 40 earned credits**, verified through the body's eligibility calculator. Candidates must purchase the certification package and submit an application before scheduling |
| Study expectation | The body recommends **a minimum of 40–50 hours** of preparation using all available study tools, spread over several weeks |
| Delivery | Online, remote-proctored via **Kryterion's Online Proctoring (OLP)** network |
| Study material | A study manual — the current edition referenced on the body's pages is the **7th edition** |
| Programme options | **Self-Study** (start any time, 12 months to certification, from **US$1,215–US$1,625**) and **Accelerator** (self-paced plus nine live virtual classroom sessions, homework, recordings and a complimentary exam attempt, ~6 months to certification, from **US$1,999–US$2,220**). Payment plans available |
| Marketing claim (recorded as the body's own) | Holders include experts at 15 of the world's 20 largest financial institutions and span more than 80 countries and jurisdictions |

*(Source: acfcs.org, "The Exam", "Certification Overview" and "CFCS Handbook and Application" pages; checked 2026-09-19.)*

**Honest reading.** The eligibility gate here is unusual: it is a **credit-based** requirement rather than a flat years-of-experience rule, and it is verified by a calculator the body publishes. The study expectation is published by the body itself (40–50 hours), which is the kind of figure this guide is willing to record because it is attributable. The institutional-distribution and country claims are the **body's own marketing**, recorded as such and not independently corroborated in this pass. Note also that the CFCS package bundles study material and is sold as a package, so the "cost" is a programme cost, not an exam fee — a materially different budgeting line from a test-centre exam fee.

**Portability:** the framing is international and the exam is remotely delivered worldwide; the credential is nonetheless **US-rooted in origin and alumni density**. Treat it as internationally delivered and broadly recognisable among financial-crime teams, not as a credential that any specific regulator recognises.

### 7.3 Singapore — IBF Certification (the national standards route)

This is the clearest example in this landscape of a credential built on **national occupational standards** rather than a body's own syllabus, and its mechanics are materially different from everything in §3–§6.

| Item | Verified detail |
| --- | --- |
| Awarding body | The Institute of Banking and Finance (IBF) Singapore, established 1974 as a not-for-profit industry association |
| What IBF is | The **national accreditation and certification agency for financial industry competency in Singapore** under the Skills Framework for Financial Services, which IBF states was developed in partnership with the industry |
| Certification basis | Not a single exam. Candidates must complete **eligible IBF-STS accredited assessment programmes** and meet criteria; certification is awarded against **Technical Skills and Competencies (TSCs)** for a job role |
| **IBF Qualified (Level 1)** | For new entrants with **less than 3 years** of industry experience. Requires **75% of the TSCs within the job role**, including Industry Rules & Regulations and Product Knowledge. **Industry experience requirement: NIL** |
| **IBF Advanced (Level 2)** | For practitioners with **3 to 7 years** of relevant experience. Requires **75% of TSCs** within the job role, and the applicant must currently be in the selected job role with at least 3 years of relevant experience |
| **IBF Advanced (Level 3)** | For practitioners with **at least 8 years** of relevant experience in the selected segment, with the same 75%-TSC requirement applied at that level |
| Designatory titles | `IBFQ` for IBF Qualified; `IBFA` for IBF Advanced |
| CPD requirement | **A minimum of 15 CPD hours annually** for all IBF-certified individuals, taking effect from the year following the year of certification. Certified individuals must **maintain records** of CPD activities (attendance certificates, appointment letters, published articles), and **IBF may conduct an audit** to check the requirement has been fulfilled |
| CPD routes | All IBF-STS and Financial Training Scheme (FTS) programmes qualify towards CPD (subject to caps) |
| Scale claim (the body's own) | A community of over **21,000** certified professionals across **12 industry segments** |
| Funding | IBF operates the IBF Standards Training Scheme (IBF-STS), providing funding support for accredited training and assessment courses, available to self-sponsored and company-sponsored individuals |

*(Source: ibf.org.sg — IBF Certification, "Why be IBF Certified", and "IBF CPD" pages; checked 2026-09-19.)*

**Three things make this structurally different from a professional-body credential:**

1. **The unit of assessment is a job role, not a syllabus.** Certification is awarded against TSCs for a function, which means the relevant AML/CFT content is whatever the occupational standards for that role specify. A compliance officer's certification and an operations analyst's certification are *not the same certification*.
2. **The CPD mechanism is auditable.** IBF states it may audit certified individuals and that records must be kept. That is a stronger compliance posture than most professional bodies adopt, and it is directly relevant to §11's discussion of the evidence-record burden.
3. **There is a funding layer.** Government-linked training funding (IBF-STS) reduces the net cost of accredited programmes, which changes the economics of credentialing a whole team in a way no professional-body credential does.

**Portability, stated plainly: national.** IBF Certification is a Singapore market credential, built on Singapore's Skills Framework for Financial Services and Singapore's occupational standards. It is **not** a globally portable professional designation, and it should not be presented to a hiring committee outside Singapore as if it were. Its value is highest in exactly one market, and within that market it is the industry-endorsed standard.

**On the MAS linkage — an important precision.** IBF describes itself as the national accreditation and certification agency for financial industry competency. That is a statement about IBF's role in the national skills architecture. **This guide found no source establishing that MAS requires any individual to hold IBF Certification.** Accordingly: IBF Certification is presented here as an **industry-endorsed, government-adjacent national credential**, not as a regulatory licensing requirement. Any stronger claim needs a named MAS instrument, and none was retrieved ⚠.

### 7.4 Malaysia — AICB CAML and ACAML

Malaysia's route is distinctive: the national banking institute operates the qualification, in partnership with a global professional body.

| Item | Verified detail |
| --- | --- |

| Target audience | CAML: new/recently appointed MLROs and deputies, financial crime and risk officers, compliance officers, internal auditors taking on ML/financial-crime responsibilities. *(Source: aicb.org.my — ICA programmes, Entry Requirements and Modules pages; checked 2026-09-19.)*

**What is genuinely useful here.** The ACAML entry requirement is one of the few places in this landscape where **the credential's own gate is explicitly AML experience** — a degree route still requires three years of AML experience, and the non-degree route requires CAML plus a year. The syllabus is also published as a downloadable specification rather than buried in a course page, and it is mapped to an occupational standard rather than to a body's proprietary outline.

**Portability, stated plainly: national, with a global partner.** CAML and ACAML are awarded under AICB's Malaysian framework with ICA, mapped to Malaysian progression routes (Chartered Banker) and eligible for Malaysian HRD Corp funding. The ICA partnership means the underlying qualification content has international provenance, and the **Malaysian-prefixed ICA certifications at Intermediate and Advanced level are recorded on ICA's own designations list as distinct designations** (`Adv.Cert(AML)` for the Malaysia certification and `Dip(AML)` for the advanced certification — the same post-nominals as the global routes, which is a genuine trap for a reader trying to tell two variants apart on a CV).

**Practical warning:** because the Malaysian variants and the global ICA routes share post-nominals, verify *which* variant a CV holder actually holds before treating it as equivalent to the global qualification.

### 8.2 The Role-Keyed Decision Framework

No ranking. Each role below gets the reasoning, and the reasoning is different in each case because the roles are genuinely different.

**1. Operations analyst (alert triage, CDD/onboarding, screening disposition)**
- *What the role needs tested:* procedural fluency, typology recognition, escalation judgement.
- *Best-fit instrument:* an **introductory or associate-level credential** — the ICA Certificate in AML, or an associate-tier credential — plus employer-specific training on the actual systems.
- *Reasoning:* the analyst does not design the programme; they operate it. A broad generalist credential teaches a great deal the analyst will never use, and the cost-per-head multiplies. The exception is an analyst on a promotion track into a generalist role, for whom funding the generalist credential early is an investment rather than a waste.

**2. Investigator (financial crime investigation, SAR/STR case work)**
- *What the role needs tested:* evidence handling, interviewing, case construction, legal framing, plus AML typologies.
- *Best-fit instrument:* a **fraud/financial-crime investigation credential** (CFE, or the ICA Advanced Certificate in Managing Fraud) **plus** AML subject knowledge from a generalist or specialist credential, or the ICA Certified Financial Crime Investigator designation for a role-named route.
- *Reasoning:* investigation is a distinct discipline. A generalist AML credential alone leaves the investigative method untested; an investigation credential alone leaves the AML programme untested. This is the clearest case in the framework for a *combination* rather than a single choice.

**3. Compliance officer (programme operation, policy, regulatory interface)**
- *What the role needs tested:* the whole AML/CFT syllabus — standards, risk-based approach, CDD, monitoring, reporting, governance.
- *Best-fit instrument:* a **global generalist credential**, or the ICA **Advanced Certificate / Diploma** route where a level-based qualification suits the candidate's background better.
- *Reasoning:* this is the role the generalist syllabi were written for. The choice between them is driven by eligibility (can the candidate meet a US-rooted experience gate?), assessment preference (timed exam versus assignments), and market (which currency do local recruiters read?).

**4. MLRO (the named accountable officer)**
- *What the role needs tested:* everything in the compliance-officer remit, plus governance, reporting to the board, and the ability to construct a defensible judgement.
- *Best-fit instrument:* an **advanced/Level 6 or above qualification** — the ICA Diploma in AML (which the body itself describes as the industry standard for MLROs and professionals managing money-laundering risk), or the advanced tiers of other ladders, or an MLRO-named practitioner designation.
- *Reasoning:* an MLRO's exposure is judgement made accountable. A written-assignment assessment is arguably a better proxy for that than a timed multiple-choice examination, which is a real argument for the ladder route — and it is worth saying plainly that this is a *fit* argument, not a quality argument. **Nor is the credential the qualification for the appointment:** in most jurisdictions the MLRO's fitness is assessed by the regulator or the firm under its own regime, not by a certificate. §11 addresses this.

**5. Auditor (internal audit, third-line assurance)**
- *What the role needs tested:* control testing, assurance methodology, reporting to the audit committee.
- *Best-fit instrument:* the **CIA**, with the **CRMA** where risk-assurance work is prominent — **not** a dedicated AML credential as the primary instrument.
- *Reasoning:* the auditor's value is methodological independence. The AML subject knowledge needed to audit an AML programme effectively is often obtained through targeted training and the firm's own programme documentation. Note the honest caveat from §6: an auditor holding only a CIA and no AML subject grounding will test controls competently and may miss whether the controls are the *right* controls.

**6. Technologist / data specialist (monitoring tuning, model development, screening systems)**
- *What the role needs tested:* not primarily a professional certification. The relevant competencies are data engineering, statistics, and model governance.
- *Best-fit instrument:* **AML subject literacy** through an introductory credential plus the firm's own model-governance framework, with formal statistical/model-risk training where the role builds or validates models. A dedicated AML credential is worth funding only insofar as the specialist must interpret alerts, explain typologies to stakeholders, or sit in a financial-crime governance forum.
- *Reasoning:* this is the role most often miscalibrated by credential-led planning. A data scientist certified in AML is not thereby a better monitoring designer, and the money is usually better spent on domain immersion and model-risk training. Where an AML credential *does* pay for itself in this role, it is as a communication instrument, not a technical one.

**7. Manager building a team (hiring and development mandate)**
- *What the role needs tested:* nothing — the manager is not the person being certified. The decision is portfolio-level.
- *Best-fit instrument:* **a credential *architecture*, not a credential**. Specifically: identify which roles need a generalist credential (compliance officers, MLROs), which need an adjacent-discipline credential (auditors, investigators), which need only an associate or introductory floor (operations), and which need no credential at all (data specialists). Then fund the CPD for whatever is funded.
- *Reasoning:* the portfolio view is where the real cost sits, and it is where credential-led planning most often goes wrong — because a per-head purchase decision repeated forty times is a budget, and a budget needs a rationale that survives review. §12 works exactly this problem.

### 8.3 The Five Questions That Decide Any Individual Case

For a specific person, in a specific role, in a specific jurisdiction, the decision reduces to five questions. If any of them is unanswered, do not spend the money.

1. **What will this credential test that the role actually requires?** Name the syllabus domains. If you cannot, the credential is being bought as a signal rather than a competence.
2. **Does the candidate meet the eligibility gate — and if not, when will they?** A credential bought before eligibility is a booking, not a development plan.
3. **With which currency does the local market read this credential?** A national credential for a national role; a global one for a cross-border role. §7's portability table is the reference.
4. **Who pays the second bill?** The recertification cycle, CPD hours and renewal fees are recurring. A funded certification without a funded renewal is a credential with an expiry date.
5. **What else would that money buy?** Internal training, tooling, an external audit, a second pair of hands. This is the question §12 answers explicitly rather than rhetorically.

### 8.4 What This Comparison Deliberately Refuses to Do

It does not **rank** the credentials — ranking needs a single objective function, and the same credential is right for a London compliance officer and wrong for a Mumbai operations analyst. It does not treat **name recognition as quality**: the credential with the broadest recognition (§3) is the one whose structure this guide could least verify. It does not treat **rigour as relevance**: a harder exam in the wrong syllabus is worse than an easier one in the right syllabus. And it does not present any of them as a **licence** — §11.3 is explicit that none is.

### 9.5 Turning a Blueprint Into a Reading List — the Method

The router is only useful with a method for using it. The sequence:

1. **Obtain the published domain list from the awarding body.** For the ICA, this is on the course page (the Certificate in AML's topic list is published outright). For others, it is in the candidate handbook or exam blueprint.
2. **Translate each heading into one of the eight domains in §9.2.** Headings are marketing-shaped; domains are concept-shaped. "Institutional vulnerabilities in designated non-financial businesses and professions" is domain (vi) with a sector lens. "AML programme management and compliance culture" is domain (vii).
3. **Route to the repository section.** Use §9.3.
4. **Weight your time by the published weighting if the body publishes one — and by breadth if it does not.** §3.4 explains why an invented distribution is worse than no distribution.
5. **Read the awarding body's own material for the examination framing and the repository's for the substance.** The body's text tells you what is examinable; the repository tells you how the thing actually works, which is what the scenario questions are testing.
6. **Record what you verified and when.** Edition, weighting, fee — with the date. §14's claims audit is the model, and the reason is simple: the next revision is a matter of when, not whether.

### 9.6 What the Routes Cannot Cover

Four honest limits. **Jurisdiction-specific law:** the repository is anchored on Singapore (MAS) and international frameworks, while a syllabus may test US (BSA/AML, OFAC), EU (AMLD), UK (MLR, OFSI) or another national regime in detail — for those, go to the regulator's own material. **The examination framing:** the repository teaches how monitoring works, not how a body phrases a question about monitoring. **Legal interpretation:** beneficial ownership, sanctions scope and tipping-off have legal edges that differ by jurisdiction; the repository describes practice, not legal advice. **Your own institution:** the most examinable thing a candidate already has is their firm's policies, procedures and case history — no guide substitutes for reading your own AML programme end to end, and §12 assumes you do.

### 10.1 Study Time — What the Bodies Themselves Publish

| **CAMS** | ⚠ Not retrieved. Third-party preparation sites circulate figures (commonly in the 60–100 hour range); these are **unattributable** and are recorded here only so that a reader recognises them as vendor claims rather than body guidance | — |

| **IBF Certification** | Not an hours figure: the requirement is **75% of the TSCs for the job role**, achieved through accredited assessment programmes | ibf.org.sg; checked 2026-09-19 |

**How to use this.** Note that the two bodies which publish an effort estimate publish *very different* estimates — and not because one credential is twice as hard. The ACFCS figure is a self-study sprint estimate for a single exam. The ICA figure is a weekly cadence for a nine-month taught programme. They are not comparable numbers, and treating them as a difficulty ranking is a category error. **The right use of a published effort figure is to size a calendar, not to rank credentials.**

### 10.2 What the Bodies Provide Versus What Third Parties Sell

| Provider type | What it is | Trust posture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The awarding body's own materials** | Candidate handbook, exam content outline/blueprint, official study guide, sample questions or an exam demonstration, practice questions (in the IIA's case, explicitly **retired** exam questions) | **Authoritative.** This is the only material guaranteed to match the current edition |
| **The awarding body's own training** | Official instructor-led or virtual classroom, official e-learning, official flashcard/prep products | Authoritative for content; commercial in framing |
| **Accredited or licensed training partners** | Training delivered under a body's accreditation or partnership arrangement (for example, a national institute delivering a global body's qualification) | Content traceable to the body; verify the accreditation |
| **Independent preparation providers** | Third-party courses, question banks, "study guides", flashcard apps | **Unauthoritative.** Quality varies from excellent to actively misleading. No independent provider has access to live exam content |
| **"Braindump" sites** | Reconstructed exam questions, "verified" question pools, memory dumps | **Prohibited, unreliable, and a term of service violation** — see §1.2 |

**The specific risk with independent providers** is not that they are all bad. It is that they are **undated**. A provider's material built against a previous edition will look identical to material built against the current one, and a candidate has no way to tell. The only defence is to buy the body's own guide as the spine and use third-party material, if at all, as a supplement — and to check the edition on every third-party product against the body's current edition.

**A note on the IIA's practice of publishing retired questions.** This is the model of how a body should publish practice material: the questions are released by the body that owns them, they are genuinely retired from use, and they come with rationale for correct *and* incorrect answers. It is the exact opposite of a braindump, and it demonstrates that a body can support candidates without leaking live content.

### 10.3 The Published Blueprint Versus What Candidates Report

There is a real gap between the two, and it is worth understanding why, because it is not evidence of dishonesty or a leak.

1. **The blueprint describes topic coverage; the exam samples from it.** A blueprint weighting of 20% on a domain does not mean 20% of your questions look like the sample question in the study guide. It means the domain contributes roughly that share of the item pool. Candidates frequently over-index on the study guide's illustrative examples and perceive a mismatch.
2. **"What came up on my exam" reports are anecdotes with a sample size of one.** Every sitting draws a different sample. Two candidates who sat the same week can honestly report different emphases.
3. **Candidates remember the questions they found hard.** Recall is biased towards the surprising, not the representative. This systematically inflates reports of unusual content.
4. **Editions change, and candidate reports do not carry an edition stamp.** A report from a previous edition is worse than useless — it is confidently wrong.

**Therefore:** the published blueprint is the only reliable guide to what the exam covers, and the only reliable guide to weighting is a weighting the body publishes. What candidate reports *are* genuinely useful for is the **logistics** experience — the booking process, the proctoring interface, the check-in friction — where the body's material is less detailed and peer experience is real evidence.

### 10.4 Proctoring and Remote Delivery — What to Expect

Delivery methods changed materially after 2020, and the differences matter to a candidate's preparation plan.

| Model | How it works | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Test centre** | Candidate attends a physical centre; invigilated on site | Requires travel and scheduling; some jurisdictions have limited or no centres |
| **Live remote proctoring** | A human proctor watches in real time over a webcam and microphone | Requires a quiet, private room, stable bandwidth, and a clear desk; interruptions are at the proctor's discretion |
| **Record-and-review remote proctoring** | The session is recorded and reviewed afterwards | Less intrusive during the exam; the candidate does not get real-time assistance if something goes wrong |

**Verified examples from this pass:** ACFCS delivers the CFCS exam online through Kryterion's Online Proctoring network, letting candidates sit from home. The IIA delivers CIA and CRMA through Pearson VUE, with testing windows in February, June, September and November, and an interactive question-type demonstration hosted on the Pearson VUE demo environment. *(Sources: acfcs.org "The Exam"; theiia.org certification pages; checked 2026-09-19.)* For the **ICA**, the introductory Certificate is assessed by an online multiple-choice examination taken at any point during studies, and the **Diploma** assessments are scheduled within published windows as part of the cohort timetable — i.e. the ICA's assessment is *scheduled against a cohort calendar*, not booked at will.

**What candidates should verify before booking:** which delivery modes are available in their country; the identification requirements; whether a room scan or desk-clear policy applies; the network and hardware requirements; and — important, and often missed — whether a failure of connectivity counts against the attempt or triggers a reschedule.

### 10.5 Common Reasons Candidates Fail — From What the Bodies Publish

This section deliberately draws only on what awarding bodies publish about preparation, rather than on candidate hearsay. Four failure modes follow directly from the published material reviewed in this guide.

1. **Studying a superseded edition.** Every credential in this landscape has been revised at some point, and the ICA's own designations page carries a whole section of **retired qualifications** — proof that the ladders change. A candidate who studies last edition's guide is preparing for a different exam. *Mitigation: confirm the current edition with the body, in writing, at the start.*
2. **Substituting breadth for the body's framing.** The repository's own material (§9) is excellent on how AML works, and that is exactly why it can mislead a candidate: the exam tests the awarding body's framing of the syllabus, not the operational reality in its full complexity. A candidate who reads only applied material has knowledge the exam cannot use. *Mitigation: the body's material for framing, the applied material for depth, in that order of authority.*
3. **Under-preparing on the assessment format you will actually face.** This is specific and it is the one most often missed. A candidate preparing for a timed, scenario-based, multiple-choice exam and a candidate preparing for a written assignment are preparing for **different tasks**, and the same underlying knowledge is not sufficient for both. The ICA's Diploma schedules MCQs before assignments, per module — that sequence is a design signal about how knowledge is expected to build. *Mitigation: practise the format, not just the content.*
4. **Mis-reading the eligibility gate and losing time on administration.** A substantial fraction of the friction in these programmes is administrative: certified true copies of certificates, CV evidence of AML experience, translations, membership being a precondition, an eligibility window closing. AICB publishes a 10-working-day processing commitment and a specific list of who may certify documents; the IIA imposes a hard three-year (CIA) or two-year (CRMA) completion window from application. *Mitigation: resolve eligibility and documents before buying study material.*

### 10.6 The Pass-Rate Rule, Restated

> **No pass-rate figure should be trusted unless the awarding body itself publishes it.**

This guide records **no pass rate for any credential**, because none was retrieved from an awarding body's own pages during this pass ⚠. Where a training vendor quotes a pass rate for its students, that figure describes its students, measures its own selection effect (people who pay for a course study harder), and is a marketing instrument. Where a vendor quotes a *body's* pass rate, the number should be checked at the body — and if the body does not publish one, the vendor's attribution is unsupported.

The same rule applies to candidate numbers. Two figures in this guide are **body-published scale claims** and are marked as such: the IIA's "220,000 CIAs from 170 countries"; the ICA's "190,000+ professionals"; the ACFE's "95,000+ members"; ACFCS's "80+ countries and 15 of the world's 20 largest financial institutions"; IBF's "21,000 certified across 12 industry segments". Each is recorded as **the body's own claim**, not as an independently verified statistic.

### 10.7 The Integrity Rule, Restated for Preparation

§1.2 applies most sharply at the preparation stage, where the temptation is greatest. There is **no legitimate shortcut to knowing a syllabus** — a question bank teaches a question bank. A **"braindump" is a liability, not an asset**: often wrong (recall is unreliable), often stale (built against a previous edition), and a breach of the certification agreement the candidate signed, for which a designation can be revoked. The bodies publish what a candidate legitimately needs — candidate handbooks, blueprints, official guides, and in the IIA's case genuine retired questions. The honest preparation advantage is unglamorous: **read the current edition of the body's guide, follow the published weightings, practise in the assessment format you will actually face, and check the logistics early.**

## 11. The Regulated-Enterprise and Banking Angle

### 11.1 What a Bank's Compliance Function Actually Expects

A bank's financial crime compliance function, in practice, weights three things when it looks at a credential, and only one of them is the credential.

| What actually gets assessed | Weight in practice | What the credential contributes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Demonstrable judgement on real cases** | High | Indirectly — a syllabus gives vocabulary and framing, and exam scenarios rehearse structured thinking |
| **Operating knowledge of the firm's own systems, policies, and regulatory context** | High | Almost nothing. This is learned in-house and is jurisdiction- and firm-specific |
| **Evidence of commitment and baseline literacy** | Moderate | Directly — this is what a credential is actually good for |

The honest summary: a credential is a **weak-to-moderate signal** about competence and a **strong signal** about baseline literacy and commitment. Firms that state otherwise — in job specifications that require a designation and then interview for case judgement anyway — are describing the second use while testing the first.

### 11.2 What a Bank Publicly States Versus What It Expects

There is a systematic gap between the two, and it is worth naming:

- **Publicly stated:** job specifications list designations as "preferred" or occasionally "required". Regulators' published expectations discuss *training* and *competence* of staff in general terms. Annual reports and disclosure documents describe financial crime training programmes and completion rates.
- **Actually expected:** the candidate can read a transaction pattern and explain why it is or is not suspicious; can navigate the firm's escalation path; can write a defensible file note; and does not need to be taught what a beneficial owner is on their first day.

A designation satisfies the first; only experience and in-house training satisfy the second. That gap is the entire reason §12's worked example ends with a recommendation that is *not* "certify everyone".

### 11.3 Is Any Credential a Regulatory Requirement? — Stated Plainly

> **No source retrieved during this research pass establishes that any jurisdiction requires a specific professional AML/CFT certification as a licensing or appointment condition. On the evidence of this pass, all of the credentials in §2's table are *voluntary professional credentials*.**

This is the honest position, and it is worth being precise about why it is stated this way rather than as a flat "no":

- **The standards framework requires training, not certification.** The FATF framework and national AML regimes place obligations on firms to train staff and maintain awareness. **FATF's own pages could not be retrieved during this pass ⚠**, so this guide does not quote the Recommendations and does not assert what an individual Recommendation says. What can be said without a source is the *direction* of the obligation: it is a firm-level obligation about staff competence, not a named-product individual licensing requirement. Readers should verify against the FATF text directly.
- **Some credentials are regulatory-*adjacent* by design.** The IBF's own certification framework is built on Singapore's national occupational standards for the financial industry, and IIBF qualifications are built for India's regulated banking sector. That makes them regulator-*aligned* credentials. **It does not make them requirements**, and §7.3 records explicitly that no source was found establishing that MAS requires any individual to hold IBF Certification.
- **Adjacent credentials in regulated roles.** The CIA, CRMA and CRCM sit in audit and compliance disciplines where the *role*, not the credential, is what the regulatory framework addresses. The credential does not create permission; the appointment does.
- **Regulators assess people, not certificates.** Where a person is subject to approval — an MLRO, a compliance officer, a senior manager under a fitness-and-propriety regime — the assessment is of the *person*: their experience, their conduct history, their competence and their honesty. **A certificate is at most an input to that assessment, and never a substitute for it.** No certificate has ever satisfied a fitness-and-propriety test on its own, and no source encountered suggests otherwise.

**The practical implication for a firm:** the sentence "the regulator requires it" should never be used to justify a credential purchase unless a named instrument can be produced. In this guide's research, that instrument was never found.

### 11.4 Credentials, Competence and Fitness-and-Propriety

Three concepts that are routinely and wrongly collapsed:

| Concept | What it is | How it is evidenced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Credential** | Evidence that a syllabus was studied and an assessment passed | A certificate, a designation, a register entry |
| **Competence** | The demonstrated ability to perform the role | Work output, case files, quality assurance results, performance records, supervisor attestation |
| **Fitness and propriety** | The regulator's or firm's judgement that a person is suitable to hold a controlled function | An approval process: experience, references, conduct history, criminal and regulatory checks, interview |

**They do not substitute for one another.** A credential is not competence. Competence is not fitness. And fitness does not imply competence — a person can be entirely honest and suitable and still not be able to run a monitoring programme.

The clearest practical test: **if the credential could be revoked tomorrow, would the person still be able to do the job?** If the answer is no, the firm is relying on a certificate for something a certificate cannot carry.

### 11.5 The CPD and Evidence-Record Burden a Firm Carries

When a firm funds a credential, it takes on an ongoing administrative obligation that is routinely omitted from the business case. The components:

1. **The renewal fee** — recurring, per holder, per cycle. For credentials with annual cycles, this is an annual line item forever.
2. **The CPD hours** — a minimum per cycle, per holder. For the IBF route this is **15 hours annually** per certified individual; for ICA qualifications the body credits CPD hours to the qualification (20 for the Certificate, 130 for the Diploma) and requires membership renewal; for other bodies the credit requirement varies ⚠.
3. **The evidence record.** This is the part firms underestimate. For the IBF route the body states that certified individuals must **maintain records** — attendance certificates, appointment letters, copies of published articles — and that **IBF may conduct an audit** of certified individuals to review whether the CPD requirement has been fulfilled. A firm with 40 certified staff therefore maintains 40 evidence files, auditable at short notice. *(Source: ibf.org.sg IBF CPD page; checked 2026-09-19.)*
4. **Lapse risk.** A person who changes role, takes leave, or moves firm can lapse. A lapsed designation on a CV is worse than no designation at all — it is a visible gap.
5. **Administrative overhead.** Booking assessments, scheduling around cohort calendars (the ICA's Diploma runs on four intakes a year with fixed assessment windows), coordinating remote-proctoring rooms, and the document work that eligibility gates require (certified true copies, CVs evidencing AML experience, certified translations — AICB publishes a specific list of acceptable certifying persons).

**The point:** the cost of a credential is not its fee. It is the fee, plus the CPD hours, plus the record-keeping, plus the renewal, times the number of years you intend to keep the person certified. §12 does that arithmetic with illustrative figures on purpose.

### 11.6 The Training Budget and Retention Argument

The business case for funding credentials generally rests on three legs, and two of them are weaker than they are usually presented.

- **Leg 1 — Competence uplift (real but modest).** A credential genuinely raises baseline literacy, gives a shared vocabulary, and shortens the time it takes a new joiner to participate in financial-crime discussions. This is a real benefit and it is directly attributable.
- **Leg 2 — Retention and loyalty (real, but conditional).** Funded professional development is a retention instrument — but it cuts both ways. A fully and expensively credentialed employee is *more* marketable, not less. The retention benefit depends on the credential being paired with progression; a credential with no promotion path attached accelerates departure rather than preventing it.
- **Leg 3 — Evidencing a control to a regulator or auditor (real and often under-appreciated).** A documented training and competence framework, with credentials and CPD records, is inspectable evidence that the firm takes staff competence seriously. §7.3's IBF CPD audit mechanism is exactly this kind of evidence in machine-checkable form. This is arguably the strongest leg, and it is the one most often left out of the business case.

**And the honest counter-argument.** For a firm with a specific, well-documented competence gap — a team that cannot read a trade-finance red flag, or a monitoring function that cannot explain its own thresholds — the money spent on credentials may be better spent on **targeted internal training plus supervised case work**. That is faster, cheaper, directly measurable, and it addresses the actual gap. The credential's comparative advantage is *portable, standardised, externally verified evidence of baseline literacy* — and where that is not what is missing, it is not the right purchase.

### 11.7 The Statement That Anchors All of This

> **A certificate evidences a syllabus, not experience.** It tells you what a person has been examined on. It does not tell you what they have done, how well they did it, whether they can do it here, or whether they can do it under pressure with an incomplete file and an impatient supervisor.

Everything in §12 and §13 follows from that sentence. A bank that funds credentials as *one input* to a competence framework is using them correctly. A bank that funds credentials as *the* competence framework has purchased a syllabus and called it a capability.

### 12.5 The Study-Time Budget and Its Effect on the Operating Plan

The cost of the programme is not its price. It is its price plus the working hours consumed.

Using the illustrative study estimates in §12.4's grouping (a blended ~70 hours per head across all rungs):

| Measure | Value | What it means operationally |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total study hours across 34 certified staff | **2,380 hours** | |
| Converted to working days (7.5-hour day) | **~317 days** | |
| Converted to full-time-equivalent years | **~1.44 FTE-years** | About one and a half full-time people's worth of hours |
| Averaged across a 12-month rollout | **~198 hours/month** function-wide | |
| Averaged per certified person per month | **~5.8 hours/month** | The number that has to appear in the operating plan |

**The operating-plan consequences, stated honestly:**

1. **~5.8 hours per person per month is roughly a working day.** In an alert-triage function measured on queues and ageing, that is a visible capacity reduction. If the monitoring team's ten analysts each lose a working day a month, the function loses roughly 1.2 analyst-months of capacity per month during the study period.
2. **The study time clusters at the wrong moment.** Cohort-based programmes have fixed assessment windows — the ICA Diploma runs three modules with MCQ and assignment windows across nine months, on four intakes a year. Those windows will collide with quarter-end, holiday periods or an internal audit. **Plan the calendar around the programme's assessment timetable, not the other way round**, because the programme will not move.
3. **Coverage, not headcount, is the constraint.** Sending everyone at once means everyone is studying simultaneously. Phasing across intakes spreads the capacity loss — and is only possible for credentials with rolling or multiple intakes, which is a genuine argument in favour of the qualification-ladder route over a single-exam route with one booking.
4. **The CPD burden is permanent but small.** At an IBF-style 15 hours annually across 34 certified people, that is **510 hours a year — about 68 working days a year**, every year, forever. On a 60-credit three-year-cycle model it is even larger: **2,040 credits across the cycle, roughly 272 working days a year equivalent**. The renewal *fee* is the smaller half of the recurring cost; the hours are the larger half, and they are the half that never appears in a budget line.

### 12.6 The Recognition and Portability Constraints

Cymbal Bank operates across more than one jurisdiction (a realistic bank assumption for this exercise). That imposes constraints from §7's portability table:

| Constraint | Consequence for the mapping |
| --- | --- |
| **A national credential is not portable** | IBF Certification is built on Singapore's occupational standards; a national qualification from one market signals competence *in that market*. For staff who may rotate across jurisdictions, a global credential is the transferable instrument |
| **The reverse is also true** | A global credential is not evidence of competence in a specific national regulatory regime. Where a role is examined against local supervisory expectations, local training or a national credential carries information the global one does not |
| **Post-nominal collisions are real** | §7.4 records that Malaysian-prefixed ICA certifications and the global ICA routes share post-nominals. A credential-tracking register must record **awarding body, variant and qualification level**, not just the designatory letters |
| **No credential is a licensing requirement** | §11.3 applies. Nothing in this mapping is justified on the basis of a regulatory requirement, because no such requirement was found for any credential in this guide. If Cymbal Bank's compliance team believes one exists in any of its jurisdictions, the named instrument must be produced before it goes in the budget rationale |
| **Recognition is per-market, and the register must say so** | A credential held by a Singapore-based analyst and a credential held by a London-based officer may have very different weight in their respective markets. The matrix should record which credentials are the local currency in each jurisdiction where the bank operates |

### 12.7 The Decision That Actually Matters — the Ten Monitoring Analysts

This is the largest single line in the budget and the one where the honest analysis changes the answer.

**The case for the generalist credential for all ten:** fairness, uniform baseline, a shared vocabulary, the ability to move people between teams.

**The case against — and it is stronger:**

1. **They do not use most of the syllabus.** Monitoring analysts operate a programme; they do not design it. Domains (i), (ii), (vii) and parts of (iii) describe work performed by the six compliance officers. The analysts need domain (iv) and (vi) in depth, plus the firm's own procedures.
2. **The cost multiplier is brutal.** At $2,300 versus $1,300, the generalist option costs **$10,000 more in year one** for this group alone, and **$1,000 more every year** thereafter — for syllabus coverage they will not use.
3. **A credential at the wrong level creates a retention problem, not a retention benefit.** §11.6's second leg applies in reverse: a monitoring analyst holding a generalist designation is *more* marketable for compliance-officer roles, and the firm has funded a departure if there is no internal progression path.
4. **The actual gap is different.** The most common competence gap in a monitoring function is not knowing what money laundering is — it is being able to explain *why* a threshold is set where it is, and what an alert's disposition should look like in a file. That is domain-specific and firm-specific, and neither credential in the comparison tests it.

**The recommendation:** associate/operations-level credential for the ten (a floor, a portable signal of literacy, and a shared vocabulary at roughly half the cost), plus a deliberate internal programme on alert quality and file standards. Fund the generalist credential for any analyst **whom the firm intends to promote into a compliance-officer role within 24 months** — as a planned investment with a named successor role, not as a blanket entitlement.

### 12.8 The Recommendation, and the Case Against Mandating a Credential for Every Role

**The recommendation, in five parts.**

1. **Certify 34 of 40, at differentiated levels** — advanced/Level-6 for the three senior roles; generalist for the six compliance officers; specialist for the four sanctions staff; investigation credential for four senior investigators; associate/operations for the ten monitoring analysts; introductory certificate for the seven CDD analysts. Illustrative first-year cost **$74,000**, with **$8,600** of annual renewal and **510 CPD hours a year**.
2. **Do not certify the two data specialists.** Fund model-risk and data-governance training plus an AML literacy briefing instead (§8.2 role 6).
3. **Fund the internal programme first, not last.** $30,000 in year one buys competence for the four junior investigators and two specialists, and the refresher programme the firm needs for its own evidence file regardless of who is certified (§11.6 leg 3).
4. **Phase the rollout across the programme's intakes** so that no team loses more than a working day per person per month at any point, aligned to the body's assessment calendar.
5. **Budget the renewal from day one, in the same line as the certification.** A three-year approval of **$91,200** is the honest number; approving $74,000 and discovering renewals in year two is how credential programmes get cancelled.

**The honest case *against* mandating a specific credential for every role.** The board asked for "stronger credentials"; the correct professional answer is that *credential coverage is not the objective*. Four reasons to resist blanket mandation:

- **Mandation converts a development decision into a compliance obligation** — funding, renewing and recording CPD forever, including for staff who will never use the syllabus. The renewal line then grows with headcount, not with need.
- **Mandation destroys the discretion that makes the money effective.** The single highest-value case in the programme — funding a generalist credential for an analyst being promoted within 24 months — works *because* it is selective.
- **It confuses the credential with the competence** (§11.7), at portfolio level rather than personal level.
- **It is the hardest decision to reverse.** A programme can be expanded in year two; telling staff that a designation they hold will not be renewed is a retention event that costs more than the renewal fee it saves.

**What the head of FCC should say at the quarterly review:** *"We are funding 34 of 40 at role-appropriate levels, plus an internal programme for everyone. The three-year cost is $91,200 in credentials, $30,000 in internal training in year one, and roughly 1.44 FTE-years of study time phased across the programme's intakes. Six people are deliberately not being certified, and I can defend each one."*

That is a defensible answer. "We certified everyone with the flagship" is not, and the difference is the analysis in §8.2 and §13.

## 13. The Anti-Patterns

Each anti-pattern below is stated as **symptom → cause → guardrail**, because the symptom is what a manager notices, the cause is what actually needs fixing, and the guardrail is the thing to put in place so that the symptom does not recur.

### 13.1 Credential as a Substitute for Competence

- **Symptom:** the team is fully credentialed and case-file quality has not moved. New joiners hold designations and still cannot write a defensible file note.
- **Cause:** the credential was used to close a *capability* gap that a syllabus cannot close. The gap was case craft, judgment under incomplete information, or firm-specific procedure — none of which any of these exams tests.
- **Guardrail:** require a named competence outcome alongside every credential purchase — an observed behaviour, a case-quality metric, or a supervised piece of work. If no such outcome can be named, the purchase is decorative. Test it with §11.4's question: *if the credential were revoked tomorrow, could the person still do the job?*

### 13.2 Mandating the Flagship for Every Role

- **Symptom:** the certification budget grows linearly with headcount; renewal fees rise every year; analysts hold syllabi they never use; attrition rises among those who become more marketable precisely because the firm funded a generalist designation for an operational role.
- **Cause:** the flagship credential's market visibility was mistaken for universal suitability. The visibility is real; the suitability is role-dependent.
- **Guardrail:** build a **role-to-credential matrix** before the budget (the §12.3 model), and fund at rung-appropriate levels. The default answer for a large operational population should be an introductory or associate floor, not the generalist credential — with a generalist exception reserved for people with a named promotion path.

### 13.3 Funding a Certification With No CPD Budget for Renewal

- **Symptom:** in year two or three, staff either lapse (a visible gap on a regulated CV) or pay out of their own pocket (an entitlement dispute). The firm has paid full price for a credential it no longer holds.
- **Cause:** the certification fee was budgeted; the renewal was not. The renewal *fee* is the smaller half of the problem — the CPD *hours* are the larger half, and they consume working time that never appears in a budget line.
- **Guardrail:** approve the **three-to-five-year total** in one decision — certification plus renewal fees plus CPD hours. On the §12 figures, that is $91,200 rather than $74,000, and the difference is the whole point. Record the renewal in the same cost centre as the certification, so that the second bill cannot be discovered late.

### 13.4 A Regional Credential Assumed Portable

- **Symptom:** a designation that is highly credible in one market is queried, discounted or simply not recognised in another. A Singapore-market credential presented to a hiring panel in London carries less information than everyone expected.
- **Cause:** the difference between *delivered internationally* and *recognised internationally* was collapsed — the conflation §7.1 sets out. A national credential built on national occupational standards is a statement about competence **in that market's** regulatory environment.
- **Guardrail:** state the jurisdictional scope on the credential register, per credential, per holder. When a role spans jurisdictions, choose the instrument whose recognition matches the role's geography, and supplement a global credential with local training rather than assuming either carries the other's information.

### 13.5 A Training Vendor's Pass-Rate Claim Treated as Evidence

- **Symptom:** a supplier selection or a business case cites "our students pass at 94%". The number is never tested and never comparable; the following year, a different supplier cites a different number for the same exam.
- **Cause:** selection effect plus marketing. A paid course selects for people who will study; the figure describes the students, not the credential. It is also unverifiable by the buyer in most cases.
- **Guardrail:** apply §10.6's rule — **no pass rate is evidence unless the awarding body publishes it**. Ask a vendor for the sample size, the cohort definition and the body's own published figure. If the body publishes none, the vendor's number is unauditable and should be struck from the business case.

### 13.6 Knowledge Concentrated in One Credential Holder

- **Symptom:** one person holds the designation and is the single point of escalation for everything it covers. Their holiday is a risk event. If they leave, the function's claimed competence leaves with them.
- **Cause:** credentialing was treated as an individual development act rather than a capability-distribution act. One person was funded deeply; nobody else was funded at all.
- **Guardrail:** certify in **cohorts against role coverage**, not one star at a time. The §12.3 matrix does this by design: six compliance officers, not one. As a secondary control, require each funded holder to brief their team on what they studied — which converts an individual syllabus into team knowledge and makes the investment visible.

### 13.7 The Study Plan That Ignored the Published Blueprint

- **Symptom:** heavy study, a failed or uncomfortable sitting, and a strong sense of having prepared for the wrong exam. Candidates report "the questions weren't what I studied".
- **Cause:** the candidate studied the syllabus they *imagined* (often from third-party material, often from an older edition) instead of the one the body publishes. §10.3 explains why candidate-recall reports amplify this rather than correcting it.
- **Guardrail:** start from the awarding body's published domain list and weightings; translate each heading into the §9.2 domain set; route the substance through §9.3; and **practise in the assessment format you will actually face** — a written-assignment assessment and a timed scenario exam demand different preparation. Confirm the current edition in writing with the body before buying anything.

### 13.8 The Anti-Pattern Behind the Anti-Patterns

All seven share one root cause: **the credential was treated as an outcome rather than as an input** — to competence (13.1), to a role (13.2), to a multi-year cost (13.3), to recognition conditioned on geography (13.4), to a difficulty claim only the awarding body can supply (13.5), to *distributed* rather than concentrated capability (13.6), and to a study plan that starts from the published blueprint (13.7).

The guardrail that catches all seven at once is procedural and cheap: **before any credential is funded, write down (a) the syllabus domains it will test, (b) the role outcome it will improve, (c) the three-year total cost including CPD hours, and (d) the name of the instrument that requires it — or the word "voluntary".** If any of the four cannot be written, the purchase is not ready for approval.

### 16.1 The Glossary

Terms are defined **as this guide uses them in the certification context**, not as they are defined in AML law.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Awarding body** | The organisation that owns a credential's syllabus, sets the assessment, and issues the designation. It may be a professional association, a national institute, a training company or a vendor |
| **Advanced certificate** | In the ICA's ladder, a Level 4 qualification sitting between the introductory certificate and the Level 6 diploma. The word describes syllabus depth and academic level, not seniority |

### 16.3 The Closing Summary

Across ten credentials and eight recurring syllabus domains, four findings survive the research and are worth carrying forward.

**First: the syllabi converge; the delivery does not.** Every credential in this landscape examines a selection of the same eight domains, because they all sit on the same FATF/Basel/Wolfsberg backplane. What differs — and differs a great deal — is *how* a candidate is assessed. A timed scenario examination and a nine-month programme of multiple-choice assessments plus written assignments test different capabilities, and the choice between them is a fit decision, not a quality one.

**Second: the published syllabus is the only reliable study guide, and its most important number was the hardest to obtain.** Domain weightings determine where study hours go. This guide could not verify a weighting for a single credential, and it therefore reproduces none — because inventing a distribution would have been the most damaging thing a guide about exam content could do. §14.7 records the gap, §9.5 gives the method for filling it, and §15 gives the action list.

**Third: none of these credentials is a licence, and this guide found no instrument anywhere that requires one.** They are voluntary professional credentials. They evidence a syllabus. Where a credential is aligned with a national occupational standard — Singapore's IBF framework, India's IIBF qualifications, Malaysia's AICB programmes mapped to international AML/CFT competency standards — that alignment makes it regulator-adjacent. It does not make it a requirement, and the sentence "the regulator requires it" should never enter a business case without a named instrument attached.

**Fourth: the money is in the portfolio, not the purchase.** The recurring examples in this guide are consistent. The renewal fee is roughly a tenth of the first-year cost every year, forever. The CPD hours are the larger recurring cost and never appear in a budget line — 510 hours a year for a 34-person certified cohort. The study time is about 1.4 full-time-equivalent years for a 40-person function, and it lands on the operating plan as lost queue capacity. And the anti-pattern that costs the most is the simplest: certifying everyone, at the flagship, for every role.

**The one sentence to keep.** A credential tells you what a person has been examined on. A competence framework tells you what they can do. A fitness-and-propriety assessment tells you whether they may do it here. Fund credentials as an input to the second and evidence for the third, never as a substitute for either — and remember, in every budget conversation about certification, to keep the distinction between **the credential and the competence.**
