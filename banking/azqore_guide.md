## 1. The Overview

### 1.1 The Short Answer

**Azqore** (azqore.com; legal entity **Azqore SA**, a Swiss public limited company registered in the commercial register of the canton of Vaud) is the **wealth-management banking-services and outsourcing company of the Crédit Agricole Group** — a subsidiary of **Indosuez Wealth Management** (the group's global wealth-management brand, also known as CA Indosuez) that specialises in **outsourcing information systems and processing banking transactions for private-banking players** (azqore.com homepage, extracted September 2026). ✅ The company's own current positioning line is "**Business & Tech partners for wealth managers**": Azqore runs the IT estate and processes the banking operations of private banks and wealth managers on its proprietary integrated banking platform, **S2i**, delivering the service either as **business-process outsourcing (BPO)** — Azqore's own back-office experts operating the bank's operations — or as **software-as-a-service (SaaS)** — the client bank's own staff working on a shared, single-version S2i (ca-indosuez.com "Banking technology" page; the July 5, 2018 brand-launch press release; both extracted September 2026). ✅

The history has three layers, and getting them straight matters because the research brief's beliefs are wrong on two of them. **Layer one is the heritage:** the business now branded Azqore was **established as Crédit Agricole Private Banking Services (CA-PBS) in 1992 in the heart of Swiss private banking**, serving private banks and wealth managers from Switzerland (brand-launch press release, July 5, 2018 — "Azqore was established as CA-PBS in 1992 in the heart of private banking in Switzerland"). ✅ **Layer two is the 2016–2018 transformation:** a comprehensive strategic review *began in 2016* — an audit and consultation process with employees, clients and industry specialists — and on **July 5, 2018** Indosuez Wealth Management launched **Azqore** as the new brand for CA-PBS, a "newly independent subsidiary" positioned to serve external private banks, not just the group (same press release). ✅ So 2016 is the year the *review* began, not the year the *company* was founded — and there was no 2016 joint venture (see §2.2, §3.3). **Layer three is the ownership event:** on **October 4, 2018** — three months after the brand launch — **Capgemini announced the acquisition of a 20% stake in Azqore** from Indosuez Wealth Management, an investment "confirming the strategic ambition of the two groups to set a global benchmark in technology outsourcing services and banking transactions for the wealth management sector" (Capgemini investors page and the Indosuez/Crédit Agricole press pages, October 4, 2018). ✅ The brief's believed "~65% CA Indosuez / 35% Capgemini at 2016 formation" split is **not supported by any primary source examined** — the verified structure is Indosuez (the Crédit Agricole Group's wealth arm) holding ~80% with Capgemini at 20% from October 2018, a structure the January 2021 Societe Generale Private Banking press release independently confirms by describing "Indosuez Wealth Management and Capgemini, as shareholders of Azqore". ✅

Two further corrections to the brief's beliefs, both verified at primary sources this pass. **Headquarters:** Azqore's registered office is **38, Chemin de Bérée, 1010 Lausanne, Switzerland** — the legal notice on azqore.com identifies Azqore SA as "a public limited company registered in the commercial register of the canton VD under IDI CHE-234.349.335 – 38, Chemin de Bérée 38 – 1010 Lausanne" (azqore.com terms-and-conditions page, extracted September 2026). ✅ Singapore is an operational hub with its own CEO (Gaël Lang, "CEO of Azqore Singapore" — azqore.com news, July 2026), not the headquarters. ✅/⚠ **Platform:** Azqore operates its **own proprietary S2i platform**, not Avaloq — the company's own pages state it has "onboarded clients from the other major platforms on the market (Avaloq, G2, Finnova, Temenos, Olympic, etc.)", i.e. it *migrates banks off* those platforms onto S2i (azqore.com our-solutions page, extracted September 2026). ✅ The Avaloq/wealth-software platform mechanics themselves belong to the [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) guide (§8) and are cross-referenced there, not re-derived here.

The one comprehensively documented external client win is **Societe Generale Private Banking** (SGPB): on **January 28, 2021**, Societe Generale announced that SGPB's international entities — **Luxembourg, Monaco, Switzerland and the Kleinwort Hambros group headquartered in London** — had signed with Azqore for the execution of **back-office operations and a large part of the IT services**, with migration staged from 2022 and subject to regulatory approvals (societegenerale.com press release, January 28, 2021). ✅ The Azqore CEO at the time, Pierre Dulon, described the platform as a "Core Banking supplemented with leading software from the world of wealth management, thanks to one of the most robust fintech ecosystems" (same release). ✅ Implementation status of that multi-year migration is not public (⚠, §7.1, §12.4).

For a bank like Cymbal Bank, Azqore matters on four fronts: as the **reference profile for the "outsourced private bank" model** — a bank that keeps its balance sheet, brand and clients but hands its IT estate and operations processing to an industrial operator (the model's regulatory centre of gravity in Singapore is MAS Notice 658, cross-referenced in §10); as a **vendor class** that differs from the software-licence vendors profiled in the [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) guide (§8) — Azqore sells outcomes (running the platform, processing the transactions), not seats; as a **concentration-and-ownership case study** — a bank-owned outsourcer whose shareholder structure (Crédit Agricole Group's wealth arm at ~80%, Capgemini at 20% from 2018, sole-group framing on current materials) raises classic vendor-governance questions about captives, roadmaps and exit; and as a **worked-example generator** for the repository's Cymbal Bank persona (§11), which runs an RFI/RFP, a MAS Notice 658 materiality assessment, and platform due diligence against an Azqore-style operator.

### 1.3 Why This Matters to a Bank

An Azqore-style wealth-management banking-services company sits at the point where a private bank's *economics* meet its *technology estate*. Private banks face a brutal cost structure — heavy regulation (KYC/AML, FATCA/CRS, suitability), thin operating margins relative to the cost of running front-to-back platforms, and relentless client pressure for digital channels — and the industrial answer the market keeps returning to is outsourcing: let a specialist own the platform and the processing, keep the balance sheet, the brand and the client relationship in the bank. Azqore is one of the few *bank-owned* operators of that model — a subsidiary of the Crédit Agricole Group's wealth arm rather than an independent software house — which makes it a distinct vendor species from the Avaloq/Temenos/Finacle profiles in the [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) guide (§8). The distinction matters to a bank's vendor strategy: a software vendor sells a licence and leaves the run-cost, the operations risk and the regulatory interpretation with the bank; an outsourced operator like Azqore sells the running itself — and with it a different risk shape (fewer internal technology risks, new concentration, dependency, and exit risks, and a harder regulatory question about where accountability sits).

For a bank like Cymbal Bank (the repository's Singapore-licensed persona), the questions §11 works through are the practical ones: How does a bank evaluate an outsourced-operator bid against a licensed-software baseline? What does MAS Notice 658 (the outsourcing notice for banks — issued December 11, 2023, effective December 11, 2024, per the [MAS Regulations](mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) guide and the [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide §8.8) require when the "relevant services" outsourced are the bank's own core banking and processing? How does a bank do due diligence on a platform — S2i — that is proprietary, single-version, and run from operations centres in Switzerland and Singapore? And how does a bank price the ownership question — that the operator's roadmap is set by a parent group (Crédit Agricole Group) whose strategic interests may not be the client bank's? Those are the due-diligence themes this guide's worked example converts into an operating design.

## 2. The Company Profile — 1992 Roots, the 2016 Review, and the 2018 Brand

### 2.1 The Roots: Crédit Agricole Private Banking Services (CA-PBS), 1992

The business that became Azqore was **established as Crédit Agricole Private Banking Services (CA-PBS) in 1992, in Switzerland** — "in the heart of private banking", in the company's own words (brand-launch press release, July 5, 2018: "Azqore was established as CA-PBS in 1992 in the heart of private banking in Switzerland and has become a recognized digital transformation partner"). ✅ The same date appears on the parent's page: "Created in 1992 in the heart of the Private Banking sector in Switzerland, since 2018 Azqore has been a subsidiary of CA Indosuez, specialised in banking outsourcing" (ca-indosuez.com, "Banking technology" page, extracted September 2026). ✅ CA-PBS was the banking-services arm attached to the Crédit Agricole private-banking operations — the unit that ran information systems and processed banking transactions for the group's private banks and, over time, for third-party wealth managers. The group context matters: the Crédit Agricole Group is one of the world's largest banking groups (the 2018 releases cite The Banker's ranking of the group among the world's top banks by Tier 1 capital — 13th in the July 2017 and July 2018 editions cited), and its wealth arm has gone by the brand **Indosuez Wealth Management** (CA Indosuez) — the "global wealth management brand of Crédit Agricole group", shaped, per the group's own framing, by roughly 140 years of wealth-management heritage. ✅ (The Crédit Agricole Group and CA Indosuez / Indosuez Wealth Management are verified corporate shareholders and are stated factually throughout this guide; the group's corporate-and-investment-banking entity is deliberately not discussed here.)

Two honest caveats about the 1992 date, per the guide's honesty convention. **First**, the date is company-narrative — it describes when the *business activity* (private-banking information systems and processing services) began, and the 2018 releases consistently frame it as the start of "more than 25 years' experience" (which by 2026 becomes "30+ years" on the Azqore homepage). **Second**, the *legal entity* now named Azqore SA may not have been incorporated in 1992 — the commercial-register record (canton Vaud, CHE-234.349.335) could not be queried this pass, so the entity's exact registration date, former names, and capital history sit in §12.4 as unverified. What is verified is the *continuity claim the company itself makes*: CA-PBS (1992) → rebranded Azqore (2018) → Azqore SA today.

## 4. The Mission and Operating Model

### 4.1 The Mission: "Partners for Tomorrow's Wealth Managers"

Azqore's mission statement, consistent across its 2018 launch materials and its current pages, is the brand promise "**Partners for tomorrow's wealth managers**". ✅ The current homepage states the mission in operational form: "Azqore supports private banks and wealth managers in their development and transformation, **managing their IT and processing their banking transactions with a very high level of service**", by "developing an IT-as-a-service platform that covers all our clients' front, middle and back office activities", "connecting a range of digital tools and data analysis… to our Core System S2i", and delivering "a secure business, free from operational risk" (azqore.com, extracted September 2026). ✅ The parent's page frames the same mission from the group's side: Azqore "assists Private Bankers and Wealth Managers in their development", offering "a complete, integrated technology solution, operational services, and consulting assignments", drawing on "the advantages of Crédit Agricole group and its technology partnership with Capgemini" (ca-indosuez.com, "Banking technology" page). ✅ Note the mission's centre of gravity: Azqore does not position itself as a software vendor selling licences, nor as a consultancy selling advice — it positions itself as the *operator* of a private bank's technology and processing estate, "designed by bankers for bankers" (azqore.com). ✅

### 4.2 The Operating Model — Information-Systems Outsourcing Plus Banking-Transaction Processing

The operating model is best read from the company's own one-line definition, which has been stable since the brand launch: Azqore is "an international company specialising in the **outsourcing of Information Systems and the processing of banking transactions** for private banking players" (azqore.com homepage; identical framing in the 2018 launch release — "integrated technology services, operational back office and project consultancy" — and in the October 2018 Capgemini release — "technology outsourcing services and banking transactions for the wealth management sector"). ✅ In plain terms, the model has three revenue/relationship layers:

1. **Platform-as-a-service on S2i** — the client bank runs its private-banking operations on Azqore's proprietary integrated platform, either operated by Azqore or by the bank's own staff, on a single shared software version that is upgraded centrally ("regular updates to enhance performance and comply with regulatory changes", 2018 release). ✅
2. **Business-process outsourcing (BPO)** — Azqore's own back-office experts execute the client's operational processes — securities and cash operations, transaction processing, clearing-adjacent work — from its operations centres ("the option of outsourcing all their back-office processes to Azqore", ca-indosuez.com; "a BPO service operated by back-office experts", 2018 release). ✅ This is the layer that makes Azqore a *banking-services* company rather than a pure software house: it employs bankers to run banking operations for other banks.
3. **Consulting and transformation services** — onboarding/migration projects ("Onboard S2i"), operational-efficiency consulting, and digital-enablement work ("Open and Digitalize your S2i", ca-indosuez.com). ✅

A structural point a bank evaluating the model must grasp: Azqore sells the *running* of the bank's technology and operations, which is why its client banks keep their licences, their regulatory status and their client relationships, and delegate the industrial layer. In Singapore's regulatory vocabulary, the client bank remains the regulated institution and Azqore is the *service provider* — the accountability boundary that MAS Notice 658 polices (cross-referenced in §10.2, not re-derived).

The company's own "Why choose Azqore" argument (azqore.com our-solutions, extracted September 2026) assembles the model's selling points into one claim, worth recording verbatim in substance: **unlike a "simple digital services company", Azqore has "deep roots in the banking sector, as a subsidiary of the Crédit Agricole group and, more specifically, in the private banking sector, as a subsidiary of Indosuez Wealth Management"** — which the company says lets it "fully understand the needs of private banks and respond to them in a precise and expert manner (a strong competitive advantage over other market players)" — plus "continuous innovation", and "rigorous processes and recognised expertise" guaranteeing "optimal management of private banks' operations". ✅/⚠ The first clause is the verified structural fact (subsidiary of the group's wealth arm); the competitive-advantage and quality claims are company-made and flagged ⚠. The rhetorical structure is itself informative: Azqore sells *comprehension* (banking DNA) and *process* (rigour, certification) rather than *features* — the classic operated-service pitch, in which trust in the operator substitutes for the buyer's own technology capability.

The three layers of §4.2 are worth holding as a *transfer table*, because the question "what actually moves to Azqore and what stays with the bank" is the one every board-level evaluation asks first. The table below is assembled strictly from the verified descriptions quoted in this section and in §6–§7 (2018 brand-launch release, ca-indosuez.com, the January 2021 SGPB release); it is the guide's synthesis of those descriptions rather than a company-published menu, and it is the transfer map the §11 worked example prices:

| Model layer | What transfers to Azqore | What stays with the client bank |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Platform layer (S2i in SaaS mode) | The run of the integrated banking estate — single-version platform, centrally-managed upgrades and regulatory updates (§4.3) | The licence to operate as a bank, the balance sheet, the client relationships; the bank's own staff operate the platform (§4.3) |
| BPO layer (operations execution) | Execution of back-office processes — securities, cash, private-equity operations — by Azqore's experts from Lausanne and Singapore (§6.2) | Regulatory accountability for those processes; the bank remains the regulated party under MAS Notice 658 (§10.2) |

The transfer table makes the model's honesty boundary visible in one glance: Azqore's own terms-and-conditions state it is neither a credit institution nor an insurance brokerage firm (§7.3), so *money, licences and accountability never move* — only the industrial layer moves. That is also the line the Singapore regulator draws: the bank remains the regulated institution and the provider remains the service provider, which is exactly the boundary MAS Notice 658 polices (§10.2–§10.3).

### 4.3 BPO vs SaaS — the Two Delivery Modes on One Platform

The two-mode design has been public since the brand launch and is worth quoting at length because it is the single clearest statement of the operating model: "Clients can benefit from Azqore's system either by **outsourcing their back office to its experts (BPO)**, or by **their own in-house teams accessing its integrated banking platform in SaaS mode**. Both are based on a single version of Azqore's proprietary integrated S2i banking software, with regular updates to enhance performance and comply with regulatory changes. Frontend functionality can be customized using secure APIs" (brand-launch release, July 5, 2018). ✅ The same two-mode description appears in the October 2018 Capgemini release and on the parent's current page ("Clients can choose between a BPO service provided by Azqore's back office experts or an SaaS solution", ca-indosuez.com). ✅

The model's economics are the classic pooling economics of an industry utility: a **single version** of the platform shared across the client community means every client benefits from upgrades and regulatory changes funded collectively — "Its user community benefits from the upgrades and innovations initiated by one of them" (ca-indosuez.com) — and the capital cost of the platform is spread across the community rather than borne per bank. The company states the client-side value proposition directly: enabling banks "to significantly reduce their operating costs for technology and banking transactions, in order to invest more efficiently in digital transformation and the quality of the customer experience" (October 2018 Capgemini release). ✅

The mode choice is not a technology detail — it is a decision about *whose people run the bank's operations*, and therefore about the client bank's own operating model and headcount. In **SaaS mode** the client bank's staff work the platform, so the bank keeps its operations team but sheds the platform-run burden (upgrades, hosting, regulatory-change engineering); in **BPO mode** Azqore's experts execute the processes, so the bank sheds the operations headcount too and manages Azqore as an operator rather than as a software supplier. The two modes therefore sit at different points on the make-vs-buy spectrum that the [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide's §2.1 (make vs buy vs partner) maps, and a bank can in principle run *both* — some processes outsourced to Azqore's experts, others executed by its own staff on the same shared S2i version — because the platform is common and only the execution differs. That flexibility is a real property of the two-mode design, but it is also a governance complication: mixed-mode operations split accountability between the bank's staff and the provider's staff inside one platform, which is precisely the seam the §11 worked example's contract schedules and the MAS Notice 658 analysis have to police. (Analytical framing on the verified two-mode descriptions.)

The pooling economics also deserve a caution that the marketing pages do not print: the *benefit* of the single version is that the community funds upgrades collectively, but the *cost* is that no single client controls the upgrade calendar or the regulatory-change backlog — a client bank's critical regulatory fix (say, a jurisdiction-specific reporting change) waits on the provider's community release plan unless the contract carves out bespoke change. The 2018 release's phrase "regular updates to enhance performance and comply with regulatory changes" is the promise; the governance of *whose* regulatory changes get prioritised, and how bespoke work is priced and ring-fenced from the shared core, is a contract design question that §11.5 converts into clauses. (Analytical framing on verified facts.)

### 4.4 The Model's Market Context

Azqore's model sits inside a well-documented industry pressure: private banks face rising regulatory complexity (KYC/AML, cross-border reporting, suitability), margin pressure, and client expectations of digital channels, while the cost of running proprietary front-to-back estates keeps rising. The October 2018 release cites an expectation that "the technology outsourcing and banking transactions market for wealth management companies and private banks… will grow by more than 30% by 2020" (⚠ the figure is a market estimate cited in the release, not independently verified this pass). ✅/⚠ Azqore's answer to that pressure is the industrial-platform model: pool the platform, pool the processing, keep the bank's licence and client franchise intact. The model's most visible external validation is the January 2021 Societe Generale Private Banking contract (§7.1) — a *competitor group's* private bank choosing a Crédit Agricole Group-owned operator for its international back office and IT, which the SGPB release explicitly frames as "Crédit Agricole Group's strategy to pool its industrial platforms with prestigious partners in the finance sector". ✅ The market context and the vendor landscape are developed further in §9, cross-referencing the [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) guide's platform profiles rather than re-deriving them.

The model's market context has three layers that a bank evaluating Azqore should keep distinct, because they imply different competitive pressures and different risk exposures:

1. **The cost-pressure layer** — private-banking operating margins are squeezed by compliance cost and technology spend, and the pooling model attacks exactly that line item (the "significantly reduce their operating costs" claim of §4.3). This is the layer where Azqore competes with the *insource-and-modernise* option and with the licence vendors' own SaaS offerings — the §11 worked example prices precisely this comparison (Option A vs Option B).
2. **The consolidation layer** — the model is an industry-consolidation play: each client bank that moves onto S2i retires its own platform estate and its own operations team, and the operator captures the scale. The "30 clients in 11 countries" launch figure and the seven migrations in 24 months (§2.3, §5.4) are the visible cadence of that consolidation. For a bank, joining the platform means accepting a *dependency* on the operator's continued existence and roadmap (§9.3), which is why ownership stability (§3) is a due-diligence topic and not a background fact.
3. **The regulatory-change layer** — private banks run under a rising tide of regulatory change (AML/KYC, cross-border reporting, suitability), and a shared platform with centrally-managed regulatory updates (§4.3) turns that tide from a per-bank engineering problem into a per-platform one. This is the layer the company itself foregrounds when it promises "regular updates to enhance performance and comply with regulatory changes" and "supporting their efforts to ensure compliance with changing regulations" (2018 release; azqore.com) ✅ — and it is the layer where the buyer's regulatory accountability does *not* transfer, which the [MAS Regulations](mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) guide's outsourcing material (cross-ref §10.2 here) makes the centre of gravity of the whole arrangement.

None of the three layers is unique to Azqore — they are the standard economics of the wealth-technology outsourcing market that the [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) guide's vendor landscape (§8) documents — but the *combination* of all three inside one bank-owned, bank-operated company is Azqore's specific position, and it is the combination the rest of this guide examines.

## 5. The Platform — S2i, the Proprietary Integrated Banking System

## 6. The Services Roster — Outsourcing Scope, BPO, and Consulting

### 6.1 The Outsourcing Scope in Practice

The scope of what Azqore will take over from a client bank is defined by the January 2021 SGPB contract better than by any marketing page, because the contract is described in a counterparty's own words: "an agreement related to the execution of **back-office operations and a large part of the IT services** of Societe Generale Private Banking's international entities", covering SGPB **Luxembourg, Monaco and Switzerland and the Group Kleinwort Hambros, headquartered in London** (societegenerale.com, January 28, 2021). ✅ The release adds that Azqore "would provide these services" — execution of back-office operations plus the running of the IT estate — and that the migration "would take place in stages over several years, starting from 2022", subject to approval by competent bodies and regulators. ✅ In one contract, the model's whole scope is visible: **application/IT operations** (running the systems), **back-office processing** (executing securities, cash and related operations), and **staged migration** of the client's entities onto Azqore's shared platform and operating model. The same scope appears in the company's generic descriptions: "integrated technology services, operational back office and project consultancy" (2018 release) and the option of "outsourcing all their back-office processes to Azqore while retaining their existing IT environment" (ca-indosuez.com) — the latter being the important *process-only* variant, where a bank keeps its own core platform and outsources only the back-office execution to Azqore's experts.

Read across the sources, the outsourcing scope Azqore will take on has three distinct depth levels, and naming them matters because the §11 worked example's options are built from exactly these depths: the insource baseline (Option A) is the counterfactual every depth is scored against, the full BPO-plus-platform bundle appears as Option B, and the process-only variant appears as Option C (§11.1).

| Depth level | What the client bank outsources | Verified anchor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Full information-systems outsourcing** | The IT estate — applications, infrastructure, platform run — typically as part of a migration onto S2i ("a large part of the IT services", SGPB §7.1; "integrated technology services", 2018 release) | ✅ SGPB release, Jan 28, 2021 |
| **Back-office BPO only** | The execution of back-office processes while the bank keeps its existing IT environment ("outsourcing all their back-office processes to Azqore while retaining their existing IT environment", ca-indosuez.com) | ✅ ca-indosuez.com |

### 6.2 The BPO Operations Centres — Lausanne and Singapore

Azqore's BPO delivery runs from two operations centres, in **Lausanne and Singapore**, staffed by what The Wealth Mosaic's profile describes as "approximately 200 dedicated banking experts" (⚠ vendor-profile figure) — the teams that execute client banks' back-office processes. ✅/⚠ The two-centre design is the operational backbone of the "follow the sun" service Azqore markets: Switzerland covers European hours, Singapore covers Asia-Pacific hours, and the company's solutions page explicitly sells "Global Presence with Local Expertise / Follow the Sun" — a global banking platform letting private banks operate across jurisdictions with local regulatory coverage (azqore.com). ✅ The parent's page gives the team context: "With more than 400 IT and banking transaction experts in Switzerland and Singapore, Azqore's multicultural team enjoys a strong identity" (ca-indosuez.com; ⚠ company-reported headcount, and note this 400+ figure predates the current 750+ total on the Azqore homepage — the two are from different years and different scopes). ✅/⚠ The Singapore centre's role in serving Asian booking centres — and the data-residency questions that raises under MAS's outsourcing expectations for banks with Singapore operations — is the subject of §10.

The two-centre geography also explains a subtle drift in the public record worth noting rather than smoothing: the 2018 launch materials say **30 clients in 11 countries** (brand-launch release ✅), while the Fintech News Singapore directory profile (December 2024, derived from Azqore's own directory submission) says **30 clients in 10 countries** with **CHF 200 billion** AUM "up 80% from 2016" ⚠. The client count held at 30 across six years while the country count moved — consistent either with definitional drift (who counts as a client, and how a country is counted) or with consolidation of the client community onto fewer booking jurisdictions. The guide records both figures as published and does not reconcile them beyond this note; the honest read is that "30 clients" has been Azqore's stable public self-description since launch, which is why the 2026 homepage's smaller-sounding "20+ client banks" (⚠ company figure, different wording) deserves scrutiny rather than assumption — the §7.3 table flags the definitional drift explicitly. The operational centres' staffing ("approximately 200 dedicated banking experts", ⚠ Wealth Mosaic) and the parent's "400+ IT and banking transaction experts in Switzerland and Singapore" (⚠ ca-indosuez.com) are different scopes (BPO specialists vs all IT+banking experts) from different years, and are labelled accordingly.

The two-centre operating design deserves a closer look, because it is the operational face of the company's regulatory and commercial story. **Lausanne** is the Swiss heart — the registered-office city (§2.4), one of the two BPO centres, and the site of the company's IT data-centre role ("an IT company and host of an IT data centre", azqore.com CSR section ✅) — while **Singapore** is the Asia hub serving Asian booking centres and the follow-the-sun handover partner (§10.1). The division of labour between the centres is not published in any source examined — which centre executes which client's processes, under which shift pattern, and how work is handed over across time zones is operational detail the company does not disclose ⚠ (§12.4) — but the *follow-the-sun* claim itself is explicit on the company's own solutions page ("Global Presence with Local Expertise / Follow the Sun", azqore.com ✅). For a bank like Cymbal Bank evaluating the model, the undisclosed part — exactly how its own processes would be split between the two centres — is a due-diligence question to put in the RFP rather than a fact to assume, and §11.4's operational workstream treats it that way.

The centre staffing question also has a quality-of-service angle that the numbers alone do not capture: the "approximately 200 dedicated banking experts" (⚠ Wealth Mosaic figure) and the parent's "400+ IT and banking transaction experts in Switzerland and Singapore" (⚠ ca-indosuez.com) are the *pool* from which client-dedicated teams are drawn, and the SGPB contract's scale (§7.1) would itself have consumed a material share of that pool at peak migration — which is, for a later-arriving client, a capacity-and-attention question a bank should ask about openly. (Analytical inference from the verified headcount figures and the SGPB deal scale.)

### 8.3 The Group Compliance and Ethics Overlay

The compliance overlay matters to this guide for one specific reason: **a client bank outsourcing to Azqore is outsourcing into the Crédit Agricole Group's compliance machinery, not a standalone vendor's.** The verified mechanics of that machinery (azqore.com ethics page): group-wide ethics charter and codes of conduct binding on entities; the FIDES corpus of procedural memos transposing regulatory changes across group entities; group-wide AML/CFT, sanctions-embargo, market-abuse, conflict-of-interest, data-protection and poor-advice risk policies; compliance training modules; and a group whistleblowing system for employees *and external service providers and suppliers* — the last point being directly relevant to a client bank's staff working inside Azqore operations. ✅ The overlay cuts both ways in an evaluation: it gives a third-party client bank the assurance of a large banking group's compliance function behind the operator (a real due-diligence plus — the SGPB deal's framing as "pooling industrial platforms with prestigious partners" is this logic in action), and it raises the governance question of whose *interests* the group's compliance machinery ultimately serves when the client is a competitor of the group (developed in §9.3 and priced into the §11 risk register).

Three concrete implications of the overlay for a client bank's own compliance function are worth making explicit, because they are the points a bank's CRO will actually test in due diligence:

1. **The overlay is not the client's overlay.** The charters, codes and FIDES corpus bind Azqore as a Crédit Agricole Group entity; they do not bind Azqore to the client bank. A third-party client cannot rely on the group's compliance machinery as its own — it must contract for the specific standards it needs (AML/CFT processing quality, data protection, conflict handling) and audit them, which is exactly what §11.5's contract table does with audit rights and regulator access. ✅/⚠ (the *existence* of the overlay is verified; its *applicability to third-party clients* is a contractual matter, not a group-policy one).
2. **The whistleblowing channel is shared.** The group's whistleblowing system explicitly covers "external service providers and suppliers" — so a client bank's own secondees or staff working inside Azqore operations can report through the group's channel. A bank should know this channel exists, decide how it relates to the bank's own speak-up arrangements, and reflect it in the joint operating procedures. ✅ (verified existence) / analytical framing.
3. **The group frame is a *culture* data point, not a *controls* data point.** The ethics charter and codes tell a due-diligence team what behavioural standards the provider's people are trained to; they do not tell the team whether the provider's *processing controls* meet the client's standard — that evidence comes from the certification stack (ISO/ISAE, §5.5) and the audit rights that pull the underlying reports. This is the distinction the [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide draws between a vendor's stated values and its demonstrated controls (§8.5 there), applied here to a group-compliance frame.

### 10.2 The MAS Outsourcing Context (Cross-Referenced, Condensed)

The Singapore regulatory context for a bank outsourcing its wealth-management systems and processing is the **MAS outsourcing regime**, and this guide deliberately does not re-derive it: the regime is documented in the [MAS Regulations, Guidelines and Industry Expectations](mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) guide (the instrument catalogue and the 2023 Outsourcing Guidelines) and, from the vendor-management side, in the [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide §8.8, which records that **MAS Notice 658** (*Management of Outsourced Relevant Services for Banks*) was issued December 11, 2023, effective December 11, 2024, replacing the earlier banks' outsourcing notice, and operationalising the 2023 Guidelines on Outsourcing — risk-based governance of *outsourced relevant services* with focus on **material** services, notification to MAS, customer-information handling, audit/access rights, and exit-plan expectations; merchant banks sit under the mirror notice (1121). ✅ (Those guides carry the detail; this paragraph is the cross-reference anchor, per the repository's no-re-derivation convention.) Two facts specific to the Azqore-shaped arrangement are worth stating here because they fall out of the verified company facts rather than the regulation: **first**, Azqore is not itself a MAS-regulated bank or financial institution — it is a service provider whose clients (the banks) hold the licences and the regulatory accountability; its own terms-and-conditions state it is neither a credit institution nor an insurance brokerage firm. ✅ **Second**, the Singapore booking-centre reality — private banks licensed in Singapore run wealth-management operations whose IT and processing can be delivered from Azqore's Singapore hub and Swiss centres — means a Singapore bank outsourcing to Azqore is entering precisely the arrangement Notice 658 governs: an ongoing outsourcing of relevant services (core banking/platform operations, back-office processing, and often customer information) to a service provider that may itself be a related party of a competitor group and that operates across jurisdictions (Singapore + Switzerland). ✅/⚠ (analytical framing on verified facts).

### 10.3 What the Regime Means for an Azqore-Style Provider

For a bank considering Azqore from Singapore, the Notice 658 overlay converts every due-diligence question into a documented artifact: the **materiality assessment** (which of the outsourced services are "material" — the platform run, the transaction processing, customer-information handling — and the analysis MAS expects), the **notification file** (material outsourcing notifications to MAS), the **contractual clauses** (audit and access rights for the bank *and* the regulator, customer-information protections, business-continuity and exit-plan commitments, sub-contractor/group-entity transparency — e.g., the role of the group's infrastructure platform company and of group data-centre hosting), and the **ongoing governance** (BCP testing with the provider, concentration analysis, annual re-materiality review). The [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide §8.8 and §11 walk this machinery in depth; §11 of this guide applies it to an Azqore-style operator with one Singapore-specific twist priced throughout: the SGPB-style cross-border delivery model (processing in Singapore and/or Switzerland, platform environments possibly in both) makes **data-residency and regulator-access questions first-order**, because MAS' expectations attach to customer information wherever the service provider processes it. The worked example that follows is the concrete expression of these requirements.

One further point of Singapore context is worth stating explicitly because the sibling guides carry it and this guide's §10 is its condensed anchor: the MAS outsourcing regime is *complemented* by the technology-risk expectations — the Technology Risk Management guidelines (TRMG), documented in the [MAS Regulations](mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) guide §4.2, and the statutory TRM notice layer that the [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide §8.8 records as in force for banks since August 2023 — and the outsourcing and TRM regimes are designed to be read together (vendor-management guide §8.8). The consequence for an Azqore-style arrangement is that the bank's due diligence must cover not only the outsourcing-specific artifacts (materiality, notification, audit/access, BCP, exit) but the *technology* dimension of the provider's operation — security operations, patch and change management, incident response, and the resilience of the S2i estate itself — which is why §11.4's operational workstream and §5.5's certification discussion treat the ISO/ISAE stack as the *starting* evidence rather than the whole answer.

For a Singapore reader, the regime's demands on an Azqore-style deal can be condensed into a five-row artifact map, which is exactly the shape the §11.3–§11.5 worked example fills in:

| MAS artifact | What it requires in an Azqore-shaped deal | Where the example builds it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Materiality assessment | Classify each outsourced service (platform run, processing, digital channels, consulting) as material or not, with reasons | §11.3 |
| Notification | File material-outsourcing notification with MAS before/at commencement | §11.3 |
| Contract clauses | Audit/access rights (bank + MAS), customer-information protections, BCP/exit commitments, sub-contractor transparency (group entities!) | §11.5 |
| Ongoing governance | BCP testing, concentration analysis, annual re-materiality review, incident reporting | §11.5 |
| Exit planning | Orderly-exit provisions, data return/deletion, migration assistance | §11.5, cross-ref [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) §11.7 |

The cross-border twist that makes the Azqore case sharper than a domestic outsourcing is that the *service provider itself operates across two jurisdictions* (Switzerland + Singapore, §6.2): MAS' expectations attach to customer information wherever it is processed, so the residency map of the S2i environments and the processing centres is not a technical appendix but a regulatory artifact in its own right — the data-residency question that §2.4 and §10.1 flag as unverified in the public record and that a Singapore bank must resolve contractually before signing (§11.4).

## 11. The Cymbal Bank Worked Example — A Wealth-Management Outsourcing Evaluation

### 11.1 The Scenario

Cymbal Bank (the repository's fictional-but-substantiated Singapore-licensed bank persona, following the persona conventions of the sibling guides — see [Fireblocks](fireblocks_guide.md) §11 and [Reap Global](reap_global_guide.md) §11) runs a private-banking business booked in Singapore with a growing HNW book. Its front-to-back estate sits on a licensed wealth platform (an Avaloq-style core — platform mechanics cross-referenced to [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) §8.1, not re-derived here) run by an in-house IT and operations team of roughly 90 people, and the board has accepted that the estate's run cost and regulatory-change burden are no longer competitive. The bank's CEO sponsors a **wealth-operations strategic review** with three options: **(A) Insource-and-modernise** — stay on the licensed platform, invest in the in-house run (the status-quo-plus baseline); **(B) Outsource to an Azqore-style operated operator** — migrate the estate and the processing onto a proprietary shared platform delivered as BPO/SaaS from operations centres in Singapore and Switzerland; **(C) Process-only BPO** — keep the licensed core platform in-house and outsource only the back-office execution to an Azqore-style provider ("outsourcing all their back-office processes… while retaining their existing IT environment", the verified option of ca-indosuez.com §6.1). The example walks the evaluation the bank actually runs, extending the [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide §11 worked-example conventions (RFI→RFP→weighted scorecard→contract→risk register) to the *operated-service* model this guide profiles. ⚠ The worked example is design fiction built on the verified facts of §2–§10; every vendor fact it uses (S2i, BPO/SaaS modes, Lausanne/Singapore centres, certifications, ownership, the group-compliance overlay) is documented earlier in this guide.

### 11.2 The RFI/RFP Process

Cymbal Bank's procurement runs a formal competitive process in the vendor-management guide's shape: **RFI to six candidates** (the licensed-wealth-platform route represented by an Avaloq-style and a Temenos-style bidder; the operated route by an Azqore-style operator plus one alternative BPO; and two integrator-led managed-service bids), **RFP to four**, then demos, a **proof-of-concept** (a test-tenant migration of a sample client book), and **weighted scoring by a cross-functional team** (business, IT, security, risk, compliance, procurement — the buyer-side machinery of [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) §11.2, extended here with the operated-model criteria):

| Criterion (weight) | Option A — licensed platform, in-house run | Option B — Azqore-style operated operator (S2i BPO/SaaS) | Option C — process-only BPO on retained core |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional fit & platform coverage (25%) | 4.5 (proven wealth platform per [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) §8) | 4.2 (S2i front-to-back coverage per §5.2) | 3.8 (only back office in scope) |
| Run-cost and pooling economics (20%) | 3.0 (in-house run cost stays on the bank) | 4.4 (pooled single-version platform; BPO centres) | 4.0 (partial relief only) |
| Migration risk & track record (15%) | 3.5 (upgrade, not migration) | 4.0 (7 migrations in 24 months at launch; "Onboard S2i" methodology — §5.4) | 4.2 (no core migration) |
| Regulatory posture & control retention (15%) | 4.3 (platform in-house; full control) | 3.6 (material outsourced relevant services under Notice 658; provider is a competitor group's subsidiary — §9.3) | 4.0 (narrower scope, retained core) |
| Security, certifications & audit rights (10%) | 4.2 (in-house, SOC/ISO run by bank) | 3.9 (ISO 27001/9001, ISAE 3000/3402 displayed — §5.5; audit rights to be contracted) | 3.8 | 
| Financial stability & ownership (10%) | 4.5 (bank's own balance sheet) | 4.4 (Crédit Agricole Group backing — §9.3) but ⚠ ownership-change risk | 4.3 |
| Strategic flexibility & exit (5%) | 4.0 (licence exit is contractual) | 2.8 (proprietary S2i; concentration and exit-cost risk — §9.2) | 3.5 |
| **Weighted score** | **3.99** | **4.03** | **3.94** |

Option B wins narrowly — the pooling economics and the operated-model cost relief outweigh the control and exit concerns on paper — and the bank's steering committee instructs the team to proceed to detailed due diligence on the Azqore-style operator **and** to run the MAS materiality assessment in parallel, because the regulatory answer will shape the deal's structure.

### 11.3 The MAS Notice 658 Materiality Assessment

Cymbal Bank's operational-risk team runs the materiality assessment that [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) §8.8 and the [MAS Regulations](mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) guide describe (Notice 658, issued 11 December 2023, effective 11 December 2024 — cross-referenced, not re-derived). The assessment classifies each outsourced service the deal would create and documents why each is or is not a **material outsourced relevant service**:

| Outsourced service (under the Option B deal) | Relevant-service test | Materiality indicators considered | Classification |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S2i platform run (application management, hosting, upgrades) | Yes — ongoing operation of the bank's core platform | Would a disruption materially affect the bank's clients or the bank's ability to continue business? (Yes — the estate is the bank's front-to-back spine); customer information processed on the platform | **Material** — full Notice 658 treatment |
| Back-office processing (securities, cash, private-equity ops from the Lausanne/Singapore centres) | Yes | Transaction-processing continuity; error/concentration impact on the HNW book; multi-centre delivery (SG + CH) raises data-residency questions | **Material** |
| Compliance/AML-KYC screening tooling run on S2i | Yes (ancillary to the platform run) | Failure would impair the bank's AML/CFT obligations — the bank remains the regulated party | **Material** (as part of the platform scope) |
| Digital channels (e-banking/m-banking) | Yes | Client-facing; outage = reputational and conduct exposure | **Material** (as part of the platform scope) |
| Consulting/transformation services during migration | Project-based, time-limited | Not an ongoing outsourced service once migration completes | **Not material** (project governance applies) |
| Software licences retained by the bank (transition period) | No — retained in-house | n/a | Out of scope |

The assessment's conclusions, recorded in the bank's outsourcing file: the deal **creates material outsourced relevant services** (platform run + back-office processing), so Cymbal Bank must (1) complete the risk-based due diligence before entering the arrangement, (2) **notify MAS** of the material outsourcing, (3) ensure the contract carries audit/access rights for the bank and the regulator, customer-information protections, BCP commitments aligned to the bank's recovery objectives, and an **exit plan**, and (4) re-run the materiality assessment annually and on material changes (provider ownership change — the Capgemini-stake history of §3 is cited in the bank's file as precedent for why *shareholder-change clauses* matter: a 20% minority stake appeared and (⚠ possibly) disappeared within the provider's first years, and the bank's credit to the provider's financial solidity must be re-tested on any ownership event). The MAS-approval outcome is deliberately left open in the worked example, per the repository's convention of not resolving regulatory questions (⚠).

### 11.4 The Platform and Operational Due Diligence

The due-diligence workstreams on the Azqore-style operator, each mapped to this guide's verified facts:

- **Platform due diligence (S2i).** The bank's technology team benchmarks the claim that S2i covers "front office, asset management, advisory, middle office, back office, financing, compliance and risk management" (§5.2) against the bank's functional catalogue, using the vendor's PMS, KYC/screening, reporting and e-banking modules as the test surface. The team documents the "core-plus-fintech-ecosystem" architecture (the Dulon description in §5.3) and probes the API catalogue's depth for the bank's integration needs (external data, payments, regulator reporting). Because S2i is **proprietary and single-version**, the team treats platform documentation, release cadence and the upgrade governance ("regular updates to enhance performance and comply with regulatory changes" — §4.3) as contractual commitments, not marketing.
- **Migration due diligence.** The team validates the "Onboard S2i" methodology and the migration track record (§5.4 — seven migrations in 24 months at launch, "30+ onboarding" claimed by 2026 ⚠) with reference calls to banks that migrated from the bank's own platform family (Avaloq/Temenos populations — the migration-origins list of §5.4). The POC from §11.2 (test-tenant migration of a sample client book) is assessed against the bank's cutover criteria: data completeness, booking-centre regulatory continuity, and a rehearsed rollback.
- **Operational and security due diligence.** The bank's security team reviews the displayed certifications (ISO 27001, ISO 9001, ISAE 3000, ISAE 3402 — §5.5) and requires the actual attestation reports (ISAE 3402 is the service-organisation-controls evidence the bank's own auditors will want), the data-centre hosting topology (the operator is "host of an IT data centre" — §5.5 — and the bank requires the *residency* commitments: which environments run in Singapore, which in Switzerland, and what the regulator-access and data-export arrangements are for the Singapore-supervised book, per §10.3), and the sub-contractor/group-entity map (the group infrastructure platform company's hosting role and any group data-centre dependencies — §2.4).
- **Financial and ownership due diligence.** The bank's risk team documents the ownership structure from the verified record (§3): ~80% Indosuez Wealth Management / 20% Capgemini as of January 2021, with the current position flagged ⚠ (sole-Crédit-Agricole-Group framing on current materials; no exit transaction verified). The team prices two ownership scenarios — minority technology partner retained vs sole group ownership — because each changes the roadmap-governance analysis: sole group ownership means the operator's roadmap is set by a group whose private banks compete with Cymbal Bank, and the contractual protections (roadmap transparency, client-council governance, data separation, no-poaching of the bank's client relationships through the shared platform) become the core of the negotiation (§9.3).
- **Compliance-overlay due diligence.** The bank's compliance team reviews the operator's group-compliance machinery (ethics charter, FIDES corpus, AML/CFT policies, whistleblowing covering the operator's suppliers — §8.3) and confirms the division of accountability: the operator supplies tooling and processing; Cymbal Bank remains the regulated party for AML/CFT, suitability and conduct (the §10.2 boundary).

The contract stack follows the [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guide §11.3 shape (MSA + SOWs + SLA schedule) with the operated-model clauses the due diligence identified:

| Term | Agreed value (worked example) | Anchored to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service scope | Platform run (S2i) + back-office processing + digital channels; delivery from Singapore centre for the Asian book, Swiss centres for cross-border | §6.1–§6.2 |
| Delivery mode | SaaS during transition; BPO for processing volumes; single-version platform with centrally-managed regulatory updates | §4.3 |
| Availability (platform) | 99.9% monthly, with service credits | Bank's standard tier-1 terms (vendor-management guide §11.3) |
| BCP | RTO/RPO aligned to bank's recovery objectives; joint BCP tests semi-annual, both centres; data-residency commitments for SG-supervised customer information | §10.3 |
| Audit | Right-to-audit annually (ISAE 3402 reports + regulator access per Notice 658) | §5.5, §10.3 |
| Materiality & notification | Bank maintains the materiality assessment; MAS notification filed by the bank (its obligation, not the provider's) | §11.3 |
| Shareholder-change clause | Provider must notify any ownership change ≥10%; bank may re-run financial and roadmap due diligence and renegotiate exit terms | §3 ownership history |
| Exit | 24 months' notice; data-export specification in vendor-neutral formats; migration-assistance window; deletion certification | vendor-management guide §11.7 extended to operated model |

The risk register (excerpt — the tier-1 shape of [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) §11.6):

| Risk | Inherent | Controls | Residual | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single-platform concentration (proprietary S2i) | High | Exit plan with data-export spec; escrow-style access to platform data; annual exit-plan review | Medium | Technology + VM |
| Ownership change / roadmap capture by the group | Medium | Shareholder-change clause; client council; roadmap commitments; competitive-protection covenants | Medium | Risk + VM |

**The decision:** the steering committee approves Option B subject to the contract terms above and the MAS notification, on the economics that the pooling model cuts the bank's estate run cost by roughly a third against Option A over ten years (⚠ worked-example arithmetic) — with the explicit recognition, minuted, that the bank is trading internal technology risk for concentration-and-exit risk, and that the *evidence trail* — materiality assessment, due-diligence file, contract, BCP tests, scorecard — is itself the deliverable MAS and the bank's board will examine. The example deliberately leaves the go-live un-narrated: the point is the evaluation machinery, not the outcome.

### 11.6 The Lessons

The worked example distils to five lessons: (1) **the operated model changes the vendor-risk shape** — an Azqore-style outsourcing replaces dozens of internal technology risks with a small number of large ones (concentration, ownership, exit, data residency), and the risk register must be redrawn, not copied, from a software-licence template; (2) **MAS Notice 658 makes the buyer's paperwork the product** — the materiality assessment, the notification and the audit/BCP/exit clauses are not compliance overhead but the actual governance of the deal, and they must be designed before signature (cross-ref the [MAS Regulations](mas_regulations_guidelines_guide.md) and [Vendor Management](../management/vendor_management_guide.md) guides); (3) **ownership is a due-diligence fact with teeth** — the verified Capgemini-20% history (§3) is a live reminder that a provider's shareholder register moves, and contracts must move with it; (4) **"full coverage" claims are entry tickets, not evidence** — the S2i functional surface (§5.2) is verified as *claimed*, and a buyer verifies depth by POC, reference calls and ISAE 3402-style attestations, exactly as the example does; and (5) **the competitor-owned provider can work but must be governed** — the SGPB deal (§7.1) proves the model across competitive lines, and the governance that made it acceptable (contractual separation, group-strategy alignment, the "pool industrial platforms" framing) is the template a Cymbal Bank-style buyer should contract for. The example's value to the repository is that it takes the Azqore facts verified in §2–§10 — the S2i platform, the BPO/SaaS modes, the ownership history, the Singapore hub, the group overlay — and shows a bank converting them into an operating and regulatory design.

**What this guide adds to the cluster.** Its specific contribution is the *operated-utility* portrait: where the private-banking guide profiles the software vendors (licence/SaaS) and the vendor-management guide profiles the buyer-side discipline, this guide profiles the third cell — a bank-owned company that sells the *running* of private-banking systems and the *execution* of private-banking processing as a service. **The closing summary.** Azqore is the wealth-management banking-services company of the Crédit Agricole Group: a Swiss business with 1992 roots as CA-PBS, relaunched in July 2018 as an independent subsidiary of Indosuez Wealth Management under the promise "Partners for tomorrow's wealth managers", and given a 20% technology-partner shareholder (Capgemini) in October 2018 — a structure the January 2021 Societe Generale Private Banking contract confirms and whose current form only the commercial register can settle (⚠). It runs its client banks' information systems and processes their banking transactions on its proprietary S2i platform — delivered as BPO or SaaS from operations centres in Lausanne and Singapore — under the compliance umbrella of the Crédit Agricole Group, and it sells the pooling economics of a single-version industry utility against the licensed-platform model profiled in the [Private Banking](private_banking_guide.md) guide. The brief's three beliefs — 2016 formation, a 65/35 joint venture, a Singapore headquarters — are corrected to the verified record: a 1992 heritage, a 2018 brand, an ~80/20 structure with Capgemini at 20% from October 2018, and a Lausanne registered office with Singapore as the Asian hub. For a bank, Azqore is the sharpest available example of a question every private bank now faces: whether the industrial layer of wealth management — the platform, the processing, the compliance tooling — should be owned and operated at all, or entrusted to an operator whose banking DNA, group balance sheet and shared platform promise efficiency the bank cannot match alone. The evaluation machinery of §11 shows how a Cymbal Bank-style institution would test that promise — by RFI and RFP, by MAS Notice 658 materiality, by platform and ownership due diligence, and by a contract that prices concentration, residency and exit as carefully as it prices the fees. The answer the repository's guides keep arriving at is that the model works when the bank governs the relationship as seriously as it governs its own balance sheet — because in wealth management, as in every outsourced core, the client's trust is not delegated, only the operations are. The operator runs the platform and processes the transactions, but the bank remains the regulated party, the brand, and the holder of the relationship — which is, in the end, the entire architecture of the outsourced trust.
